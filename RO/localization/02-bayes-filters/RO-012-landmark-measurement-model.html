Landmark Measurement Model

Landmark Measurement Model

31 min read

Open as PDF

1. Overview

Key point

A landmark reading is a range and a bearing to a known point. The model predicts both from a guessed pose and scores the guess with two Gaussians around the predictions. One reading puts the robot on a ring; three readings pin it to one spot, and a reading can also be turned around to propose poses.

Figure 1: The 5 m by 4 m room with three coloured poles: pink at (4.4, 3.8), green at (0.4, 3.2), blue at (3.2, 0.4). The robot at A = (2, 2, 0) reads each one as a range and a bearing: pink 3.10 m at 40 degrees, green 1.95 m at 141 degrees, blue 2.05 m at −55 degrees

The likelihood fieldLikelihood field: A grid over the map that stores, for each cell, the density of a beam endpoint landing there; computed once from the distance transform, so scoring a pose becomes one look-up per beam. Explained in: Likelihood Fields and Scan Matching, §3. The likelihood field model lets the robot score a laser scan about 250 times faster than the beam model. What still breaks: a scan gives hundreds of distances, all of which look alike: wall, wall, wall. This Note adds readings that say which object was seen. A camera that spots a pink pole gives far less data, but the data says exactly which object it saw, and the map says exactly where that object stands. Such a recognisable object at a known place is a landmark. Robots pull landmarks out of sensor dataExplained in: Maps and Landmarks, §6.2 Finding the poles in a scan, and each one is seen as a range (how far), a bearing (at what angle from the robot’s heading) and a signature (what it looks like, here its colour).

Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §1. Overview, now with three coloured poles that the robot sees. To use these readings, the robot needs the same thing the beam modelBeam model: The measurement model of a range finder that mixes four densities for one reading (measurement noise, unexpected objects, failures, random readings) with weights that add up to 1, so no single odd reading can rule out the true pose. Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §2.2 Why we need a measurement model gave it for a laser: a measurement model ()Measurement model p(z∣x,m): The density of getting sensor readings z if the robot were at pose x in map m (also sensor or observation model); read over poses it is a likelihood that tells a filter which poses fit the readings. Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §2.2 Why we need a measurement model, the density of the readings p(z∣x,m) for any guessed pose x in the map m. The question is: how well does a guessed pose, such as A or a pose B 1 m to its right, explain these three readings? This Note builds the model that answers it:

  • what range and bearing a pole should show from a guessed pose, and the two pitfalls in computing the angle (Section 3);
  • the density of a reading, and why it is a product of two Gaussians (Section 4);
  • why one reading puts the robot on a ring, and several readings pin it down (Section 5);
  • how to turn one reading around and draw poses that fit it (Section 6).

2. A landmark reading

Key point

Each reading is a range, a bearing and a signature; the signature tells us which landmark of the map was seen.

First, what the robot at A gets from each pole of Figure 1. We write one landmark reading as a list of three entries, the range, bearing and signature (, , )Range (of a landmark reading): The distance from the robot to a sensed landmark, such as 2.236 m; together with the bearing it fixes where the landmark is relative to the robot. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §6.3 Range, bearing and signatureBearing: The angle from the robot’s heading to a sensed landmark, positive to the left, such as 86.6 degrees; together with the range it fixes where the landmark is relative to the robot. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §6.3 Range, bearing and signatureSignature (of a landmark): A label that says what kind of landmark was sensed, such as its colour or ID number; it tells the robot which landmark in the map a reading belongs to. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §6.3 Range, bearing and signature:

  • r is the range: the distance from the robot to the landmark, in metres;
  • φ (phi) is the bearing: the angle from the robot’s heading to the landmark, counter-clockwise positive, as for the heading ()Heading θ: The angle from the world x-axis to the direction a robot faces, measured counter-clockwise; it decides which way the robot moves when it drives forward. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading itself;
  • s is the signature: what identifies the landmark, here its colour.

A sensor that gives both numbers, such as a laser that picks poles out of its scan or a calibrated camera that also judges size, is a range-bearing sensor (Brown CS148 tutorial; Freiburg sensor-model slides, “Landmarks”). The signature solves a problem the laser scan never had to face: which landmark of the map is this? Knowing which map landmark a reading belongs to is known correspondence ()Known correspondence: Knowing which landmark of the map a reading belongs to, for example from a unique colour (its signature); with it the model can predict that one landmark’s range and bearing. Explained in §2. A landmark reading.. Here each colour appears once, so the colour gives the correspondence directly (Brown CS148 tutorial, §3). We write c for the index of that landmark and (mc,x​, mc,y​) for its place in the map; for the pink pole:

(mc,x​, mc,y​)=(4.4, 3.8)

When colours repeat, the correspondence is unknown and must be worked out too; later Notes on SLAM deal with that. Here each pole is known, so for any guessed pose the robot can ask what that pole should read.

3. What a guessed pose predicts

Key point

From pose (x,y,θ), a landmark should appear at the straight-line distance and at its direction minus the heading. Compute the direction with atan2, and wrap every angle difference into −180° to 180°.

3.1 Expected range and bearing

To score a guessed pose against the three readings of Figure 1, we first ask what the robot would read from there. The steps are those that turn a known pose and the map into a readingExplained in: Maps and Landmarks, §6.3 Range, bearing and signature, now done from a guessed pose. Figure 2 draws the pink pole seen from (2, 2). The pole lies 2.4 m further along x and 1.8 m further along y:

Δx=4.4−2=2.4 Δy=3.8−2=1.8

Figure 2: The pink pole seen from a robot at (2, 2): a right-angled triangle with sides 2.4 along x and 1.8 along y and long side 3.0 m. The direction to the pole is 36.87 degrees from the x-axis; the bearing is that direction minus the robot’s heading

Expected range. The straight-line distance is the long side of the triangle (Pythagoras), the Euclidean distance ()Euclidean distance: The straight-line distance between two points. Explained in: Handling Missing Data: KNN Imputer, §4.1 The Euclidean distance:

r^=Δx2+Δy2​ =5.76+3.24​=3.0 m

Expected bearing. The direction from the robot to the pole, measured from the world x-axis, is the angle whose tangent is Δy/Δx:

direction=atan2(1.8, 2.4)=36.87∘

The bearing is measured from the robot’s heading, not from the x-axis, so we subtract the heading (Figure 2). At pose A, θ=0:

φ^​=36.87∘−θ =36.87∘−0∘=36.87∘

A hat marks a predicted value. Together, the expected range and bearing ()Expected range and bearing (r^,φ^​): The range and bearing a landmark would show from a guessed pose: the straight-line distance to its place in the map, and its direction (by atan2) minus the robot’s heading; the model compares the real reading with them. Explained in §3.1 Expected range and bearing. of landmark c from pose (x,y,θ) are (Thrun §6.6; Brown CS148 tutorial, Eq. 5–6):

r^=(mc,x​−x)2+(mc,y​−y)2​ φ^​=atan2(mc,y​−y, mc,x​−x)−θ

3.2 Why atan2 and not arctan

Take the green pole, at (0.4, 3.2). From A:

Δx=0.4−2=−1.6 Δy=3.2−2=1.2

The plain arctangent of the ratio:

arctan(1.2/−1.6)=arctan(−0.75) =−36.87∘

That direction points down and to the right, away from the pole (Figure 3). The ratio has lost the signs: a point at (1.6, −1.2) gives the same ratio −0.75. So the formula uses atan2 ()atan2: A function atan2(rise, run) that returns the direction of a line over the full circle from its rise and run given separately; needed because the plain inverse tangent of the ratio cannot tell opposite directions apart. Explained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §4.2 Turn, drive, turn, which takes Δy and Δx separately, so it knows which quarter of the plane the point lies in, and returns the angle in the right quarter, between −180° and 180° (Brown CS148 tutorial, §3):

atan2(1.2, −1.6)=143.13∘

Figure 3: The green pole seen from the robot at (−1.6, 1.2). atan2 gives 143.13 degrees, pointing up and left at the pole; arctan of the ratio 1.2 / −1.6 gives −36.87 degrees, pointing down and right, the opposite way

3.3 Why angle differences must be wrapped

Scoring a reading means comparing it with the prediction. For range, the difference is a plain subtraction. For bearings it is not. Suppose the bearing reads 175° and the prediction is −178°. The two directions are only 7° apart, across the ±180° line, but the plain difference is:

175−(−178)=353∘

A bell-shaped error curve around the prediction, a Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is, would call that a wild miss. The fix is to add or subtract 360° until the difference lies between −180° and 180°:

353∘−360∘=−7∘

This is angle wrapping ()Angle wrapping: Adding or subtracting full turns until an angle or angle difference lies between −180° and 180°, so that 175° and −178° count as 7° apart, not 353°; needed whenever bearings or headings are compared. Explained in §3.3 Why angle differences must be wrapped., the wrap-around of headings on a dial, where 350° and 10° are 20° apart. Every bearing difference and every heading in this Note is wrapped (Brown CS148 tutorial, §3, which uses the angle between two unit vectors to the same effect). With the prediction and a fair way to subtract angles, the robot can now score how far each reading lies from what pose A predicts.

4. The landmark measurement model

Key point

The density of a reading is a Gaussian of the range error times a Gaussian of the bearing error, because the two errors are taken as independent. Several landmarks multiply in the same way.

4.1 Two Gaussians, multiplied

The pink pole reads 3.10 m and 40°, but from pose A it should read 3.00 m and 36.87°: the real reading differs from the prediction by sensor noise. As for the measurement noise of a laserExplained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §4.1 Measurement noise: a Gaussian around the expected range, small errors follow a Gaussian centred on 0. Our sensor has these standard deviations ()Standard deviation: How far values typically lie from the mean; divide by n−1 for a sample (pandas default) or by n for a population (NumPy default). Explained in: Understanding Your Data: Seven First Questions, §7.1 Count, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum, the typical size of each error:

σr​=0.1 m σφ​=5∘=0.0873 rad

We treat the range error and the bearing error as independent: knowing that the range came out 10 cm long says nothing about the bearing (Brown CS148 tutorial, Eq. 13). For independent events ()Independent events: Events where one happening does not change the probability of the other. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Independent Events, §2. The definition the probabilities multiply, and so do the densities. So the density of one reading is the product of two Gaussians:

p(z∣x,m)=N(r−r^; 0,σr2​) × N(φ−φ^​; 0,σφ2​)

with the bearing difference wrapped. A camera can confuse colours, so Thrun adds a third Gaussian for the signature; with known correspondence that factor is the same for every pose and can be left out (Thrun §6.6; Brown CS148 tutorial, Eq. 16–17). This is the landmark measurement model ()Landmark measurement model: The density of a landmark reading (range, bearing) given a pose and the map: a Gaussian of the range error times a Gaussian of the bearing error, with the errors taken as independent; it scores how well a guessed pose explains what the robot saw. Explained in §4.1 Two Gaussians, multiplied. with known correspondence (Thrun §6.6, Table 6.4; Freiburg sensor-model slides, “Probabilistic Model”).

Worked example: the pink pole at pose A. The errors:

r−r^=3.10−3.00=0.10 m φ−φ^​=40∘−36.87∘=3.13∘ 3.13∘=0.0546 rad

The range Gaussian. Its peak height is one over σr​2π​:

0.1×2.5071​=3.989 per m

The error is one standard deviation, so:

3.989×e−21​×12 =3.989×0.607=2.420 per m

The bearing Gaussian. Its peak height:

0.08727×2.50661​=4.572 per rad

The error, in standard deviations:

0.08730.0546​=0.627 4.572×e−21​×0.6272 =4.572×0.822=3.758 per rad

The product:

2.420×3.758=9.09

The result is a density, per metre per radian, so it can be larger than 1; only areas under it are probabilities (what a density meansExplained in: Probability Density Function and the CDF of a Continuous Variable, §5. What the density at a point means, )Probability density: The height of a continuous distribution’s curve; compares how likely nearby values are. Explained in: Probability Density Function and the CDF of a Continuous Variable, §5. What the density at a point means. It is easy to call such a number “the probability of the reading”, and wrong.

These scores judge poses the robot already guessed. The next section asks the reverse: which positions fit the readings at all?

5. Where can the robot be?

Key point

One range reading allows every position on a ring around the landmark. Two rings meet in at most two points, and a third ring, or the map, picks one.

5.1 One landmark: a ring

Section 4 scored two guessed poses, A and B. Now turn the question around: the readings of Figure 1 are fixed, and we ask which robot positions fit them, starting with one. Take the pink pole’s range, 3.10 m, alone. Every position 3.10 m from the pole predicts that range exactly, whatever its direction from the pole. So the positions that fit form a ring of radius 3.10 m around the pole, blurred by the 0.1 m noise (Freiburg sensor-model slides, “Distances only”).

Figure 4 draws the range Gaussian over every position of the room as a surface: a curved ridge of height 3.99 per m along the ring, falling to zero on either side. The true position A sits on the ridge, and so does every other point of it.

Figure 4: The density of the pink pole’s range reading over every robot position in the room, drawn as a surface: a curved ridge of radius 3.10 m around the pink pole, about 0.2 m wide, with the true position A on its crest

5.2 Two and three landmarks: rings meet

The green pole’s range, 1.95 m, gives a second ring. A position that fits both readings must lie on both rings. Two circles cross in at most two points, and here one of the two lies outside the room. Finding a position from distances to known points is trilateration ()Trilateration: Finding a position from measured distances to points at known places: each distance puts the robot on a circle, and the circles meet at the position; GPS works this way. Explained in §5.2 Two and three landmarks: rings meet., the principle GPS uses with satellites.

Figure 5 multiplies the range densities in, one pole at a time; the product of densities is how readings combine, as in Section 4.2. With pink alone the whole ring fits. With pink and green, only the spot near (1.9, 2.0) is left inside the room. With all three, the highest point is (1.90, 1.98), 10 cm from the true position, because each reading carries its own noise.

Figure 5: Animation over the room: first the density of the pink range reading, a ring around the pink pole; then pink times green, which leaves one bright spot near (1.9, 2.0); then pink times green times blue, one sharp spot at (1.90, 1.98) next to the true position (2, 2), marked by a black cross

The bearings add the heading. Once the position is known, a bearing says which way the robot faces: the pole must appear at the measured angle from the heading. Rings and bearings say where the robot could be; Section 6 turns them into poses the robot can try.

6. Sampling poses from a landmark reading

Key point

To propose poses that fit one reading, pick a random angle around the landmark, step out by the measured range, and turn the robot so the landmark appears at the measured bearing.

6.1 Why turn a reading around

The scores of Section 4 can only judge guesses the robot already has; they cannot help when no guess lies near A. A particle filter keeps a cloud of guessed poses and moves them with the motion model. If the robot is carried somewhere else, or starts with no idea where it is, none of its guesses may lie near the truth, and weighting them by the measurement model cannot fix that. A remedy is to add guesses drawn from the reading itself: poses where the robot could be, given what it just saw. Lenser and Veloso used exactly this on soccer-playing legged robots, adding poses drawn from the landmark readings whenever the filter was lost (“sensor resetting localization”, Lenser and Veloso 2000). Drawing poses that fit a reading is sampling poses from a measurement ()Sampling poses from a measurement: Drawing poses that fit one reading: for a landmark, a random angle around it, the measured range plus noise, and the heading that makes the landmark appear at the measured bearing; used to add good guesses to a particle filter that is lost. Explained in §6.1 Why turn a reading around. (Thrun §6.6.3). The particle filter loopExplained in: Particle Filter, §4.6 The algorithm runs the filter itself; drawing random values from a distribution is sampling ()Sampling (from a distribution): Drawing random values so that, over many draws, each value comes up as often as a given distribution says; it turns a motion model into a cloud of possible poses for particle filters. Explained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §5.1 Why sample instead of scoring.

6.2 The recipe, and where the heading comes from

Section 5.1 says the robot lies on a ring around the pole, at any angle. So we pick that angle at random, and add noise to the reading so the samples spread as the sensor’s errors do (Thrun §6.6.3, Table 6.5):

  1. Draw an angle γ (gamma) around the pole, uniformly from 0° to 360°.

  2. Draw a noisy range and bearing:

    r~=r+noise of std. dev. σr​ φ~​=φ+noise of std. dev. σφ​

  3. Place the robot at distance r~ from the pole, in direction γ:

    x=mc,x​+r~cosγ y=mc,y​+r~sinγ

  4. Set the heading (wrapped):

    θ=γ+180∘−φ~​

Step 4 needs a reason. Figure 6 shows it. The robot sits in direction γ from the pole, so seen from the robot the pole lies in the opposite direction, γ+180∘. The bearing is that direction minus the heading (Section 3.1):

φ~​=γ+180∘−θ

Solving for the heading gives step 4. Thrun writes γ−π−φ, which is the same angle, since +180∘ and −180∘ differ by a full turn.

Figure 6: Placing a pose from one reading of the pink pole. The robot sits on the circle of radius r = 3.0 m around the pole, in direction gamma = 216.87 degrees from it. Seen from the robot, the pole lies at 216.87 − 180 = 36.87 degrees; with bearing phi = 36.87 degrees, the heading is 0

Check with the noise-free reading. From the pink pole, A lies in the direction:

γ=atan2(2−3.8, 2−4.4)=216.87∘

With the predicted range 3.0 m and bearing 36.87°:

x=4.4+3.0cos216.87∘=4.4−2.4=2.0 y=3.8+3.0sin216.87∘=3.8−1.8=2.0 θ=216.87∘+180∘−36.87∘=360∘ =0∘ after wrapping

The recipe gives back pose A exactly. With the real reading (3.10 m, 40°) it gives (1.92, 1.94, −3.13°): close to A, off by the reading’s own noise.

6.3 The samples

Figure 7 draws 200 poses from the pink reading. They form a ring of radius about 3.1 m around the pole, and each arrow is turned so that the pole appears about 40° to its left. Seen from the 200 samples, the pole’s range averages 3.10 m with standard deviation 0.10 m and its bearing averages 39.8° with standard deviation 4.9°: the samples reproduce the reading and its noise, as they should.

Figure 7: 200 poses drawn from the pink pole’s reading (3.10 m, 40 degrees): dots on a ring of radius about 3.1 m around the pole, each with a short arrow for its heading, turned so the pole appears 40 degrees to its left. The true pose A is on the ring; most of the ring lies outside the room

Only 64 of the 200 samples lie inside the room. One reading does not know about walls; the map does. Samples outside the free space are thrown away, as the motion model does for poses inside wallsExplained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §7. Ruling out poses inside walls, and readings of the other poles, through the measurement model of Section 4, then weight the rest.

  • The opening question has its answer: the three readings of Figure 1 score the true pose A at 2006 and the pose B one metre away at about 10−43, because each pole, reduced to a range and a bearing, misses by several standard deviations from the wrong pose.
  • A landmark reading is a range, a bearing and a signature; the signature gives the correspondence, so the robot knows which map landmark to predict.
  • The expected bearing uses atan2, because the plain arctangent of Δy/Δx loses the signs and can point the opposite way (−36.87° instead of 143.13° for the green pole).
  • Bearing differences are wrapped into −180° to 180°, because 175° and −178° are 7° apart, not 353°.
  • The density of a reading is the product of a range Gaussian and a bearing Gaussian, because the two errors are taken as independent; readings of several landmarks multiply for the same reason. It is a density, so it can exceed 1 (9.09 for the pink pole).
  • One range reading fits a whole ring of positions; two rings cross in at most two points, and a third reading or the map picks one; here the three poles put the robot within 10 cm of the truth.
  • A reading can be turned around to draw poses that fit it: a random angle around the landmark, the measured range, and the heading γ+180∘−φ, so that a lost particle filter can put guesses where the robot could really be.

The robot now has a motion model and sensor models for a laser and for landmarks, so it can score any guessed pose.
