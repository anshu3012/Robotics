1. Overview

Key point

A robot has two noisy ways to know where it is: its commands, which drift, and its sensor, which jumps. The Kalman filter blends them with the weight that makes the result as certain as possible, and it does so one step at a time from two numbers.

2.1 The Bayes filter in brief

The robot’s belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. The Bayes filter ()Bayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.4 The algorithm updates it once a second, in rounds numbered by the step t. At step t:

  • xt​ is the robot’s position, in m;
  • bel(xt​) is the belief, the probability distribution over the position xt​;
  • ut​ is the command, 0.5 m forward;
  • zt​ is the beacon’s reading, in m;
  • p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​) is the motion model, the probability of each new position given the command and the last position xt−1​;
  • p(zt​∣xt​) is the sensor model, the probability of each reading given the position.

Each round has two steps:

  • predict with the command ut​, spreading the belief by the motion model;
  • update with the reading zt​, multiplying by the sensor model and rescaling to a total of 1.

2.4 Adding independent errors adds variances

The robot’s next position is its present position plus the commanded 0.5 m plus a random slip. The estimate moves by the same 0.5 m. The error is the true position minus the estimate. With:

  • x is the robot’s present true position, in m;
  • x^ is the present estimate, in m;
  • w is the next step’s wheel slip, in m, with mean 0 and a standard deviation of 0.1 m;
  • e is the present error, the true position minus the estimate, in m.

The next error:

(x+0.5+w)−(x^+0.5) =x−x^+w =e+w

The present error has mean 0, because the estimate is the mean, and variance ()Variance of a random variable: How spread out a random variable’s values are: the expected squared distance from its expected value, Var(X)=E[(X−E[X])2]. Explained in: Random Variables as Functions, Expected Value and Variance, §4. Variance of a random variable p, the estimate’s variance, 1 m² at the start (Section 2.2). The slip has mean 0 and does not depend on e, so the two errors are independent. Their variances add, where Var stands for the variance:

Var(e+w)=Var(e)+Var(w)

Why. Take a present error of 1 m or −1 m and a slip of 0.1 m or −0.1 m, all four pairs equally likely. Square each next error:

(1+0.1)2=1+0.2+0.01 (1−0.1)2=1−0.2+0.01 (−1+0.1)2=1−0.2+0.01 (−1−0.1)2=1+0.2+0.01

Average the four squares; each +0.2 cancels a −0.2:

Var(e+w)=1+0.01

The same in symbols:

(e+w)2=e2+2ew+w2

Averaged, the 2ew parts cancel the same way: whatever e is, the slip is as often negative as positive.

Var(e+w)=Var(e)+Var(w)

So every slip adds its variance, and the commands’ drift grows step by step.

  3. the first belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Our robot meets all three. At step t:

  • xt​ is the robot’s true position, in m;
  • ut​ is the command, 0.5 m forward;
  • wt​ is the wheel slip, the source of the commands’ drift, normal with mean 0 and variance Q=0.01 m² (a standard deviation of 0.1 m);
  • zt​ is the beacon’s reading, in m;
  • vt​ is the beacon’s error, the source of its jumps, normal with mean 0 and variance R=0.16 m² (a standard deviation of 0.4 m).

xt​=xt−1​+ut​+wt​

The reading is the position plus the beacon’s error:

Both are linear with normal noise, which meets conditions 1 and 2. The first belief, mean 0 m and variance 1 m² (Section 2.2), is normal, which meets condition 3. Key point

6.6 The state a controller needs: the filter as an observer

The speed estimate of Section 6.4 is what a controller needs. A controller that holds the robot at 0.5 m/s must know the speed; one that stops it at a door must know the position. Many controllers need the whole state, but sensors give only part of it (FBS §8.1). Something must rebuild the missing parts.

The obvious attempt is to run the model alone, with no readings: each step, multiply the last estimate by the state transition matrix F.   • xˉ is the estimate the model predicts, a vector of 2 entries: position, in m, and speed, in m/s;
  • z is the beacon’s reading, in m;
  • H is the measurement matrix of Section 6.2, the row (1, 0), which turns the state into the reading the sensor should give;
  • L is the observer’s gain, a vector of 2 entries: how far to correct the position and the speed per metre of gap; for the Kalman filter at step 1, 0.926 and 0.461;
  • x^ is the corrected estimate, a vector of 2 entries.
