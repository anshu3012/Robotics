Key point

A robot never sees its own position. What it can keep is a belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. With the landmark modelExplained in: Landmark Measurement Model, §4. The landmark measurement model, the robot has a motion model ()Motion model p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​): The probability distribution of a robot’s pose after a move, given its pose before and the control (command or odometry); it describes how uncertain motion is, so a filter can score possible end poses or draw them at random. Overview as before, with the same layout and numbers, because a belief over 10 cells is 10 numbers we can draw and check by hand. A robot stands in a hallway of 10 cells, each 1 m long. The hallway is a loop: one step to the right of cell 9 is cell 0. There are doors in front of cells 1, 3 and 7, and all three look the same.

The robot has two things to go on:

  • a door sensor that reads “door” or “wall”, and is sometimes wrong;
  • its commands, such as “move 2 cells to the right”, which its wheels carry out only roughly.

The robot stands in cell 1, but nothing tells it so. A reading of “door” fits three cells. A move of “2 cells” may really be 1 or 3. So the question of this Note is: what should the robot keep in memory about where it is, so that it can act sensibly?

We answer it in steps:

  • what the robot really knows: the list of its commands and readings (Section 2);
  • a first summary of that list: the set of cells it could be in, and why a set is not enough (Section 3);
  • a better summary: a probability for every cell, the belief (Section 4);
  • the two probability tables that tell the robot how commands and readings change the belief (Section 5);
  • one picture of how states, commands and readings depend on each other, the hidden Markov model ()Hidden Markov model (HMM): A Markov chain whose states cannot be seen, where each state produces an observation that can, such as hidden robot cells producing “door” or “wall” readings; it is the model behind robot state estimation, speech recognition and many trackers. Explained in §6.3 Hidden Markov model. (Section 6).

The state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.1 State: from one number to many is what the robot keeps track of, and the commands and readingsExplained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §5.1 Measurements and controls are the two streams of data it receives. In symbols:

  • t is the time step, counted 1, 2, 3;
  • xt​ is the state, the robot’s cell at time t, a cell number from 0 to 9 (cell 1 at t=1);
  • ut​ is the command at time t, such as “move 2”;
  • zt​ is the reading at time t, “door” or “wall”.

2. What the robot knows: its history

Key point

The robot cannot know its true cell. It can only know the commands it gave and the readings it got; that list is its information state ()Information state (history): Everything a robot knows at time t: its starting knowledge plus every command and every reading so far, such as (stay, door, move 2, door); every decision must be based on it, because the true state is never seen. Explained in §2.2 The information state: everything the robot knows..

2.1 Why the true cell is out of reach

The robot stands in cell 1, but its sensor reads “door” in front of three different cells, and it is sometimes wrong. The wheels slip, so the command “move 2” does not say exactly how far the robot went. Nothing the robot has ever received names its cell. 2.2 The information state: everything the robot knows

The framed part of Figure 2 is everything the robot knows at time t: what it knew at the start, followed by every command and every reading so far. This list is the information state ()Information state (history): Everything a robot knows at time t: its starting knowledge plus every command and every reading so far, such as (stay, door, move 2, door); every decision must be based on it, because the true state is never seen. Explained in §2.2 The information state: everything the robot knows., also called the history information state (LaValle §11.1.2). At time 2 it is the list (stay, door, move 2, door). Each new step adds one command and one reading.

Why give the list a name? Because it is the only thing a robot’s decisions can be based on. A rule such as “if you think you are at the second door, open it” has to be a rule on what the robot knows, not on its true cell, which it never sees (LaValle §11.1.3).

The trouble with the list is its length. The list grows by two entries every step and never shrinks. A robot that reads its sensor 10 times a second has, after one hour:

10×3600=36,000 readings

Storing and searching that whole list at every step is not practical. So we look for a short summary of the history that keeps everything the robot needs and throws away the rest (LaValle §11.2). Sections 3 and 4 give two such summaries.

3. Summary 1: the set of cells the robot could be in

Key point

We can keep the set of cells that agree with every command and reading: a move turns each cell into all the cells it can reach, and a reading keeps only the cells that agree with it. With a sensor that can be wrong, the set stops shrinking.

3.1 The set, step by step

A first thing to keep in memory, shorter than the history: the cells the robot could be in. Suppose for a moment that the sensor is never wrong. The reading “door” then means “one of the door cells”. So after the first reading the robot can be in:

{1,3,7}

Next, the command “move 2” moves the robot 1, 2 or 3 cells. Every cell of the set becomes all the cells it can reach, and we collect them together (the move stage of Figure 3). The symbol ∪ (union) means “all cells in either set”:

{2,3,4}∪{4,5,6}∪{8,9,0} ={0,2,3,4,5,6,8,9}

The second reading “door” keeps only the cells of this set that are doors. The symbol ∩ (intersection) means “cells in both sets”:

{0,2,3,4,5,6,8,9}∩{1,3,7} ={3}

Figure 3 plays these steps, one set per row. Watch the reading cross out the wall cells, each door cell fan out to the three cells the move can reach, and the second reading cross out every cell but cell 3. Its last stages replay the second reading with a sensor that can be wrong (Section 3.2).

3.2 Why a set is not enough

Now use the real sensor, which reads “door” at a wall 20 percent of the time. A reading of “door” is then possible in every cell. In symbols:

  • S is the set of cells the robot could be in before the reading, such as {0,2,3,4,5,6,8,9} after the move of Section 3.1;
  • {0,1,…,9} is the set of cells where “door” can be read, which is now all 10 cells.

So the intersection ∩ removes nothing:

S∩{0,1,…,9}=S

The set never shrinks, however many readings arrive (the last stages of Figure 3). Crossing places out only works for a perfect sensor, for the reason shown in why noisy sensors forbid crossing places outExplained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §3.3 Why noisy sensors forbid crossing places out: a wrong reading would cross out the true cell. The set also cannot say that cell 3 fits the readings much better than cell 9: both are simply “possible”. We need a summary that keeps a number for every cell, saying how strongly the readings point to it. Probabilities are that number.

4. Summary 2: a probability for every cell

Key point

The belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in §4.2 The definition. gives every cell the probability that the robot is there, given its whole history. A reading sharpens it; a move shifts it and blurs it.

4.1 The belief, as a picture

The second thing the robot can keep in memory: instead of “possible” or “impossible”, one probability for each of the 10 cells. Together they form a probability distribution ()Probability distribution: A list (or curve) of every possible outcome of a random variable with its probability, adding up to 1; it says how likely each outcome is, so the chance of any event can be worked out from it. Explained in: Random Variables and Probability Distributions, §3. Probability distributions as tables (a list of every outcome with its probability, adding to 1). Figure 4 plays the running example.

Figure 4: The belief over the 10 cells in five stages: uniform at 0.1; after reading door, three peaks (modes) of 0.19 at the doors; after moving 2 cells, the modes sit at cells 3, 5 and 9; because the move is uncertain, they spread to 0.16; after reading door again, one main mode of 0.31 at cell 3. An arrow marks the true cell

What to watch in each stage:

  1. No idea yet. The robot has no reason to prefer any cell, so each gets the same share:

    101​=0.1

  2. Reads “door”. The three door cells grow and the seven wall cells shrink. The wall cells do not drop to zero, because the sensor reads “door” at a wall 20 percent of the time. Each door cell is now a peak of the belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in §4.2 The definition., a mode ()Mode: The value that appears most often in the data, so it sits under the highest peak of a histogram or a distribution curve; it gives a centre for data that are not numbers, and a belief with several peaks has several modes. Explained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §5. Mode, so the belief has three modes: it is multimodal ()Multimodal: Having more than one mode, that is more than one peak (two peaks: bimodal), such as a robot’s belief peaking at three doors; it shows the data point to several separate values, so one mean or one best guess would hide them. Explained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §5. Mode.

  3. Moves 2 cells. Every mode moves 2 cells to the right, with the robot.

  4. The move is uncertain. The move may have been 1 or 3 cells, so each mode spreads onto its neighbours and gets lower (0.19 becomes 0.16).

  5. Reads “door” again. Only the mode at cell 3 sits on a door, so it grows to 0.31 while the others shrink. The tallest bar is the true cell.

Why do the door cells get 0.19 in stage 2? The sensor reads “door” 0.6 of the time at a door and 0.2 at a wall, so each door cell keeps 3 shares to a wall cell’s 1, and with 3 doors and 7 walls a door cell gets 3 of the 16 shares, 0.1875, shown rounded to 0.19 (why noisy sensors forbid crossing places outExplained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §3.3 Why noisy sensors forbid crossing places out). The exact rule behind this share-counting is Bayes’ theorem, applied at every reading by the correction step ()Correction step (measurement update): The Bayes-filter step after a reading: multiply each state’s predicted belief by the measurement probability p(zt​∣xt​) and rescale so the beliefs add to 1; it raises the states that explain the reading and sharpens the belief. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.3 The correction step.

4.2 The definition

The robot’s probability for each cell, given everything it knows, is its belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in §4.2 The definition.. Each symbol:

  • t is the time step, here 2;
  • bel(xt​) is the belief, the probability that the robot is in cell xt​ at time t, a number from 0 to 1 (0.31 for cell 3 at t=2);
  • xt​ is the cell at time t, any cell number from 0 to 9; the belief has one value for every possible cell;
  • p(…) is the probability of what stands left of the bar;
  • z1:t​ means all readings from time 1 to t, here (z1​,z2​)=(door,door) at t=2;
  • u1:t​ means all commands from time 1 to t, here (stay,move 2);
  • the bar ∣ is read “given”, as in a conditional probability ()Conditional probability: The probability of an event given that another event has happened: P(A∣B); it updates a probability with what is now known, such as the chance of reading “door” once the robot is known to be at a door. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Conditional Probability, §2. The definition.

Written out:

bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​, u1:t​)

The right-hand side conditions on z1:t​ and u1:t​: exactly the information state ()Information state (history): Everything a robot knows at time t: its starting knowledge plus every command and every reading so far, such as (stay, door, move 2, door); every decision must be based on it, because the true state is never seen. At t=2 in our run:

bel(x2​=3)=0.31 bel(x2​=9)=0.10

Unlike the set of Section 3, the belief says that cell 3 fits the readings three times better than cell 9.

4.3 A belief is not a position

The robot is always in exactly one cell. A belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in §4.2 The definition. of 0.19 on three cells does not mean the robot is spread over three places; it means the robot does not know which of the three it is in (Labbe ch.2). The belief describes the robot’s knowledge, not the world. For the same reason, the arrow for the true cell and the bars in Figure 4 are drawn separately: the bars can be wrong, and the true cell does not care.

4.4 Why the whole history appears in the definition

The definition conditions on every command and reading since the start, which looks like the long list we wanted to avoid. The history is there because the robot does not know its earlier cells either: if it knew its previous cell xt−1​, that one cell plus the latest command and reading would be enough, but it only has the history (PR §2.3.4). Why does it depend on xt​ alone? Once the true cell is known, how the robot got there and what it read before tell us nothing more about what it will read now (PR §2.3.3). Depending on the current cell alone is the Markov property ()Markov property: The property of a sequence of states that the future depends on the past only through the present state; it lets a filter forget the history and work from the latest state alone. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.3 Complete state and the Markov property applied to readings.

Why do we write p(z∣x), reading given place, and not p(x∣z), place given reading? Because p(z∣x) is the one we can measure. We park the robot in front of a door 100 times and count how often it reads “door”; doing the same at a wall gives the other row. This direction, from cause to effect, is easy to collect; the other direction, from effect back to cause, is what Bayes’ theorem computes from it (Labbe ch.2). For a real range sensor the table becomes the beam model ()Beam model: The measurement model of a range finder that mixes four densities for one reading (measurement noise, unexpected objects, failures, random readings) with weights that add up to 1, so no single odd reading can rule out the true pose. Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §5.1 The mixture.

Together the two tables say what each cell and each reading depends on; Section 6 draws those links as one picture.

6.1 A small piece of maths: Bayesian networks

The two tables of Section 5 say what depends on what: the next cell on the cell and the command, the reading on the cell alone. A Bayesian network ()Bayesian network: A graph of random variables drawn as circles, with an arrow into each variable from the variables its probability depends on directly; each circle holds one table of its probability given those variables, and multiplying the tables gives the probability of everything together, so one huge table splits into small ones. Explained in §6.1 A small piece of maths: Bayesian networks. draws each variable as a circle, with arrows into it from the variables its probability depends on directly. Each circle has one table, its probability given the circles with arrows into it, and the probability of all variables together is the product of these tables (CS188 §6.3).
