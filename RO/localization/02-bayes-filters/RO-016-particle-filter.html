Particle Filter

1. Overview

Key point

A particle filter keeps the robot’s belief as a cloud of guesses. Each reading reweighs the guesses, resampling keeps the good ones, and each move shifts them all; the cloud can take any shape, including several peaks at once.

Figure 1: A corridor that loops round a block, opened out from 0 to 10 m, with three doors set back into the right-hand wall; the robot at 2.1 m reads 1.46 m to the side. Below: the map, the reading expected at each position

So far the robot can run the Bayes filter with a histogram filter ()Histogram filter (discrete Bayes filter): The Bayes filter run on a space cut into cells, keeping one probability per cell; sensing multiplies each cell by p(z∣x) and rescales, moving slides and blurs the list. It makes the Bayes filter computable for a continuous position. Explained in: Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter, §2.1 Why cells: one probability per cell of the 10-cell hallway. That breaks when the state grows: a floor robot’s pose (x,y,θ) already needs 7.2 million cellsExplained in: Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter, §3.1 Why the number of cells explodes. This Note keeps the belief as a cloud of samples instead, placed only where the robot may be.

A robot is somewhere in the corridor of Figure 1. It has a map of the corridor, but it does not know where it is. Its side sensor reads 1.46 m, which means “a door is beside me”. Three doors fit that reading, so the reading’s answer to “where am I?” is “at door 1, door 2 or door 3, about equally likely”: a belief with a peak at each door. These peaks are the belief’s three modes ()Mode: The value that appears most often in the data, so it sits under the highest peak of a histogram or a distribution curve; it gives a centre for data that are not numbers, and a belief with several peaks has several modes. Explained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §5. Mode, so the belief is multimodal ()Multimodal: Having more than one mode (two modes: bimodal). Explained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §5. Mode.

The Bayes filter ()Bayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.4 The algorithm gives the rule for updating such a belief. Two other ways of running it struggle here:

  • the grid filter, or histogram filter ()Histogram filter (discrete Bayes filter): The Bayes filter run on a space cut into cells, keeping one probability per cell; sensing multiplies each cell by p(z∣x) and rescales, moving slides and blurs the list. It makes the Bayes filter computable for a continuous position. Explained in: Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter, §2.1 Why cells, stores one number per cell, which is fine on a 10 m line but grows very fast with the number of state variables;
  • the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system stores the belief as a single bell-shaped curve, a Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is, and one Gaussian has only one mode (Section 3.1).

The particle filter stores the belief as a set of sample guesses instead. This Note builds it on the corridor of Figure 1:

  • why a cloud of samples can stand for a belief, and how accurate it is (Section 3);
  • the three steps, move, weigh and resample, worked by hand and then run on the corridor (Section 4);
  • the low-variance sampler, the standard way to resample (Section 4.3);
  • what goes wrong with too few particles, and the fixes (Section 5).

2. The running example and the Bayes filter in brief

Key point

To tell the three doors apart, the robot has a map, a noisy side sensor and wheels that slip. The Bayes filter turns these into a belief with two steps per round: predict with the motion, update with the reading.

2.2 The Bayes filter in brief

The robot’s belief bel(xt​) is a probability distribution over where it is at time t, given every command and reading so far (belief ())Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition. The Bayes filter ()Bayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.4 The algorithm updates it in two steps per round.

Predict with the command ut​: every place xt−1​ the robot may have been sends probability to the places the move may take it.

bel(xt​)=∫p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​) bel(xt−1​) dxt−1​

Update with the reading zt​: multiply by how well each place explains the reading, then rescale so the total is 1.

bel(xt​)=η p(zt​∣xt​) bel(xt​)

Here bel (read “bel bar”) is the predicted belief and η is the number that makes the total 1. The update is Bayes’ theorem ()Bayes’ theorem: The rule that reverses a conditional probability: from P(B∣A) it gives P(A∣B), so a belief about A can be updated after seeing B; P(A∣B)=P(B∣A)P(A)/P(B). Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §4. The formula and its proof with the predicted belief as the prior.

The two formulas are exact, but they hold whole curves, and an integral over a curve cannot be computed for most maps and sensors (Labbe ch.12). Every Bayes-filter variant is a way of storing the curve so that the two steps become sums a computer can do. The particle filter stores it as samples.

3. A belief as a cloud of samples

Key point

The three-door belief of Figure 1 has modes that one Gaussian cannot hold, but many samples drawn from it can: the share of samples in a region estimates the probability of that region. Giving each sample a weight lets us change the distribution without moving the samples.

3.1 Why one Gaussian is not enough

The simplest way to store the curve would be one Gaussian. After the second reading, the exact belief (computed on a fine grid, Section 4.5) has two modes, one at door 2 and one at door 3 (Figure 2). The robot has seen “door, then 2.5 m later door again”, and two places fit.

Figure 2: The exact belief after the second reading has two modes, at door 2 and door 3; the single Gaussian with the same mean and standard deviation peaks at 5.73 m, in front of plain wall

Try to describe this belief by one Gaussian, as a Kalman filter must. The best we can do is a Gaussian with the same mean and standard deviation:

mean=5.73 m standard deviation=1.31 m

Figure 2 shows the result: the red curve peaks at 5.73 m, in front of plain wall, the one stretch where the last reading says the robot cannot be. One Gaussian has one mode, so it cannot hold “here or there” (Labbe ch.12). We need a way of storing a belief that can take any shape.

3.2 Samples stand for a distribution: the Monte Carlo idea

Suppose we could draw guesses of the robot’s position from its belief, many of them. Where the belief is high, guesses would fall often; where it is low, rarely. Then the guesses alone would answer every question about the belief: “how likely is door 3?” becomes “what share of the guesses is at door 3?“.

Estimating a probability or an average by counting random samples is a Monte Carlo estimate ()Monte Carlo estimate: An estimate of a probability or an average made by counting or averaging random samples, such as the share of samples at door 3; it needs no formula for the distribution, and its standard deviation shrinks like one over the square root of the number of samples. Explained in §3.2 Samples stand for a distribution: the Monte Carlo idea. (Labbe ch.12). It needs no formula for the distribution’s shape, which is why it can follow two modes, a step or anything else.

Its price is randomness: a different set of samples gives a slightly different answer. More samples give a steadier answer. In our corridor, the share of weight at door 3 after the third reading is exactly 0.956. Estimated by the particle filter of Section 4 with M particles, repeated 1000 times (notebook):

Samples MAverage estimateStandard deviation
1000.9500.030
10000.9560.008

Ten times the samples cut the standard deviation by a little under four times, close to the square root of 10, which is 3.16. The standard deviation of an average of independent draws, its standard error ()Standard error: How much a statistic, such as the sample mean, changes from one sample to the next, so how precise it is as an estimate. It is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution; for the mean, σ/n​. Explained in: Sampling Distributions and the Central Limit Theorem, §4.2 Mean and variance of the sample means, shrinks at that same rate.

Figure 3 shows the same thing as a picture: the particles’ histogram after the second reading, for three sample sizes, against the exact belief. With 20 samples the bars around the two modes are ragged; with 2000 the bars follow the curve.

Figure 3: The particle histogram after the second reading against the exact belief: with 20 particles the bars are ragged, with 200 closer, with 2000 they follow the two modes

3.3 Weighted samples

In a particle filter each sample is a particle ()Particle: One sample guess of the full state, such as a position of 1.6 m, together with a weight; a particle filter keeps M of them so that together they stand for the belief. Explained in §3.3 Weighted samples.: one guess of the full state, here one position x[m], with a number attached to it, its weight w[m]. The superscript [m] counts the particles from 1 to M. The belief is the whole set:

bel(xt​)≈{⟨xt[m]​, wt[m]​⟩}

The probability of a region is the sum of the weights of the particles in it (with weights that add up to 1). Equal weights give the plain sample count of Section 3.2. What is left is how to move and reweigh the particles as each command and reading arrives.

4. The particle filter, step by step

Key point

Each round moves every particle by the command plus its own random slip, weighs it by how well it explains the reading, and resamples the set in proportion to the weights.

This is how the robot of Figure 1 gets from three doors to one: the particle filter ()Particle filter: A Bayes filter that stores the belief as a set of weighted samples (particles) and runs each round as move, weigh, resample; it can hold beliefs of any shape, such as a belief with three modes, one at each of three doors. Explained in §4. The particle filter, step by step. runs the two Bayes-filter steps on particles (Figure 4). We work each step by hand first, then run all of them on the corridor.

Figure 4: One round of the particle filter: move every particle by the command plus its own random slip; weigh each by p(z | x); resample M new particles in proportion to the weights

4.1 Start: spread the particles evenly

The robot has no idea where it is, so every position is equally likely. We draw M=100 positions at random, evenly between 0 and 10 m, and give each the weight

w[m]=1/M =1/100=0.01

Frame 1 of Figure 7 (Section 4.5) shows this cloud.

4.2 Weigh: the update step

Key point

A particle’s weight is the height of the sensor curve, centred on the reading the particle expects, at the reading we got.

The idea. The reading is 1.46 m. A particle in front of a door expects 1.5 m: close, so it explains the reading well. A particle beside plain wall expects 1.0 m: 0.46 m off, more than three sensor standard deviations, so it explains the reading badly. The weight turns “explains well” into a number.

The weight. For each particle we ask the sensor model how likely the reading z is if the robot were where the particle says:

w[m]=p(z∣x[m])

The result is the importance weight ()Importance weight: The weight a particle gets from a reading: the height of the sensor model p(z∣x) at that reading, normalised over all particles; it says how well the particle explains what the robot sensed. Explained in §4.2 Weigh: the update step. of the particle. It is the height of the Gaussian, centred on the particle’s expected reading h(x[m]), at the reading z (Figure 5). It is a height, not an area: the area under a density over a single point is zero, and the height is what a likelihood ()Likelihood: How probable the observed data is under given parameter values; read as a function of the parameters with the data fixed. Explained in: Probability vs Likelihood, §2.2 Likelihood: from the event back to the parameter is. The height is a density, not a probability, so it can be larger than 1.

Figure 5: Left: five particles on the corridor, three at doors (blue) and two beside wall (grey). Right: the sensor’s Gaussian centred on 1.5 m for a door particle and on 1.0 m for a wall particle; at the reading 1.46 m the heights are 2.567 and 0.024

4.2.1 Why weighting gives the right belief

After the move step (Section 4.4), the particles are samples of the predicted belief bel(x). The update of Section 2.2 multiplies that belief by p(z∣x) and rescales. Giving each particle the weight p(z∣x) and normalising does that same update on the cloud, so the weighted cloud stands for the updated belief bel(x) (Freiburg PF slides). It is the multiply-then-rescale of the histogram filter’s sense stepExplained in: Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter, §2.2 Sense: multiply, then rescale, with particles in place of cells.

4.3 Resample: copy the heavy particles, drop the light ones

Key point

Resampling draws a fresh set of M particles from the weighted set, each pick in proportion to weight, so the computation goes where the belief is. The low-variance sampler does it with one random number and keeps every particle whose weight is at least 1/M.

Why resample. After weighing, the two wall particles carry a weight of 0.0031 each; after a few more readings, most of 100 particles would carry weights near zero. They still cost a motion step and a sensor check each round but add almost nothing to the belief. Worse, a particle’s weight only ever shrinks or grows by multiplication, so a few particles soon carry nearly all the weight and the cloud behaves as if it had two or three particles (Labbe ch.12 calls this degeneracy).

The idea. Draw M new particles from the weighted set, with replacement ()With replacement: Sampling in which each drawn item is put back, so it can be drawn again. Explained in: Bagging: Bootstrap Aggregation, §2.3 Drawing with replacement, each draw picking particle i with probability w[i]. A heavy particle is picked several times, a light one probably never. The new particles all get the weight 1/M again: the copies have taken the place of the weight. This step is resampling ()Resampling (particle filter): Drawing a fresh set of M particles, with replacement, from the weighted set, each pick in proportion to weight, then giving all weight 1/M; heavy particles are copied and light ones dropped, so computation goes where the belief is. Explained in §4.3 Resample: copy the heavy particles, drop the light ones.; it is the same “draw rows in proportion to weight” as upsampling in AdaBoostUpsampling (resampling by weight): Drawing a new dataset of the same size in which each row is picked with probability equal to its weight, so heavily weighted rows appear more often for the next model. Explained in: AdaBoost Step by Step, §9. Step 7: upsampling, a new dataset drawn by weight.

Plain resampling. Lay the weights end to end along the line from 0 to 1, so each particle owns a stretch as long as its weight (Figure 6, bottom). Draw M independent random numbers between 0 and 1; each picks the particle whose stretch it lands in. This is multinomial resampling ()Multinomial resampling: Plain resampling: M independent random numbers each pick a particle in proportion to weight; simple, but a good particle can get no copy by chance. Explained in §4.3 Resample: copy the heavy particles, drop the light ones.. With our five weights, one draw of five numbers gave:

Particle 3 carried a third of the belief and got no copy, by bad luck alone. The chance of that, with five independent draws each missing it with probability 0.6687:

0.66875=0.134

A good guess lost by chance is never recovered, because resampling only copies existing particles. Section 5 shows where that leads.

The low-variance sampler. Instead of M independent random numbers, draw one random start r between 0 and 1/M, and place M pointers exactly 1/M apart from it (Figure 6, top). Each pointer picks the particle it lands on. This is the low-variance sampler ()Low-variance sampler (systematic resampling): A way to resample with one random start between 0 and 1/M and M pointers 1/M apart along the stacked weights; it keeps every particle with weight at least 1/M, adds little randomness and runs in time proportional to M. Explained in §4.3 Resample: copy the heavy particles, drop the light ones., also called systematic resampling (Freiburg PF slides; Labbe ch.12).

  • No good particle is lost. A particle whose weight is at least 1/M owns a stretch at least as long as the pointer spacing, so at least one pointer must land in it. Particles 2, 3 and 5 (weight 0.3313, above 0.2) are always kept (Freiburg PF slides).

4.4 Move: the predict step

Key point

Each particle moves by the command plus its own random slip, drawn from the motion model, so copies spread apart again.

The idea. The robot is commanded 2.5 m. Each particle is one guess of where the robot was, so each moves 2.5 m too. The wheels slip, so we add to each particle its own random slip, drawn from the normal distribution with standard deviation σu​=0.15 m. For a particle at 2.0 m whose slip draw is +0.08 m:

2.0+2.5+0.08=4.58 m

Each particle’s new position is one sample from p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​), so the whole set is a sample from the predicted belief bel(xt​): the integral of Section 2.2 is done by sampling, with no integral computed (Freiburg PF slides; sampling a motion modelExplained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §5. Drawing sample poses).

Why each particle gets its own slip. After resampling, particle 2 exists twice at exactly 1.6 m. With the same slip for both, the copies would stay identical forever, and after a few rounds the cloud would be a handful of points repeated many times. Independent slips spread the copies apart, so the cloud keeps covering the places the robot may be (Labbe ch.12 calls the collapse sample impoverishment).

One answer from a cloud, and a trap. A controller usually wants one position. The usual choice is the weighted mean of the particles:

x^=m=1∑M​w[m] x[m]

After reading 3 it gives 7.05 m; the robot is at 7.08 m. After reading 2, the same formula gives 5.73 m, between the two clusters, in front of plain wall (the black diamond in frame 5 of Figure 7). The weighted mean is a good answer only when the cloud has one cluster; with several, report the heaviest cluster or keep the whole cloud (Labbe ch.12).

4.6 The algorithm

Putting the steps together gives the particle filter of Thrun Table 4.3, as also given in the Freiburg PF slides. Inputs: the particle set Xt−1​ from the last round, the command ut​ and the reading zt​.

  1. For each particle m=1,…,M:
    • move: sample xt[m]​ from p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1[m]​);
    • weigh: set wt[m]​=p(zt​∣xt[m]​).
  2. Normalise the weights so they add up to 1.
  3. Resample: draw M particles from the weighted set in proportion to the weights (low-variance sampler); they form Xt​, each with weight 1/M.

Every line uses the models only to draw a sample or to evaluate a density. No model needs to be linear, smooth or Gaussian: our map is a step, which a Kalman filter could not use (Section 3.1). With 100 particles the loop found the right door; with fewer, it can fail.

5. Particle deprivation

Key point

If no particle is near the true state, no step can create one there, and the filter settles on a wrong answer. More particles and fewer, smarter resamplings are the fixes.

5.1 Too few particles

The cloud can hold the three doors of Figure 1 only if some particles sit at each of them. Resampling only copies particles that exist, and the move step only shifts them a little. So once every particle near the true position has been dropped, the filter has no way back. Losing the particles near the true state is particle deprivation ()Particle deprivation: The failure in which no particles are left near the true state, so the filter settles confidently on a wrong answer; caused by too few particles or unlucky resampling, and fought with more particles and fewer resamplings. Explained in §5.1 Too few particles. (Thrun §4.3).

With few particles it happens often. We ran the corridor example 1000 times for each particle count and counted how often the filter ended at the right door (Figure 8):

Figure 8: Share of 1000 runs that end at the right door: 43.9 percent with 5 particles, 63.0 with 10, 87.8 with 20, 99.6 with 50, 100 with 100

With 5 particles, the start often has no particle at door 1, the robot’s true door, or resampling drops the few there; the filter then follows whatever survived, with full confidence. Too few particles make the filter overconfident: it reports one tight cluster at the wrong door. The average share it gives door 3 shows the same bias: 0.432 with 5 particles against the exact 0.956 (notebook); a small particle set gives a biased belief (Thrun §4.3).

The problem grows with the number of state variables. A cloud of 100 points covers a 10 m line densely, but spread over a room’s (x,y,θ) the same 100 points leave wide gaps, so the number of particles needed grows very fast with the state’s dimension (Labbe ch.12).

5.2 Other fixes

  • More particles. The direct fix, paid for in computation (Labbe ch.12).

  • Resample less often. Each resampling throws away variety. One rule resamples only when the weights have become uneven: when the effective number of particles

    N^eff​=1/∑m​(w[m])2

    falls below half of M (Labbe ch.12). With equal weights it equals M; with all weight on one particle it equals 1.

  • A very accurate sensor is a trap. If the sensor curve is much narrower than the gaps between particles, almost every particle gets a weight near zero and the cloud collapses onto one or two. Pretending the sensor is noisier than it is, or using more particles, keeps enough particles alive (Labbe ch.12).

6. Summary

Moveeach particle moves by u plus its own slipsamples the predicted belief, no integral needed
Weighw=p(z∣x), then normaliseweighting by p(z∣x) and rescaling turns the predicted cloud into the updated belief
Resampledraw M new particles in proportion to weightputs the computation where the belief is
  • The robot of Figure 1, which saw a door and could be at any of three (about 0.33 each), ends after three readings and two moves with 0.96 of its belief at door 3, where it is, because the cloud held all three doors until the readings could tell them apart.
  • A cloud of samples can stand for any belief, because the share of samples in a region estimates its probability; the estimate’s standard deviation shrinks like one over the square root of M (0.030 at 100 particles, 0.008 at 1000).
  • One Gaussian cannot hold “door 2 or door 3”: its peak lands at 5.73 m, in front of wall, so a multimodal belief needs a filter whose belief can take any shape, such as particles.
  • A particle’s weight is the height of the sensor curve at the reading (2.567 at a door, 0.024 at a wall), because weighting by p(z∣x) and normalising is the multiply-and-rescale of the update step.
  • Resampling with the low-variance sampler keeps every particle with weight at least 1/M and halves the randomness of plain resampling, so good guesses are not lost by chance.
  • Each particle gets its own slip in the move step, so copies spread out and the cloud keeps covering the possible positions.
  • The weighted mean is a safe answer only for one cluster; with two it can fall in front of a wall.
  • Too few particles cause deprivation (43.9 percent right with 5 particles, 100 percent with 100).

The robot can now keep a belief of any shape, such as three doors at once. Still missing: every particle costs a motion step and a sensor check each round, and when the belief is a single Gaussian, two numbers, a mean and a variance, carry all of it, which is the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible.
