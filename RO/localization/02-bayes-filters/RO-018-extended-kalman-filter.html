1. Overview

Key point

Most robot sensors and motions are nonlinear functions of the state, and a nonlinear function bends a Gaussian out of shape. The extended Kalman filter replaces each nonlinear function by its tangent line at the current estimate and then runs the ordinary Kalman filter; checking its innovations ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance tells us whether its variance can be trusted.

Now the robot has a new sensor in the same corridor: a radio beacon fixed on the wall at 6 m along the corridor, 2 m to the side, and it measures its straight-line distance to the beacon (Figure 1). Such range sensors are common: radio beacons, GPS ranges to satellites, a lidar’s range to a landmark.

The reading is no longer the position plus noise. Its graph against the position is a curve (Figure 1), so no fixed multiple of the position plus a constant gives it: the reading is a nonlinear function ()Nonlinear function: A function whose graph is not a straight line, so it is not a fixed multiple of the input plus a constant, such as the beacon distance h(x)=(6−x)2+4​; it bends a Gaussian into another shape, which is why the Kalman filter cannot use it as it is and the extended Kalman filter linearises it. Explained in §1. Overview. of the position:

  • far from the beacon, the distance changes almost one for one with position, nearly a straight line;
  • under the beacon, the distance stops changing: it bottoms out at 2 m;
  • past the beacon, it grows again, so 4 m and 8 m give the same reading.

The Kalman filter needs linear models (Section 2), so it cannot use this reading as it is. This Note adds the extended Kalman filter, which straightens the curve at the current estimate, and a check that tells when the result has gone wrong:

  • pushing a Gaussian through a nonlinear function, and the tangent-line fix (Section 3);
  • the extended Kalman filter, step by step on the corridor (Section 4);
  • its limits: one Gaussian can hold only one side (Section 4.4);
  • consistency, whether the filter’s variance matches its real errors: the innovation, its covariance, and the NIS and NEES tests (Section 5).

2. The Kalman filter in brief, and why nonlinear functions break it

Key point

The Kalman filter is exact only because linear functions ()Linear function: A function y=ax+b: a fixed multiple of the input plus a constant, whose graph is a straight line; passing a normal variable through it gives another normal variable, which is why the Kalman filter is exact for linear models. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.3 A linear function keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian, whose graphs are straight lines, keep a Gaussian a Gaussian. A nonlinear function, such as the beacon distance, does not.

To see why the nonlinear beacon distance of Figure 1 breaks it, recall what the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system does. The Kalman filter keeps the belief as a mean x^ and a variance p and runs two steps per round:

  • predict: move the mean by the command, 0.5 m, and add the process noise Q, the variance of each step’s wheel slip, 0.01 m², to the variance;
  • update: blend in the reading z with the Kalman gain ()Kalman gain K: The weight the update gives to the reading, K=pˉ​/(pˉ​+R) in one dimension: the weight that makes the updated variance smallest, near 1 when the prediction is vague and near 0 when the reading is. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.3 The best weight: the Kalman gain, which gives the reading the weight that makes the new variance smallest.

Both steps rest on one fact: a linear function keeps a Gaussian a GaussianLinear function: A function y=ax+b: a fixed multiple of the input plus a constant, whose graph is a straight line; passing a normal variable through it gives another normal variable, which is why the Kalman filter is exact for linear models. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.3 A linear function keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian, with the variance multiplied by the slope squared. The update also assumed the reading was a linear function of the position: a fixed number H times the position, plus the sensor’s noise. Our new reading is the straight-line distance to the beacon, 6 m along the corridor and 2 m to the side. With:

  • x is the robot’s position along the corridor, in m;
  • h(x) is the distance from position x to the beacon, the reading before the sensor’s noise, in m.

By Pythagoras, from the gap along the corridor and the 2 m across:

h(x)=(6−x)2+22​

For example, at 0.5 m:

h(0.5)=5.52+4​ =34.25​=5.852 m

No single number H turns position into this reading everywhere, so the five lines of the Kalman filter cannot be used as they are. The first guess is that of the Kalman filter: the estimate x^ is 0 m, with variance p of 1 m². The beacon’s distance readings have a standard deviation of 0.1 m, so the measurement noise ()Measurement noise R: The random error of a sensor reading, given as its variance or covariance R; it sets how much the filter trusts each reading and is taken from the datasheet or measured in a calibration. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.1 Two estimates of one position is R=0.01 m². Before the filter can use these readings, we need to know what the beacon distance does to a Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is belief, a bell-shaped curve over positions.

3. Pushing a Gaussian through a nonlinear function

Key point

A nonlinear function ()Nonlinear function: A function whose graph is not a straight line, so it is not a fixed multiple of the input plus a constant, such as the beacon distance h(x)=(6−x)2+4​; it bends a Gaussian into another shape, which is why the Kalman filter cannot use it as it is and the extended Kalman filter linearises it. Explained in §1. Overview., one whose graph is a curve, bends a Gaussian into a lopsided shape. Near a point, its graph looks like its tangent line, and a line keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian; that approximation is good when the Gaussian is narrow compared with the bend.

3.1 What a nonlinear function does to a Gaussian

The worst place is near the beacon, where the distance bottoms out at 2 m (Figure 1). Suppose the robot’s position there is a Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is, a bell-shaped curve, with mean 5.5 m and standard deviation (sd) 0.5 m. What does the predicted reading look like? We cannot use a formula yet, so we use the Monte CarloExplained in: Particle Filter, §3.2 Samples stand for a distribution: the Monte Carlo idea way: draw 200,000 positions from the Gaussian, push each through the beacon distance h, and look at the results (notebook).

Figure 2 shows the result in its frame with input mean 5.5 m, sd 0.5 m. The readings pile up against 2 m, the smallest possible distance, with a long tail upwards: the Gaussian has become lopsided. Its lopsidedness, the skewness ()Skewness: A number for how lopsided a distribution is: 0 symmetric, positive right tail, negative left tail. Explained in: Univariate Analysis: Exploring One Column at a Time, §10. Skewness, is 1.89, where a Gaussian has 0. A Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system has no way to store that shape: it keeps only a mean and a variance. To keep the belief Gaussian, the filter needs a straight line in place of the curve.

3.2 The tangent line: linearisation

Zoom in on any smooth curve and it looks straight. Near a point, the curve is close to its tangent line, the straight line that touches the curve there with the curve’s slope. Figure 3 zooms in on the beacon curve at two places at once, the window shrinking from 8 m to 0.1 m wide. Watch the gap between the curve and its tangent line: at 1 m it is already too small to see when the window is 2 m wide, while at 6 m, under the beacon, where the curve bends most, it is still visible at 0.5 m wide. So the tangent line can stand in for the curve close to where it touches, and the more the curve bends, the closer that has to be.

Figure 3: The beacon distance h(x) (black) and its tangent line (purple dashed) at x = 1 m (left, slope −0.928) and at x = 6 m under the beacon (right, slope 0), seen through a window that shrinks from 8 m through 2 m and 0.5 m to 0.1 m wide, with both axes on the same scale. Each panel gives the largest gap between curve and line inside the window, as a share of the window’s height: at 1 m it falls from 7.1% to 0.77%, 0.17% and 0.032%; at 6 m from 31% to 12%, 3.1% and 0.62%. The last frame, 0.1 m wide: in both panels the curve cannot be told from its tangent line

Replacing a function near a point by its tangent line is linearisation ()Linearisation: Replacing a function near a point by its tangent line (its first-order Taylor polynomial), so that a curve can be handled as a straight line close to that point, where straight-line methods apply. Explained in: Derivatives of One Variable, §6.3 Degree 1: the tangent line. In Figure 2 the tangent line is the purple dashed line, drawn at the input’s mean. With:

  • μ is the position where the line touches the curve, in m (1 m in the example below);
  • h(μ) is the beacon distance there, in m;
  • h′(μ) is the derivative ()Derivative: The slope of a function at a point: how fast the output changes as the input changes there. It is zero at the lowest point of a smooth curve, which is how training finds a minimum. Explained in: Derivatives of One Variable, §4.1 From secant to tangent of h at μ, the slope of the curve there, in metres of distance per metre of position;
  • x is a position near μ, in m.
h(x)≈h(μ)+h′(μ)(x−μ)

The beacon distance is the square root of an inside part, so the chain rule ()Chain rule: To differentiate a function of a function, multiply the outer derivative by the inner derivative. Explained in: The Derivative of the Sigmoid Function, §2. Two rules we need gives its slope: the slope of the square root times the slope of the inside part. With:

  • s(x) is the part under the square root, in m², 29 m² at 1 m;
  • dh/ds is the slope of h against s, in m per m², 0.0928 at 1 m;
  • ds/dx is the slope of s against x, in m² per m, −10 at 1 m.

At 1 m first, each line followed by the same step at any position x:

s(1)=(6−1)2+4=29 s(x)=(6−x)2+4 h(1)=29​=5.3852 h(x)=s(x)​ dsdh​=2×5.38521​=0.0928 dsdh​=2s​1​=2h(x)1​ dxds​=−2×(6−1)=−10 dxds​=−2(6−x) h′(1)=0.0928×(−10)=−0.928 h′(x)=2h(x)1​×(−2)(6−x) =h(x)−(6−x)​

Driving 1 m towards the beacon from 1 m cuts the distance by about 0.93 m.

The tangent line is a straight line, and a straight line keeps a Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is a Gaussian. So the predicted reading is a Gaussian whose mean is h(μ) and whose standard deviation (sd) is the input’s sd times the size of the slope, where ∣h′(μ)∣ is the slope without its sign:

mean≈h(μ) sd≈∣h′(μ)∣×sd of x

Carrying a mean and a standard deviation through a function this way, with its value and slope at the mean, is first-order uncertainty propagation ()First-order uncertainty propagation: Carrying a mean and a standard deviation through a function by its value and slope at the mean: mean out ≈ h(μ), standard deviation out ≈ slope × standard deviation in (with a Jacobian, JPJT); accurate when the function is nearly straight across the standard deviation. Explained in §3.2 The tangent line: linearisation. (Labbe ch.9; Becker ch.13).

3.3 When the tangent line fails

The tangent line describes the curve well only near the point where it touches. The wider the Gaussian ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is, and the more the curve bends inside it, the worse the approximation (Labbe ch.9; Freiburg EKF slides). Three of the frames of Figure 2, with the notebook’s numbers:

Input GaussianTrue readings: mean, sdTangent line: mean, sd
mean 1.0, sd 0.5 (curve nearly straight)5.39, 0.465.39, 0.46
mean 5.5, sd 0.5 (near the bend)2.12, 0.142.06, 0.12
mean 5.5, sd 1.5 (near the bend, wide)2.48, 0.572.06, 0.36

Near the bend, the tangent-line Gaussian puts its mean too low and is too narrow, so a filter using it would be too sure of itself. The tangent-line Gaussian even gives probability to readings below 2 m, which no position can produce. Both failures grow with the width of the input: with standard deviation 1.5 m, the tangent line misses the true standard deviation by more than a third.

3.4 Several variables: the Jacobian

The beacon distance has one input, the position, so one slope is enough. A robot’s pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. A function of several numbers has a slope for each pair of output and input. The table of all these slopes is the Jacobian ()Jacobian: The table (matrix) of all first partial derivatives of a function with several inputs and outputs, one row per output and one column per input, Jij​=∂fi​/∂xj​; it shows how every output changes with every input near a point. For motion the Jacobian is called G, the motion Jacobian ()Motion Jacobian G: The table of slopes of the motion model at the last estimate, one row per new state variable and one column per old one; the EKF uses it to carry the covariance forward as GPGT+Q. The EKF predicts and updates with the nonlinear functions themselves, and uses their slopes at the current estimate wherever the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. 4.1 The algorithm

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) ()Extended Kalman filter (EKF): The Kalman filter for nonlinear motion and sensor models: it moves the mean through the nonlinear functions and uses their Jacobians at the latest estimate in place of F and H, so it can track unimodal beliefs through nonlinear models. Explained in §4.1 The algorithm. uses that tangent line to run the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system, which keeps a Gaussian belief as a mean and a covariance, on a nonlinear model such as the beacon distance: it linearises the nonlinear models at the latest estimate, every step (Freiburg EKF slides; Thrun §3.3; Labbe ch.11). 4.2 Step 1 by hand

On the corridor every symbol is one number. At step 1, with the step number as a subscript:

  • xˉ1​ is the predicted position, in m;
  • pˉ​1​ is its variance, in m²;
  • h(xˉ1​) is the reading the prediction expects, the beacon distance at xˉ1​, in m;
  • H is the measurement Jacobian, the slope of the beacon distance at xˉ1​, in metres of distance per metre of position;
  • z1​ is the beacon’s reading, 5.61 m;
  • S is the innovation variance, the variance the filter expects of the innovation, in m²;
  • K is the Kalman gain, which turns the innovation into a correction of the position, in metres of position per metre of distance;
  • x^1​ is the updated estimate, in m;
  • p1​ is its variance, in m².

Predict, as in the Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system, from the first guess, 0 m with variance 1 m², with the command 0.5 m and the slip’s variance Q, 0.01 m²:

xˉ1​=0+0.5=0.5 m pˉ​1​=1+0.01=1.01 m2

Linearise the sensor at the prediction. The expected reading and the slope, kept to four digits because the variance below subtracts two nearly equal numbers:

h(0.5)=5.852 H=−5.5/5.852=−0.9398

Innovation. The innovation ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance is the gap between the reading and the reading the prediction expected. The reading is z1​=5.61 m:

z1​−h(xˉ1​)=5.61−5.852=−0.242

The robot is 0.24 m closer to the beacon than predicted.

Gain. First the innovation variance, with the sensor’s variance R, 0.01 m²:

S=0.93982×1.01+0.01 =0.892+0.01=0.902

then

K=1.01×(−0.9398)/0.902 =−1.0523

The gain is negative because the slope is: a shorter distance than expected means the robot is further along, so a negative innovation must move the estimate forward. Its size above 1 is expected too: it converts metres of distance into metres of position, and here the distance changes a little less than one for one.

Update:

x^1​=0.5+(−1.0523)(−0.242) =0.5+0.255=0.755 m p1​=(1−(−1.0523)(−0.9398))×1.01 =0.01105×1.01=0.0112 m2

The true position is 0.73 m. A check. After a reading this good, the position should be known about as well as the sensor allows, which is the sensor’s standard deviation divided by the slope:

sd=0.1/0.94=0.106 m sd2=0.1062=0.0112 m2

matching p1​. The slope 0.94 set that precision, and the slope changes as the robot drives towards the beacon.

  • Near the beacon, where the distance bottoms out at 2 m (Figure 1), the slope goes to 0: the distance hardly changes with position, so a reading says almost nothing about where the robot is. 4.4 The limits of the EKF

One Gaussian holds one side. Positions 4 m and 8 m give the same reading, 2.83 m (Figure 1); so do 1 m and 11 m. Figure 5 feeds the same 16 readings, one step per frame, to a second EKF that starts at 11 m, on the far side of the beacon. Every reading fits the mirror position as well as the true one, so the filter follows the mirror path, confident and wrong: at step 1 it says 11.24 m when the robot is at 0.73 m, 10.5 m off, with only about 10 cm of uncertainty. Watch it move left while the robot moves right: only when the robot passes the beacon, where the mirror and the true path meet, does it come right (7.96 m against the true 7.86 m at step 16, notebook).

Explained in: Particle Filter, §4. The particle filter, step by step, which stores the belief as a cloud of samples, can keep both until the readings decide (Labbe ch.12).

The other limits (Freiburg EKF slides; Labbe ch.11):

  • Strongly nonlinear models. Where the Gaussian is wide compared with the bend (Section 3.3), the linearised covariance is wrong, usually too small, and the filter becomes overconfident.
  • Jacobians must exist. A model with steps, like the door map of the particle filter ()Particle filter: A Bayes filter that stores the belief as a set of weighted samples (particles) and runs each round as move, weigh, resample; it can hold beliefs of any shape, such as a belief with three modes, one at each of three doors. Explained in: Particle Filter, §4. The particle filter, step by step (1.0 m beside wall, 1.5 m at a door), has slope 0 almost everywhere and no slope at the edges, so the EKF cannot use it.
  • Jacobians must be derived. Deriving them by hand is error-prone for big models; symbolic tools such as SymPy can do it (Labbe ch.11).

The unscented Kalman filter ()Kalman filter: The Bayes filter for a linear Gaussian system: it keeps the belief as a mean and a (co)variance and updates them with a predict step and an update step, blending a model’s prediction with a sensor reading so the result is as certain as possible. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.5 The linear Gaussian system pushes a few chosen sample points through the nonlinear function instead of a tangent line, and the particle filter pushes many; both avoid Jacobians at a higher cost (Labbe ch.9; Freiburg EKF slides). Each of these limits can leave the EKF wrong while its variance says it is sure, so it needs a check.

5. Consistency: is the filter telling the truth about itself?

Key point

A filter is consistent when its real errors are as big as the variance it reports with its estimate: on average, error squared divided by variance comes to the number of variables. The innovations ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance allow the check without knowing the truth.

5.1 Why we need a check

To tell when the result has gone wrong, the filter’s own variance is no help. The EKF’s variance comes from tangent lines and from the noise variances we chose, the slip’s Q and the sensor’s R, 0.01 m² each, so it can be wrong without any error message: the mirror filter of Figure 5 reported about 10 cm of uncertainty while 10 m off. A controller or a planner that trusts a variance needs to know whether that variance matches the real errors. A filter whose errors match its own variance is consistent ()Consistent filter: A filter whose real errors are as large as its own reported covariance says, on average; only then can its uncertainty be trusted by a controller or planner. Explained in §5.1 Why we need a check. (Chen et al. 2018; Labbe ch.8). On a real robot the true errors are unknown, but the innovations ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance, the gaps between each reading and the reading the filter predicted, are not.

5.2 The innovation and its covariance

Every step, before it sees the reading, the filter predicts it. The filter also predicts how far off the reading will be. At step 1:

  • xˉ is the predicted position, 0.5 m;
  • h(xˉ) is the reading the filter predicts, the beacon distance at xˉ, 5.852 m;
  • z is the reading, 5.61 m;
  • H is the measurement Jacobian, the slope of the beacon distance at xˉ, −0.940;
  • Pˉ is the prediction’s covariance matrix, here one number, the variance 1.01 m²;
  • HT is the transpose of H, H flipped over its diagonal; for one number, H itself;
  • R is the sensor’s variance, 0.01 m²;
  • S is the innovation covariance, in m².

The innovation ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance z−h(xˉ) has two independent sources of error, the prediction’s and the sensor’s. The prediction’s error reaches the reading through the slope H, so its variance is multiplied by H2 (variance times slope squaredExplained in: Kalman Filter, §2.3 A linear function keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian); then the two independent variances addExplained in: Kalman Filter, §2.4 Adding independent errors adds variances:

S=HPˉHT+R

This S is the innovation covariance ()Innovation covariance S: The variance the filter expects of its next innovation, S=HPˉHT+R: prediction uncertainty seen through the sensor plus sensor noise; it scales the gain and is the yardstick for the NIS test. Explained in §5.2 The innovation and its covariance.: the variance the filter expects of its innovation (Labbe ch.8; Chen et al. 2018). At step 1, the innovation was −0.242 m with S=0.902 m², a predicted standard deviation of:

S​=0.902​=0.95 m

So an innovation of 0.24 m was well within what the filter expected. Unlike the true error, the innovation is known on a real robot at every step, so it is the everyday test.

5.3 NIS: the innovation test, no truth needed

To turn each innovation ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance into one test number, divide its square by its predicted variance. At step 1:

  • y is the innovation, the reading minus the reading the filter predicted, −0.242 m;
  • S is its predicted variance, the innovation covariance, 0.902 m²;
  • εz​ is the test number, with no unit.
εz​=y2/S

For several readings at once, y is a vector with one entry per reading, S is a matrix with one row and one column per reading, yT is y written as a row, and dividing by S becomes multiplying by its inverseExplained in: Multiple Linear Regression: The Normal Equation, §6. The normal equation S−1:

εz​=yTS−1y

The number εz​ is the normalised innovation squared (NIS) ()NIS (normalised innovation squared): The squared innovation divided by its predicted covariance, yTS−1y; for a consistent filter it follows chi-square with m degrees of freedom (one per reading) and averages m, so it tests consistency from readings alone, even on the robot. εz​=0.2422/0.902=0.065

Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. On one run. Figure 6 plots 60 innovations with the band ±2S​ the filter predicts. With the right sensor variance R, 0.01 m², 2 percent fall outside. 5.4 NEES: the error test, when the truth is known

In a simulation, or with a much better reference sensor, the true state is known and we can test the error itself. Divide the squared error by the filter’s variance. At step 1:

  • x is the true position, 0.73 m;
  • x^ is the filter’s estimate, 0.755 m;
  • p is the filter’s variance, 0.0112 m²;
  • εx​ is the test number, with no unit.
εx​=(x−x^)2/p

For a state of n numbers, the error x~, the true state minus the estimate, is a vector of n entries, P is the filter’s covariance matrix, n rows by n columns, and dividing by P becomes multiplying by its inverse P−1:

εx​=x~TP−1x~

The number εx​ is the normalised estimation error squared (NEES) ()NEES (normalised estimation error squared): The squared estimation error divided by the filter’s variance, x~TP−1x~; for a consistent filter it follows chi-square with n degrees of freedom and averages n, so its average over simulated runs tests consistency when the truth is known. Explained in §5.4 NEES: the error test, when the truth is known. (Labbe ch.8; Chen et al. 2018). By the same argument it follows chi-square with n degrees of freedom and averages n, here 1. For step 1:

εx​=(0.73−0.755)2/0.0112 =0.056

One value says little; the test averages many. 5.5 Reading the results

  • Both near 1: the filter’s variances match its real errors; it can be trusted.
  • Both well above 1: the filter is overconfident. Increase the noise it assumes; which one is told by tuning experiments such as those of choosing Q and RExplained in: Kalman Filter, §7.2 Q: tune the motion noise.
  • NEES rising over time: errors pile up faster than the filter allows, the mark of too small a slip variance Q: the filter trusts its motion model and stops listening to readings.
  • Values well below 1: the filter is too timid; its noises are too large, and it wastes information.

The innovations ()Innovation: The gap between a reading and the reading the prediction expected, z−Hxˉ; the update moves the estimate by the gain times this gap, and its size is used to check the filter. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §4.4 The updated estimate and its variance should also look like random noise: centred on zero and with no slow trend. A trend means the model misses something, such as a bias or too little process noise (Labbe ch.8). NEES needs the truth, so it is a design-time test in simulation; NIS needs only the readings, so it can also run on the robot.
