2. Why a robot needs a map

Key point

A map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in §2.1 What a map is for. is the robot’s model of its surroundings: a list of the objects that matter, with where they are. Localization compares readings with it, and planning searches it for routes.

2.1 What a map is for

A reading such as “the pink pole is 2.236 m away, to the left” says where things are relative to the robot. To turn it into “where am I?”, the robot needs to know where those things are in the room. That is what a map gives: the robot compares what it sees with the map and keeps the poses where the two agree. Planning needs the map too: a route to the door must go round the table.

A map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in §2.1 What a map is for. is a list of the objects in the environment that matter to the robot, with their positions and properties (Thrun et al. 2005 §6.2). We write it as a list of objects:

  • mi​ is object number i of the map, such as the pink pole at (1 m, 3 m);
  • N is the number of objects, 2 when the map holds only the two poles (Section 3);
  • m is the whole map, the list of all N objects.
m={m1​, m2​, …, mN​}

Fixed things, such as walls and furniture, belong in the map; things that move, such as people, belong in the robot’s state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.1 State: from one number to many instead.

3. Feature-based and grid maps

Key point

A feature-based map lists only landmarks and where they are; a grid map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in §2.1 What a map is for. gives every cell of space a value, free or occupied. The first is tiny but says nothing about free space; the second is large but answers “can the robot be here?” at once.

What should the map of Figure 1’s room hold: only the two poles, or every spot of floor? Maps come in two kinds (Thrun et al. 2005 §6.2). Figure 2 draws our room both ways.

4. Obstacles as polygons made of half-planes

Key point

A line splits the floor into two half-planes, told apart by the sign of one formula. A convex obstacle is the overlap of a few half-planes, so a point is inside exactly when every formula is at most 0; any other obstacle is a union ()Union (A ∪ B): The set of elements in A or B (or both), such as the points inside either of two convex pieces of an obstacle; for events, the event that A or B (or both) happens. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Mutually Exclusive Events, §5. The addition rule: where mutual exclusivity pays off of convex pieces.

4.1 Why store obstacles exactly

The grid map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in §2.1 What a map is for. of Section 3 says whether the robot can stand somewhere in the room, but it blurs every edge to the nearest cell. Collision checks in route planning often need the exact shape: does the robot’s path clip the table’s corner or not? The table in Figure 1 is a four-sided polygon, given by its corners in counter-clockwise order (LaValle §3.1.1):

(3, 1), (5, 1), (5.5, 2.5), (3.5, 3)

The list draws the table’s outline, but it does not answer the question a planner asks a million times: is the point (4, 2) inside the table? Half-planes ()Half-plane: All points on one side of a line, together with the line: the points where ax+by+c≤0; one sign test, used as a building block for polygon obstacles. Explained in §4.2 Half-plane: one side of a line. answer it with a few multiplications.

4.2 Half-plane: one side of a line

Every edge of the table lies on a line, which we can write in the general form of a lineExplained in: The Equation of a Hyperplane, §3.1 The general form of a line. For a point on the floor:

  • x is the point’s x coordinate, in m;
  • y is its y coordinate, in m;
  • a is the number that multiplies x, set by the line’s direction (1.5 for the table’s right edge below);
  • b is the number that multiplies y (−0.5 for the right edge);
  • c is the constant that shifts the line into place (−7 for the right edge);
  • f(x,y) is the line’s function: 0 on the line, negative on one side and positive on the other.
f(x,y)=ax+by+c=0

The vector (a,b) is the line’s normal vector ()Normal vector: A vector perpendicular to a line, plane or hyperplane; for wTx+w0​=0 it is w. Explained in: The Equation of a Hyperplane, §6.1 The argument and points towards the positive side. All the points on one side of a line, together with the line, form a half-plane ()Half-plane: All points on one side of a line, together with the line: the points where ax+by+c≤0; one sign test, used as a building block for polygon obstacles. Explained in §4.2 Half-plane: one side of a line. (LaValle §3.1.1):

  • H is the half-plane on the negative side: the set of floor points (x,y) where f is 0 or less; in the braces below, the colon reads “such that”.
H={(x,y):f(x,y)≤0}

Worked example: the right edge of the table. It runs from (5, 1) to (5.5, 2.5), so its direction is (0.5, 1.5). The corners run counter-clockwise, so the table always lies on the left of each edge as we walk along it. Turning the direction a quarter turn clockwise therefore gives a normal that points to the right of the edge, out of the table:

(a,b)=(1.5, −0.5)

The line passes through (5, 1), where f must be 0:

1.5×5−0.5×1+c=0 c=−7

Doubling every number gives whole numbers and the same line, with the same signs. We call this right-edge function f2​:

f2​(x,y)=3x−y−14

Our table’s four edge functions of a floor point (x,y), in m, each scaled to whole numbers and signed so that the inside is negative:

  • f1​ is the bottom edge’s function, for the edge from (3, 1) to (5, 1);

Inside test. A point is inside the table when all four values are at most 0. For the point (4, 2):

f1​=1−2=−1 f2​=12−2−14=−4 f3​=8+16−31=−7 f4​=11−16+2=−3

All four are negative: inside. For the point (5.5, 1.5):

f2​=16.5−1.5−14=1

One positive value is enough: outside, on the far side of the right edge. In logic, the table’s test joins the four conditions with AND (LaValle §3.1.1): f1​≤0 AND f2​≤0, and so on for all four edges.

4.4 Non-convex obstacles are unions of convex pieces

The cabinet is L-shaped, so it is not convex: the segment between a point in one arm and a point in the other leaves the cabinet (Figure 4). No set of half-planes ()Half-plane: All points on one side of a line, together with the line: the points where ax+by+c≤0; one sign test, used as a building block for polygon obstacles. Instead we cut it into convex pieces, two rectangles, and take their union ()Union (A ∪ B): The set of elements in A or B (or both), such as the points inside either of two convex pieces of an obstacle; for events, the event that A or B (or both) happens.   • R1​ is the cabinet’s top arm, a rectangle along the top wall;
  • R2​ is its right arm, a rectangle along the right wall;
  • ∪ joins them into their union: the points in either piece.
R1​: 5.6≤x≤7,  3.4≤y≤4 R2​: 6.4≤x≤7,  2.4≤y≤4 cabinet=R1​∪R2​

A point is in the cabinet if it is in R1​ OR in R2​. The point (6.2, 3.0), in the notch of the L, fails both:

  • in R1​? No: y=3.0 is below 3.4;
  • in R2​? No: x=6.2 is left of 6.4.

So it is free. 5. Triangle meshes and bitmaps

Key point

In 3D, obstacles are often stored as many triangles, which graphics hardware draws fast but which do not say what is inside. A bitmap ()Bitmap (obstacle bitmap): A grid of cells marked 1 if the cell contains any obstacle point and 0 otherwise, like a black-and-white image; simple and natural for sensor data, but it overstates obstacles, and finer cells cost many more cells. Explained in §5.2 Bitmaps and occupancy grids. marks every cell that touches an obstacle; it is simple and fits sensor data, but finer cells cost many more cells.

5.1 Triangle meshes

Half-planes ()Half-plane: All points on one side of a line, together with the line: the points where ax+by+c≤0; one sign test, used as a building block for polygon obstacles. Explained in §4.2 Half-plane: one side of a line. describe the table’s outline on the floor plan, but the room’s table and cabinet are really 3D, and the common 3D model is a set of triangles, each given by its three corner points in space. A set of triangles that together cover an object’s surface is a triangle mesh ()Triangle mesh: A set of triangles, each given by three corner points in space, that together cover an object’s surface (a table as 12 triangles on 8 corners); fast to draw in 3D, but it does not say what is inside. Explained in §5.1 Triangle meshes. (LaValle §3.1.3). Figure 5 builds our table, 0.75 m high, from 8 corner points and 12 triangles: 2 for the top, 2 for the bottom and 2 for each of the 4 sides.

Figure 5: The table as a 3D triangle mesh: 8 corner points, at floor height and at 0.75 m, joined into 12 triangles covering the top, the bottom and the four sides

Triangles are popular because graphics hardware is built to draw them (LaValle §3.1.3). Neighbouring triangles share corners, so meshes often store each corner once and list triangles by corner numbers, as the table’s 12 triangles share 8 corners. The drawback: a mesh is only a surface. Nothing in a loose set of triangles says which side is inside, and meshes made from scans or design files often have holes, so a planner cannot always tell whether a point is inside an object (LaValle §3.1.3).

Key point

A scan of 360 distances is mostly wall. 6.1 Why extract features

The other half of the comparison is the reading itself. A laser scanner on our robot at (2, 1) measures 360 distances, one per degree. Comparing all 360 with a map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Pulling such objects out of raw sensor data, so that a scan of hundreds of numbers becomes a short list of objects, is feature extraction ()Feature extraction (from sensor data): Pulling a few distinctive objects out of raw sensor data, such as two poles out of a 360-beam laser scan; it shrinks hundreds of numbers to a short list that is easy to match with a map. Explained in §6.1 Why extract features. (Thrun et al. 2005 §6.6, as in the Freiburg sensor-model slides).

An object makes a good landmark ()Landmark: An object the robot can detect reliably and tell apart from the others, such as a coloured pole or a door; its known position in the map lets readings of it fix the robot’s pose. Explained in §6.1 Why extract features. when two things hold:

  1. the robot can detect it reliably, such as a pole that stands out in a laser scan or a coloured marker in a camera image;
  2. the robot can tell it apart from the other landmarks: tree 1 from tree 2, the pink pole from the green one.

Landmarks can be natural objects (trees, doors, road signs) or artificial ones put there for the robot (coloured markers, reflectors). Some are passive, only seen; others are active and send a signal, such as a radio beacon (Freiburg sensor-model slides). The two poles of our room are passive landmarks; the next step finds them in the scan.

6.2 Finding the poles in a scan

Figure 7 shows a simulated scan from our robot at (2, 1) facing 30°: 360 beams, one per degree, each with 1 cm of range noise. The grey dots are where the beams hit: walls, the near side of the table, and two small clusters.

Figure 7: 360 scan points from the robot at (2, 1) facing 30 degrees: grey dots along the walls and the table’s near side; two small clusters are extracted as poles, the pink one at range 2.23 m and bearing 87 degrees from 5 beams, the green one at range 4.00 m and bearing −33 degrees from 3 beams

A pole shows up as a few neighbouring beams that are much shorter than the beams on both sides, which pass it and hit the wall behind. So the extraction rule, applied to the scan in order, is:

  1. cut the scan wherever the range jumps by more than 0.3 m between neighbouring beams;
  2. keep a piece that is narrower than 0.35 m and nearer than the beams on both sides of it;
  3. report its direction as the average direction of its beams, and its distance as its nearest range plus the pole’s radius of 0.1 m, so that the distance is to the pole’s centre.
