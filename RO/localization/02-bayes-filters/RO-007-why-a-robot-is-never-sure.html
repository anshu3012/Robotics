1. Overview

Key point

A robot’s sensors and wheels are never exact, so one reading can fit many places and one move can end in several. The robot therefore keeps a probability for every place it could be, instead of a single guess.

Figure 1: A hallway of 10 cells of 1 m, numbered 0 to 9 and joined into a loop, with doors at cells 1, 3 and 7. The robot’s sensor says “door”. The robot could be at any of the three doors, so each of those cells gets probability 0.33

So far the kinematic model ()Kinematic model: Equations that give how a robot’s configuration changes from its speed inputs alone, ignoring masses and forces, such as x˙=vcosθ, y˙​=vsinθ, θ˙=ω; used to predict motion at low speed. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model predicts where wheel commands take a robot: wheels at 12 and 8 rad/s drive it round a circle of radius 0.5 m. That prediction assumes wheels that never slip. Real wheels slip, so a command “move 2” can end 1, 2 or 3 cells on, and a robot that only counts such moves soon does not know where it is. This Note adds the answer: keep a probability for every place. We leave the floor for a hallway of cells, because one number per place keeps the counting small (Section 4.1 returns to the floor).

A delivery robot drives along the hallway of Figure 1, the hallway the belief and grid-filter Notes of this chapter return to. It has a map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §2.1 What a map is for: 10 cells of 1 m each, numbered 0 to 9, with doors at cells 1, 3 and 7. The hallway is a loop, so one step to the right of cell 9 is cell 0. The robot has a sensor that says “door” or “wall” for the cell beside it. Its sensor now says “door”. Where is it?

There is no single answer. Three cells fit that reading, and the robot has no reason to prefer one of them. A robot that picks one cell and forgets the rest is wrong two times out of three. A robot that keeps all three, each with probability 1/3, still has the true cell among them.

2. Why a robot is never sure

Key point

A move of 2 cells can end 1, 2 or 3 cells on, and a reading can be wrong, because every part of a robot adds uncertainty: the world changes, sensors are limited and noisy, wheels slip, models are simplified and computers must answer in time. Errors in counted moves add up, so a robot that only counts its moves gets more and more lost: after 8 moves it is where it thinks only with probability 0.33.

2.1 Why counting moves is not enough: dead reckoning

The simplest way to know where we are is to start at a known place and add up our own moves: “I started at 0 m and moved 2 m eight times, so I am at 16 m.” The method is dead reckoning ()Dead reckoning: Working out a robot’s position from a known start by adding up its own moves, without looking at its surroundings; simple, but its error grows with every move, so it needs readings to correct it. Explained in §2.1 Why counting moves is not enough: dead reckoning.: working out the position from a known start plus the robot’s own motion, with no look at the surroundings. Facing north at 1 m/s for 3 s, a robot reckons it is 3 m north of where it started, without checking anything.

The trouble is that the moves themselves are not exact. Our hallway robot is told “move 2”, two cells of 1 m. Because its wheels sometimes slip or spin on, the real move is (Labbe ch.2):

Real moveProbability
1 cell (it falls short)0.1
2 cells0.8
3 cells (it runs on)0.1

We count positions in whole cells, so a move that falls well short counts as 1 cell and one that runs on counts as 3. The hallway robot moves this way throughout the chapter.

Dead reckoning is good over short stretches; over longer ones its error grows until something outside the robot corrects it. 2.2 Five sources of uncertainty

Slipping wheels are only one cause. The textbook on probabilistic robotics ()Probabilistic robotics: The approach in which a robot keeps a probability distribution over what might be the case (such as where it is) instead of one best guess, so it can handle ambiguous readings and recover from errors. Explained in §3.2 Why a single best guess gets lost., robotics that reasons with probabilities, lists five (Thrun et al. 2005 ch.1, as given in Khamis L5), shown in Figure 3:

Figure 3: Five sources of uncertainty around a robot: the environment, the sensors, the robot’s actuation, the models and the computation, each with an example

  1. The environment. The world is not fully predictable: people walk past, doors open and close, chairs move. A person who steps between the robot and the wall makes a distance sensor read much less than the true distance to the wall.
  2. The sensors. A sensor sees only so far and so finely; a camera cannot see through walls. Eight distance sensors around a robot tell it how far the nearest obstacle is in eight directions, which is usually not enough to say where it is in a room. And every reading carries sensor noise ()Sensor noise: Small random errors in a sensor’s readings, so the same quantity measured twice gives slightly different numbers (a wall 2 m away reads 1.92 to 2.07 m); it is one reason a robot cannot take a reading at face value. Explained in §2.2 Five sources of uncertainty.: small random errors, so the same wall measured again from the same spot gives slightly different numbers.
  3. The robot’s actuation. Motors and wheels do not do exactly what they are told. A command to move 1 m gives 95 cm one time and 1.1 m the next; gears slip and wear.
  4. The models. Every model simplifies. The kinematic model ()Kinematic model: Equations that give how a robot’s configuration changes from its speed inputs alone, ignoring masses and forces, such as x˙=vcosθ, y˙​=vsinθ, θ˙=ω; used to predict motion at low speed. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model, the equations that turn wheel speeds into motion, assumes wheels that never slip.
  5. The computation. A robot must decide in time, so its algorithms take shortcuts and approximate (Khamis L5).

In Figure 4, watch sensor noise and the environment at work on one wall: no two readings of the wall agree exactly, and while a person walks through the beam the readings fall far short of the wall. A distance sensor stands 2.00 m from a wall and takes 200 readings. The 190 readings of the wall itself spread from 1.92 m to 2.07 m, because of sensor noise. The 10 readings taken while a person walked past are between 0.61 m and 1.16 m, nowhere near the wall.

So no reading and no move can be taken at face value. Section 3 starts with the reading: “door” fits three cells, and the robot must still say where it is.

3. Keep a distribution, not one best guess

Key point

This section answers the opening question for one reading: “door” fits cells 1, 3 and 7. A robot that keeps one guess throws the other two away and gets lost when it guessed wrong; a robot that keeps a probability for every cell keeps the true one among its candidates.

3.1 Why one reading fits many places

Back to the hallway. For this section we make two simplifications: the moves are exact and the door sensor never errs. At the start the robot knows nothing, so each of the 10 cells has the same probability. We write:

  • n is a cell number, 0 to 9;
  • P(n) is the probability that the robot is in cell n, a number from 0 to 1; the ten cells’ probabilities add up to 1.
P(each cell)=101​=0.1

The sensor says “door”. Three cells have a door (1, 3 and 7) and nothing favours one of them, so each gets a third:

P(1)=P(3)=P(7)=31​=0.33

The other seven get 0, as Figure 1 shows. The same thing happens in two dimensions. A robot in a room whose laser scan shows “a flat wall 1 m straight ahead” could stand 1 m from any straight stretch of any wall, facing it (Figure 5). The reading rules out spots near a corner or facing the door, where the scan would look different, but it leaves many poses ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading, positions together with a heading.

Figure 5: A room 7 m by 4 m with a door. The scan shows a flat wall 1 m ahead; every pose 1 m from a straight stretch of wall, facing it, fits the reading, so candidate poses line all four walls

Such a list of every possible value with its probability, adding up to 1, is a probability distribution ()Probability distribution: A list (or curve) of every possible outcome of a random variable with its probability, adding up to 1; it says how likely each outcome is, so the chance of any event can be worked out from it. Explained in: Random Variables and Probability Distributions, §3. Probability distributions as tables. Ours has three separate peaks, so it is a multimodal ()Multimodal: Having more than one mode, that is more than one peak (two peaks: bimodal), such as a robot’s belief peaking at three doors; it shows the data point to several separate values, so one mean or one best guess would hide them. Explained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §5. Mode distribution: the robot is not in three places at once, it has narrowed its position down to one of three (Labbe ch.2).

3.2 Why a single best guess gets lost

A robot could instead keep only one value: its best guess, a point estimate ()Point estimate: A single number computed from sample data as the best guess for an unknown population parameter. Explained in: Estimating a Population Mean with the Central Limit Theorem, §1. Overview of its position. Figure 6 runs both robots side by side. The true robot is in cell 1 (red square).

Figure 6: Animation: after the reading “door” the distribution keeps cells 1, 3 and 7 while a best guess picks cell 7; after “move 2” the candidates become cells 3, 5 and 9; after a second “door” only cell 3 fits, while the best guess at cell 9 expected a wall and is lost

Step by step:

  1. Reading “door”. The distribution keeps cells 1, 3 and 7. The best-guess robot must pick one and picks cell 7. With three equal candidates, a guess is right only one time in three.
  2. Control “move 2”. Every candidate moves with the robot: cells 1, 3 and 7 become 3, 5 and 9. The best guess becomes cell 9.
  3. Reading “door” again. Of cells 3, 5 and 9, only cell 3 has a door. Each other candidate would have read “wall”, which does not match. So the distribution is now certain: cell 3, with probability 1.

The best-guess robot expected a wall at cell 9 and read “door”. It now knows its guess was wrong, but it threw the other candidates away at step 1, so it has nothing to fall back on: it is lost. The robot that kept the whole distribution found its place, because it remembered every place the readings allowed (Labbe ch.2).

Keeping the whole distribution is the central idea of probabilistic robotics ()Probabilistic robotics: The approach in which a robot keeps a probability distribution over what might be the case (such as where it is) instead of one best guess, so it can handle ambiguous readings and recover from errors. Explained in §3.2 Why a single best guess gets lost.: the robot computes a probability distribution ()Probability distribution: A list (or curve) of every possible outcome of a random variable with its probability, adding up to 1; it says how likely each outcome is, so the chance of any event can be worked out from it. Explained in: Random Variables and Probability Distributions, §3. Probability distributions as tables over what might be the case, instead of a single best guess, so it can handle ambiguous readings and recover from errors (Thrun 2000). The probability distribution a robot keeps over where it might be is its belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition, defined later in this chapter.

3.3 Why noisy sensors forbid crossing places out

Section 3.2 crossed out every cell that did not match a reading. Crossing out works only for a perfect sensor. A real door sensor errs. The sensor of our hallway robot, used throughout the chapter, behaves like this:

Robot is atReads “door”Reads “wall”
a door cell0.60.4
a wall cell0.20.8

A robot that still crosses out non-matching cells throws away its true cell every time the reading is wrong. In Section 3.2 the true robot stood at a door both times (cells 1 and 3). The chance that both readings say “door” is:

0.6×0.6=0.36

So the chance that at least one says “wall”, and the true cell is crossed out, is:

1−0.36=0.64

Nearly two times in three, the robot would delete the cell where it really is. (The two readings err independently (), which is why their probabilities multiply.)Independent events: Events where one happening does not change the probability of the other; for them the probability that both happen is the product of their probabilities. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Independent Events, §2. The definition

So with a noisy sensor the robot does not cross out; it weights. A reading of “door” makes each door cell 3 times as likely as each wall cell, because the sensor reads “door” 3 times as often at a door as at a wall (Labbe ch.2):

0.20.6​=3

Three door cells and seven wall cells make 16 equal shares, and the probability P of a cell (Section 3.1) is its shares out of 16:

3×3+7×1=16 P(each door)=163​=0.1875 P(each wall)=161​=0.0625

Check: the ten cells add up to 1.

3×0.1875+7×0.0625=1

Figure 7 plays the same reading on the cells, for the perfect sensor and the noisy one. Every cell starts at 0.1; watch each one be multiplied by the chance of reading “door” there, and then every cell be divided by the sum of the products, so that the ten add up to 1 again. The noisy sensor ends at the same 0.1875 and 0.0625 as the shares.

Figure 7: Animation of one reading “door” for a perfect sensor (top, blue) and the noisy sensor (bottom, purple). Every cell starts at 0.1 and is multiplied by the chance of reading “door” there: 1 at a door and 0 at a wall for the perfect sensor (products 0.1 and 0), 0.6 and 0.2 for the noisy sensor (products 0.06 and 0.02). The products add up to 0.3 and 0.32, and each cell is divided by its sensor’s sum. The last frame: the perfect sensor gives 0.33 to each door cell and 0 to each wall cell; the noisy sensor gives 0.1875 to each door cell and 0.0625 to each wall cell

The wall cells keep a small probability, so one wrong reading lowers the true cell but never deletes it; later readings can lift it again. So far the answer to “where is it?” has been one cell number; Section 4 asks what a robot must track in general.

4. The state: what the robot keeps track of

Key point

“Where is it?” asks for the robot’s state: in the hallway one number, its cell. In general the state is every quantity the robot needs to predict what happens next: its pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading, often its speeds, and the things around it that can change. A state is complete when knowing it now makes the past useless for predicting the future.

4.1 State: from one number to many

In the hallway the robot needs one number, its cell. A robot on a floor needs three, its pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading: position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°).

The collection of quantities the robot keeps track of because they decide what happens next is its state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in §4.1 State: from one number to many.. In the hallway run of Section 3.2 the robot is in cell 3 at time 2:

  • t is the time step, counted 0, 1, 2 and so on, here 2;
  • xt​ is the whole state at time t, not only the position x of a pose; in the hallway it is the robot’s cell number, here 3.
x2​=3

Depending on the robot and the task, the state can also include:

  • the pose of the robot;
  • the speeds of the robot, when it does not stop the moment its motors stop (Section 4.2);
  • the positions of things around it: fixed things such as walls and doors are kept in a map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §2.1 What a map is for, while things that move, such as people, go into the state, often with their speeds.

4.3 Complete state and the Markov property

That test gives the definition. A state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in §4.1 State: from one number to many. is a complete state ()Complete state: A state that, once known, makes earlier states, controls and measurements useless for predicting the future (position plus speed for a coasting robot, not position alone); it lets the robot keep only its latest estimate. Explained in §4.3 Complete state and the Markov property. if knowing it now, the earlier states, commands and sensor readings, called controls and measurements ()Measurement zt​: What a robot’s sensors report about the world at time t, such as “door”, a laser scan or a camera image; it does not change the state but tells the robot something about it, which narrows its distribution. Explained in §5.1 Measurements and controls., tell us nothing more about the future (Thrun et al. 2005 §2.3.1). For robot B:

  • position only: not complete, because the position one second ago improves the prediction;
  • position and speed: complete, because nothing from the past improves it.

A sequence of states with this property, where the future depends on the past only through the present, has the Markov property ()Markov property: The property of a sequence of states that the future depends on the past only through the present state; it lets a filter forget the history and work from the latest state alone. Explained in §4.3 Complete state and the Markov property.. We want it because it lets the robot forget its history: to predict and to update its belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition, its probability for every place, it needs only the latest state, not every reading since it was switched on. The Bayes filter ()Bayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.4 The algorithm, which updates the belief after every move and every reading, is built on it.

In practice no robot’s state is truly complete: people move, gears wear, the floor changes. Robots still treat their state as complete, and the methods of this chapter work well when the leftover effects are small (Thrun 2000). The robot never sees its state directly; Section 5 names the two kinds of data it does receive.

5. Controls and measurements: two streams of data

Key point

The robot answers “where is it?” from two kinds of data. Controls, such as “move 2”, change its state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in §4.1 State: from one number to many., here its cell; measurements, such as “door”, only report on it. A control comes first, then the measurement in the new state.

5.1 Measurements and controls

The data that reaches a robot comes in two kinds (Thrun 2000; Khamis L5):

  • A measurement ()Measurement zt​: What a robot’s sensors report about the world at time t, such as “door”, a laser scan or a camera image; it does not change the state but tells the robot something about it, which narrows its distribution. Explained in §5.1 Measurements and controls. zt​ is what the sensors report about the world at time t: our door sensor’s “door”, a laser scan of distances, a camera image. In the hallway at time 1:

    z1​=door

  • A control ()Control ut​: Information about how a robot’s state changed between two time steps, such as the command “move 2” or a count of how far the wheels turned; it changes the state, so a filter uses it to predict where the robot went, which spreads its probability distribution. Explained in §5.1 Measurements and controls. is information about how the state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in §4.1 State: from one number to many. changed: the command “move 2”, or a count of how far the wheels turned. We write it ut​, the same symbol as the control input ()Control input u: The numbers we choose to drive a robot, such as u=(v,ω); the model turns them into motion. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model of a robot model. In the hallway at time 2:

    u2​=move 2

We keep them apart because they do opposite things to the robot’s knowledge (Section 6). A control causes the state to change; a measurement does not change the state, it only tells the robot something about it.

5.2 The order in time

The robot starts in some state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. 6. Moving makes the robot less sure, sensing makes it more sure

Key point

Here the whole opening problem comes back at once: moves that slip and a sensor that errs. Every uncertain move widens the robot’s distribution (its standard deviation (), the typical distance of a cell from the average, grows from 0 to 0.63 m in two moves)Standard deviation of a random variable: The square root of a random variable’s variance, in the units of X; it says how far values typically fall from the expected value. Explained in: Random Variables as Functions, Expected Value and Variance, §4.1 Variance term by term; every informative reading narrows it (to 0.42 m). Neither alone is enough: moves alone drift, readings alone are ambiguous, and together they pin the robot down.

Now we drop both simplifications: the moves are the noisy moves of Section 2.1, and the sensor is the noisy sensor of Section 3.3. Take a robot that knows it is in cell 3, as after Section 3.2. It drives two moves of 2 cells without a reading in between, and then reads its sensor. Figure 10 plays the four stages.

2 then 2:0.8×0.8=0.64 1 then 3:0.1×0.1=0.01 3 then 1:0.1×0.1=0.01 P(7)=0.64+0.01+0.01=0.66

Moves add uncertainty because the robot cannot tell which of the possible outcomes happened; readings remove it because they make places that do not fit less likely, or rule them out (Thrun 2000). So a robot needs both streams of data:

  • with moves alone it drifts, as in Section 2.1;
  • with readings alone it stays ambiguous, as the three doors of Section 3.1 show;
  • with both, each reading is checked against where the moves say the robot can be, and the places that fit everything gain probability, as in Section 3.2 and here.
