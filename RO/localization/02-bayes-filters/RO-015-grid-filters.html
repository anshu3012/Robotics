Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter

Grid Filters: Histogram Filter and Binary Bayes Filter

44 min read

Open as PDF

1. Overview

Key point

Chop the robot’s space into cells and keep one probability per cell: the Bayes filter then becomes plain arithmetic on a list of numbers. The same list of numbers also answers two questions about the past: where the robot most likely was, and which whole path it most likely took.

Figure 1: A hallway of 10 cells, numbered 0 to 9 and joined into a loop, with doors in front of cells 1, 3 and 7; a robot stands in cell 1 and does not know it

The robot can now update its belief from the newest command and reading alone, with the Bayes filterBayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §4.4 The algorithm. What still breaks is its prediction step, a sum over every possible previous state. In the door worldExplained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §2. The door world that sum had 2 terms; a position along the hallway can be any number of metres, so the sum has infinitely many terms and cannot be done as written. This Note adds one number per cell, and answers: how does a computer run the Bayes filter, and what else can the same numbers tell us?

We go back to the hallway of beliefBelief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §1. Overview (Figure 1), the same world with the same numbers: 10 cells of 1 m in a loop, doors at cells 1, 3 and 7, a door sensor that reads “door” with probability 0.6 at a door and 0.2 at a wall, and a command “move 2” that moves the robot 1, 2 or 3 cells with probability 0.1, 0.8 and 0.1.

The Note covers:

  • the histogram filter: one probability per cell, updated by multiplying (sensing) and sliding (moving) (Section 2);
  • how to cut a real, continuous space into cells: fixed cells or cells that adapt to the belief (Section 3);
  • the binary Bayes filter for a yes-or-no state that does not change, run in log odds (Section 4);
  • looking back: the smoothed belief at an earlier time, and the most likely whole path (Section 5).

2. The histogram filter

Key point

Keep one probability per cell. Sensing multiplies every cell by how well it explains the reading, then rescales. Moving slides the list along and blurs it with the motion probabilities.

2.1 Why cells

A continuous position, such as “3.42 m along the hallway”, can take infinitely many values, and a computer can only store a finite list. So we cut the hallway into cells, here 10 cells of 1 m, and keep one probability for each: the probability that the robot is somewhere in that cell. This is the same move as discretization ()Discretization: Turning a continuous column into a discrete one by cutting its range into intervals. Explained in: Binning and Binarization: Equal Width, Equal Frequency and k-means Binning, §1. Overview of a feature into bins, and the list of probabilities, drawn as bars, is a histogram ()Histogram: A bar chart of how many values fall in each equal range (bin) of a numerical column. Explained in: Univariate Analysis: Exploring One Column at a Time, §6. Histogram of the belief.

The Bayes filter run on such a list of cells is the histogram filter ()Histogram filter (discrete Bayes filter): The Bayes filter run on a space cut into cells, keeping one probability per cell; sensing multiplies each cell by p(z∣x) and rescales, moving slides and blurs the list. It makes the Bayes filter computable for a continuous position. Explained in §2.1 Why cells. (PR §4.1). When the states are finite to begin with, as in the door world, the same algorithm is called the discrete Bayes filter (Labbe ch.2). The sum of the prediction step now has only 10 terms, so it can be done exactly.

2.2 Sense: multiply, then rescale

The robot has no idea where it is, so each cell starts at 0.1. It reads “door”. Figure 2 shows the four stages.

Figure 2: Four panels: the prior, 0.1 in every cell; the measurement probability, 0.6 at doors and 0.2 at walls; their products, 0.06 at doors and 0.02 at walls, adding to 0.32; the products divided by 0.32, giving 0.1875 at doors and 0.0625 at walls

Multiply each cell by the probability of the reading in that cell:

door cell: 0.1×0.6=0.06 wall cell: 0.1×0.2=0.02

Add the 10 products, 3 door cells and 7 wall cells:

3×0.06+7×0.02=0.32

Rescale by dividing every product by 0.32, so the cells add to 1 again:

door cell: 0.06/0.32=0.1875 wall cell: 0.02/0.32=0.0625

This is the correction step of the Bayes filter, done cell by cell (Labbe ch.2). The robot reads “door” three times as often at a door as at a wall, and after the update each door cell holds three times the belief of a wall cell.

2.3 Move: shift, then blur

Next the robot moves 2 cells. If the move were exact, every probability would simply travel 2 cells to the right, wrapping round the loop (Figure 3, middle). But the move is 1 cell with probability 0.1 and 3 cells with probability 0.1, so some belief also lands one cell short or one cell too far (Figure 3, right).

Figure 3: Left: the belief after reading door, 0.1875 at cells 1, 3 and 7. Middle: shifted exactly 2 cells, 0.1875 at cells 3, 5 and 9. Right: shifted and blurred by the 0.1/0.8/0.1 move, 0.1625 at cells 3, 5 and 9 and 0.0875 at cell 4

Which old cells can end in cell 3? Cell 2 (by moving 1), cell 1 (by moving 2) and cell 0 (by moving 3). Each contributes its old belief times the probability of that move:

new cell 3=0.1×0.0625 + 0.8×0.1875 + 0.1×0.0625 =0.1625

This is the prediction step of the Bayes filter: the law of total probability ()Law of total probability: The rule for the overall probability of an event: add its probability under each case, weighted by how likely that case is, P(B)=∑i​P(B∣Ai​)P(Ai​), when the cases Ai​ are mutually exclusive and cover every possibility. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: A Bayes’ Theorem Problem, §1. Overview with three terms instead of ten, because only three old cells can reach each new one.

Every new cell is built the same way, with the same three weights, slid along the hallway. Figure 4 builds the whole predicted belief this way, one cell at a time.

Figure 4: Top: the old belief with the three cells that can reach the new cell highlighted and weighted 0.1, 0.8 and 0.1. Bottom: the new belief filled in cell by cell; new cell 3 = 0.1 x 0.0625 + 0.8 x 0.1875 + 0.1 x 0.0625 = 0.1625

The list of weights, here (0.1, 0.8, 0.1), is the motion kernel ()Motion kernel: The short list of move probabilities, such as (0.1, 0.8, 0.1) for one cell short, exact, one cell too far, slid along the belief in the prediction step; it spreads each cell’s belief over the cells the robot may have reached. Explained in §2.3 Move: shift, then blur.. Sliding a small list of weights along a longer list and adding the products at each position is a convolution ()Convolution operation: Sliding a small filter over an input and, at each position, multiplying cell by cell and adding up, so the output (a feature map) is large where the input contains the filter’s pattern, such as an edge. Explained in: The Convolution Operation, §6. The convolution operation, the same operation a CNN filter performs on an image (Labbe ch.2). It is valid here because the move’s error does not depend on where the robot is: the same kernel applies in every cell.

2.4 Moving without looking loses everything

What if the robot keeps moving and never reads its sensor? Figure 5 starts it sure that it is in cell 1 and repeats “move 2”.

Figure 5: After 1 move the highest cell holds 0.80, after 3 moves 0.56, after 10 moves 0.29, after 80 moves 0.11: the belief flattens towards 0.1 in every cell

Each move spreads the belief of every cell over three cells, so the highest cell’s belief shrinks:

Moves without a reading0131080

The belief tends to 0.1 in every cell, where the robot started before it knew anything. A prediction can only lose information; only readings bring it back (Labbe ch.2).

2.5 Sense and move together

A real robot alternates: move, read, move, read. Figure 6 runs seven steps. The true robot starts in cell 1 and really moves 2 cells each time (cells 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 1, 3). Every reading is correct except at t=5, where the sensor says “door” in front of the wall of cell 9.

Figure 6: Seven steps of the histogram filter: orange frames show the predicted belief after each move, blue frames the belief after each reading; the tallest bar follows the true cell except at t = 5, after the wrong reading, when it jumps to cell 3; at t = 6 and t = 7 it is back on the true cell

The tallest bar at each step:

Two correct readings later the filter is back on the true cell with 0.47. A filter that kept only one best guess would have been lost at t=5 (Labbe ch.2).

2.6 The formal version

With the cells numbered k=0,…,9, let pk,t​ be the belief in cell k at time t and pˉ​k,t​ the predicted belief. The histogram filter is (PR §4.1.1):

pˉ​k,t​=i∑​p(xk​∣ut​, xi​) pi,t−1​ pk,t​=η p(zt​∣xk​) pˉ​k,t​

The first line is the prediction step with the sum over the old cells i; the second is the correction step. Both lines visit every cell, so one step costs a fixed amount of work per cell.

3. Cutting a real space into cells

Key point

Fine cells give an accurate belief but their number explodes with the size of the space and the number of state variables. A static decomposition fixes the cells in advance; an adaptive one makes cells small only where the belief needs them.

3.1 Why the number of cells explodes

Cells turned the sum over infinitely many positions into 10 terms, at a price: with cells of 1 m, the robot’s position is never known better than “somewhere in this metre”. Smaller cells give a sharper answer, but every cell costs memory and time, because both steps visit every cell. A real floor robot has a pose: position x, y and heading θ (see posePose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading), so the grid has three directions. For a 30 m by 30 m floor with 15 cm cells and the heading in steps of 2° (Fox 1999):

0.1530​=200 cells along x 0.1530​=200 cells along y 2360​=180 headings 200×200×180=7,200,000 cells

Halving the cell size multiplies the count by 4 for the position alone, and each extra state variable, such as speed, multiplies it again by the number of its values (Labbe ch.2). Fox, Burgard and Thrun found that updating 7.2 million cells at every reading was too slow for the computers of 1999 (Fox 1999).

3.2 Static and adaptive decomposition

Most of those millions of cells hold a belief close to zero: the robot is not inside a wall or at the far end of the building. Two ways of cutting the space follow from that (PR §4.1.4):

  • A static decomposition ()Static decomposition: Cutting the state space into cells fixed before the robot starts, usually a regular grid such as 15 cm cells and 2° headings; simple, but it spends as much work on empty regions as where the robot is. Explained in §3.2 Static and adaptive decomposition. fixes the cells before the robot starts, whatever the belief turns out to be. A regular grid of equal cells is the usual choice (Fox 1999). It is simple, and every cell is updated the same way, but it spends as much work on empty regions as on the region where the robot is.
  • An adaptive decomposition ()Adaptive (dynamic) decomposition: Cutting the state space into cells that change with the belief: small cells where the belief is high, large cells where it is near zero, as in a quadtree; it gives fine detail with far fewer cells. Explained in §3.2 Static and adaptive decomposition., also called a dynamic decomposition, changes the cells as the belief changes: small cells where the belief is high, large cells where it is near zero. A common way is a quadtree, which splits a square into four only where more detail is needed (ShanghaiTech slides; Fox 1999, §6).

Figure 7: Left: an 8 m by 8 m floor cut into 64 fixed 1 m cells, with the belief concentrated in one spot. Right: the same floor cut adaptively into 19 cells: 4 m cells where the belief is near zero, down to 0.5 m cells at the peak

Figure 7 compares the two on an 8 m by 8 m floor. The fixed grid uses 64 cells of 1 m. The adaptive one uses 19 cells, yet its smallest cells (0.5 m) are finer than the fixed grid’s where the belief peaks. The price is bookkeeping: cells must be split and merged as the belief moves. The smallest grid of all has just 2 cells.

4. The binary Bayes filter

Key point

For a yes-or-no state that never changes, each reading multiplies the odds by a fixed factor. In log odds that becomes adding a fixed number, which is fast and does not round to 0 or 1.

4.1 A state that does not change

The smallest grid has 2 cells, open and closed. Go back to the door worldExplained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §2. The door world, but now nobody touches the door: it is open or closed, and it stays that way while the robot reads its sensor again and again. A state that the robot’s commands do not change is a static state ()Static state: A state that the robot’s commands do not change while it is being estimated, such as a door nobody touches; the prediction step then does nothing and only readings update the belief. Explained in §4.1 A state that does not change. (PR §4.2). With a static state the prediction step does nothing (the belief before a step equals the belief after it), so only the correction step is left.

The same situation appears in mapping: whether one small square of the floor is occupied by an obstacle is a yes-or-no question about something that does not move. A map built this way keeps one such filter per square (PR §4.2; Freiburg mapping slides).

4.2 Each reading multiplies the odds

The odds ()Odds: How often an event happens divided by how often it does not, e.g. 5 placed to 3 not placed is 5/3. Explained in: Gradient Boosting for Classification, §4. Stage 1: the log-odds of class 1 of the door being open compare the two possibilities:

odds=p(closed)p(open)​

Why does a reading multiply the odds? Write Bayes’ theorem once for “open” and once for “closed”, after a reading z. Both share the same denominator p(z):

p(open∣z)=p(z)p(z∣open) p(open)​ p(closed∣z)=p(z)p(z∣closed) p(closed)​

Divide the first line by the second. The p(z) cancels:

p(closed∣z)p(open∣z)​ =p(z∣closed)p(z∣open)​×p(closed)p(open)​

The left side is the new odds; the last fraction is the old odds. The ratio of the two measurement probabilities in between is the Bayes factor ()Bayes factor: A number that says how strongly the evidence favours yes over no: how many times more likely the evidence is if the answer is yes than if it is no, P(evidence∣yes)/P(evidence∣no). Multiplying the prior odds by it gives the posterior odds. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: A Bayes’ Theorem Problem, §6.1 The size of the update: the Bayes factor:

new odds=old odds×p(z∣closed)p(z∣open)​

We never need p(z), the normaliser η of the correction step, which is why odds are convenient.

For a reading of “sense open” the factor is:

0.6/0.2=3

For “sense closed” it is:

0.4/0.8=0.5

Starting from odds of 1 (that is, 0.5 against 0.5), two readings of “open” give:

1×3×3=9

Odds of 9 mean 9 parts open to 1 part closed:

p(open)=9+19​=0.9

the same 0.9 as in the Bayes filterBayes’ rule with background knowledge: Bayes’ theorem with an extra condition C in every term, p(x∣z,C)=p(z∣x,C) p(x∣C)/p(z∣C), such as earlier readings; it lets the posterior after one reading serve as the prior for the next. Explained in: The Bayes Filter: Predict, Then Update, §3.2 A second reading: Bayes’ theorem with background knowledge.

4.3 Log odds turn the products into sums

Taking the logarithm turns a product into a sum. The natural log of the odds is the log odds ()Log-odds: The natural log of the odds, ln(p/(1−p)): it turns a probability between 0 and 1 into any number (0 at p=0.5), so a model such as logistic regression can predict it with a straight line, and the sigmoid turns it back into a probability. Explained in: Gradient Boosting for Classification, §4. Stage 1: the log-odds of class 1:

l=ln1−pp​

Figure 8 shows how probabilities map to log odds: 0.5 goes to 0, probabilities above 0.5 to positive numbers, below 0.5 to negative ones, and 0 and 1 are never reached.

Figure 8: The curve from log odds to probability: p = 0.5 at l = 0, p = 0.75 at l = 1.10, p = 0.9 at l = 2.20, p = 0.33 at l = -0.69; the curve flattens towards 0 and 1 but never reaches them

Each reading now adds a fixed amount:

sense open: ln3=+1.10 sense closed: ln0.5=−0.69

Figure 9 runs five readings: open, open, closed, open, open.

Figure 9: Left: the log odds as a stack of blocks, +1.10 for each sense open and -0.69 for the sense closed, reaching 1.10, 2.20, 1.50, 2.60 and 3.70. Right: the matching probability that the door is open, 0.75, 0.90, 0.818, 0.931 and 0.976

Step by step:

0+1.10=1.10 1.10+1.10=2.20 2.20−0.69=1.50 1.50+1.10=2.60 2.60+1.10=3.70

To read a probability back out, undo the log, which is the sigmoid function ()Sigmoid function: An S-shaped function that squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, σ(z)=1/(1+e−z); it turns a score into a probability, as in logistic regression. Explained in: Logistic Regression: The Sigmoid Function, §4. The sigmoid function:

p=1−1+el1​ l=3.70:p=0.976

The notebook checks that Bayes’ theorem applied five times gives the same 0.976.

This filter, a static binary state updated in log odds, is the binary Bayes filter ()Binary Bayes filter: The Bayes filter for a yes-or-no state that does not change, such as “door open” or “this map cell is occupied”, run in log odds: each reading adds the log of its Bayes factor. It is fast and does not round to 0 or 1. Explained in §4.3 Log odds turn the products into sums. (PR §4.2).

4.4 Why work in log odds

Two reasons:

  1. Adding is cheaper than multiplying and rescaling. A map can hold millions of cells, each updated at every scan; one addition per cell is the cheapest update possible, and every cell can be updated at the same time (Freiburg mapping slides: the log odds update “only requires to compute sums”).
  2. Probabilities near 0 or 1 round off. Run 40 readings of “open” in ordinary probabilities. The odds become 3 to the power 40, about 12 billion billion, and the computer stores p(open) as exactly 1.0. After that, a reading of “closed” changes nothing, because 1.0 times anything, divided by itself, is still 1.0. In log odds the same run reaches 43.94, and a “closed” reading moves it to 43.25, as it should. The notebook shows both.

Every filter so far answers where the robot is now; the same numbers can also say where it was.

5. Looking back: smoothing and the most likely path

Key point

Later readings also say where the robot was earlier. The forward-backward algorithm combines readings from before and after a time step into the smoothed belief; the Viterbi algorithm finds the single most likely path.

5.1 Three questions about a run

Now the second half of the opening question: what else the same cell probabilities tell us. Take the hallway robot’s run from beliefBelief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §2.1 Why the true cell is out of reach: at t=1 it stays and reads “door”; at t=2 it moves 2 and reads “door”; at t=3 it moves 2 and reads “wall”. Its true cells are 1, 3 and 5. The hidden Markov modelHidden Markov model (HMM): A Markov chain whose states cannot be seen, where each state produces an observation that can, such as hidden robot cells producing “door” or “wall” readings; it is the model behind robot state estimation, speech recognition and many trackers. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §6.3 Hidden Markov model of the robot can answer three different questions (CS188 §8.2–8.3; J&M App. A):

  1. Where is the robot now, given the readings so far? This is filtering ()Filtering (state estimation): Estimating the current state from the readings so far, p(xt​∣z1:t​); what a robot needs while it drives, and what the Bayes filter computes. Explained in §5.1 Three questions about a run.; the histogram filter of Section 2 does it.
  2. Where was the robot at an earlier time, given all readings, including later ones? This is smoothing ()Smoothing: Estimating the state at an earlier time using all readings, including later ones, p(xt​∣z1:T​); later readings often settle where the robot was, such as raising the true starting cell from 0.19 to 0.36. Explained in §5.1 Three questions about a run..
  3. Which whole path of cells is most likely, given all readings? This is the most likely path ()Most likely path: The one sequence of hidden states with the highest probability given all readings, such as cells 1, 3, 5; one consistent answer for a whole run, found by the Viterbi algorithm. Explained in §5.1 Three questions about a run., found by the Viterbi algorithm.

Filtering is what a robot needs while it drives. Smoothing and the most likely path are for looking back over a recorded run, for example to draw the path it took.

5.2 Smoothing with the forward-backward algorithm

At t=1 the filter knew only the first reading, so cells 1, 3 and 7 were equally likely (Figure 11, left). Later readings settle it. A robot at cell 3 at t=1 would be at cell 5 at t=2, a wall, and would have read “door” there only 20 percent of the time. A robot at cell 1 would be at cell 3, a door. So the reading at t=2 points back to cell 1.

The forward-backward algorithm ()Forward-backward algorithm: The method that computes smoothed beliefs in an HMM by multiplying a forward pass (evidence up to t) by a backward pass (evidence after t) and rescaling; it uses every reading of a recorded run for each time step. Explained in §5.2 Smoothing with the forward-backward algorithm. makes this exact. It combines two quantities for every cell x and time t (J&M App. A):

  • the forward probability ()Forward probability αt​: The probability of the readings up to time t together with being in state x at t; the histogram filter before rescaling, carrying the evidence from the past. Explained in §5.2 Smoothing with the forward-backward algorithm. αt​(x): the probability of the readings up to t and of being in cell x at t. It is the histogram filter before rescaling; at t=1 it is 0.06 at the doors and 0.02 at the walls (Section 2.2);
  • the backward probability ()Backward probability βt​: The probability of the readings after time t, given state x at t, computed from the last step backwards starting at 1; it carries the evidence from the future. Explained in §5.2 Smoothing with the forward-backward algorithm. βt​(x): the probability of the readings after t, given cell x at t. It is computed from the end backwards, starting from 1 at the last step.

Figure 10: The chain x1, x2, x3 with readings door, door, wall. A forward arrow alpha_1 brings the evidence of readings up to t = 1; a backward arrow beta_1 brings the evidence of readings after t = 1; their product, rescaled, is the smoothed belief

Figure 10 shows the idea: α carries the evidence from the past, β from the future. Their product, rescaled to add to 1, is the smoothed belief:

γt​(x)=η αt​(x) βt​(x)

Why does a plain product work? Write the two pieces out:

αt​(x)=p(z1:t​, xt​=x) βt​(x)=p(zt+1:T​∣xt​=x)

By the Markov propertyMarkov property: The property of a sequence of states that the future depends on the past only through the present state; it lets a filter forget the history and work from the latest state alone. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.3 Complete state and the Markov property, once the cell at t is known, the earlier readings tell nothing more about the later ones. So the later readings can be added to the condition without changing β, and the product is the probability of all readings together with cell x at t:

αt​(x) βt​(x)=p(z1:T​, xt​=x)

Dividing by its total over all cells, p(z1:T​), gives p(xt​=x∣z1:T​), the smoothed belief (J&M App. A).

β for cell 1 at t=1, worked backwards. At t=3 there are no later readings, so β3​=1 everywhere. One step back, from cell 3 at t=2 the robot reaches cell 4, 5 or 6 (all walls), and each reads “wall” with probability 0.8:

β2​(3)=0.1×0.8+0.8×0.8+0.1×0.8 =0.8

Another step back, from cell 1 at t=1 the robot reaches cell 2 (a wall), 3 (a door) or 4 (a wall), and must then read “door” and continue. β2​(2) is worked like β2​(3): from cell 2 the robot reaches cell 3 (a door, reads “wall” with probability 0.4), 4 or 5 (walls):

β2​(2)=0.1×0.4+0.8×0.8 + 0.1×0.8=0.76

β2​(4)=0.76 the same way (notebook). Then:

β1​(1)=0.1×0.2×0.76 + 0.8×0.6×0.8 + 0.1×0.2×0.76 =0.4144

The same calculation for cell 3 gives only 0.1072, because a robot there would read “door” at a wall. Then:

α1​(1) β1​(1)=0.06×0.4144=0.0249 α1​(3) β1​(3)=0.06×0.1072=0.0064

The products over all 10 cells add to 0.06896, the probability of the three readings; it is the same at every t, which the notebook checks. Rescaling gives the smoothed belief:

γ1​(1)=0.0249/0.06896=0.36 γ1​(3)=0.0064/0.06896=0.09

Figure 11: Left: the filtered belief at t = 1, 0.19 at each of cells 1, 3 and 7. Right: the smoothed belief at t = 1 using all three readings, 0.36 at cell 1 and 0.09 at cells 3 and 7

Figure 11 compares the two. Using the later readings nearly doubles the belief in the true starting cell, from 0.19 to 0.36.

5.3 The most likely path: the Viterbi algorithm

Smoothing gives a probability for each cell at each time. Sometimes we want one answer instead: the single path of cells that best explains all readings. Trying every path is hopeless for long runs: with 10 cells and T steps there are 10 to the power T paths, 1000 for our 3 steps but a 1 followed by 100 zeros for 100 steps (J&M App. A).

The Viterbi algorithm ()Viterbi algorithm: The method that finds the single most likely sequence of hidden states in an HMM by keeping, for each state and time, only the best path ending there and where it came from, then tracing back; it costs states squared times steps instead of trying every path. Explained in §5.3 The most likely path: the Viterbi algorithm. avoids this with one observation: the best path to a cell at time t must continue the best path to some cell at time t−1. So for each cell it keeps only the probability of the best path ending there, mt​(x), and which cell that path came from, the back-pointer ()Back-pointer: The note the Viterbi algorithm keeps for each state and time of which earlier state the best path came from; following them backwards from the best end recovers the most likely path. Explained in §5.3 The most likely path: the Viterbi algorithm. (CS188 §8.3). It is the forward algorithm with the sum replaced by a maximum:

mt​(x)=p(zt​∣x) x′max​p(x∣ut​,x′) mt−1​(x′)

Here x′ runs over the cells at t−1. Storing each sub-answer once and reusing it is dynamic programming ()Dynamic programming: Solving a problem by storing the answer to each overlapping sub-problem the first time it is computed and looking it up afterwards, so no work is repeated; memoization is one form of it. At t=2, cell 3. The best path into cell 3 comes from cell 1, cell 2 or cell 0, and m1​ is 0.06 at a door and 0.02 at a wall:

from cell 1: 0.8×0.06=0.048 from cell 2: 0.1×0.02=0.002 from cell 0: 0.1×0.02=0.002

The best is from cell 1, so the back-pointer of cell 3 at t=2 is cell 1, and:

m2​(3)=0.6×0.048=0.0288

This 0.0288 is the probability of the best path into cell 3, which is the robot’s true path: cell 1, then cell 3.

At t=3, cell 5. The best way in is from cell 3 at t=2:

m3​(5)=0.8×0.8×0.0288=0.0184

These are the true cells. The notebook checks all 1000 paths by brute force and finds the same path with the same probability, 0.0184. The Viterbi algorithm needed only 10 × 10 comparisons for each of the two steps after the first, 200 in all, against 1000 paths; for 100 steps it needs 10,000 against 10 to the power 100 (J&M App. A).

6. Summary

MethodAnswersOne stepCost
Histogram filterwhere the robot is nowslide and blur (predict), multiply and rescale (correct)every cell, every step
Static or adaptive cellshow to cut a continuous spacefixed grid, or fine cells only where the belief is highadaptive saves cells, costs bookkeeping
Binary Bayes filtera fixed yes-or-no stateadd the log of the Bayes factor for each readingone addition per reading
Forward-backwardwhere the robot was at an earlier timemultiply forward α and backward β, rescaletwo passes over the run
Viterbithe single most likely pathmaximum instead of sum, then follow back-pointers10 × 10 per step, not 10 to the power T
  • The histogram filter runs the Bayes filter exactly on a list of cells, because the sum over previous states has only as many terms as there are cells: 10 on the hallway instead of infinitely many, and only 3 of them count for each new cell.
  • Sensing multiplies each cell by p(z∣x) and rescales, so cells that explain the reading gain belief; moving convolves the belief with the motion kernel, because the same move error applies in every cell.
  • Moving without reading flattens the belief, so a robot must keep reading its sensors to stay localized.
  • A wrong reading moves the tallest bar but never removes the true cell, so the filter recovers after a few correct readings.
  • Fine cells explode in number with the space size and the number of state variables (7.2 million for a 30 m floor), so adaptive decompositions put small cells only where the belief is high.
  • For a static yes-or-no state each reading multiplies the odds by the Bayes factor, so in log odds it adds a fixed number; this is fast and does not round to 0 or 1.
  • Later readings tell where the robot was earlier: forward-backward raised the true starting cell from 0.19 to 0.36, and the Viterbi algorithm recovered the true path 1, 3, 5 with 200 comparisons instead of 1000 path products.

The robot can now run the Bayes filter on a computer, one number per cell, and look back over a recorded run.
