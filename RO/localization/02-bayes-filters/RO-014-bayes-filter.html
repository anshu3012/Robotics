1. Overview

Key point

The Bayes filter ()Bayes filter: The algorithm that keeps a robot’s belief up to date one step at a time: a prediction step after each command and a correction step after each reading; it gives the exact belief without storing the history, under the Markov assumption. Explained in §4.4 The algorithm. updates a robot’s belief one step at a time: a prediction step pushes the belief through the motion model after each command, and a correction step multiplies it by the measurement probability after each reading. The robot never needs to store its history.

So far the robot keeps a belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition: a probability for every possible state, given every command and every reading since it started. That list is what still breaks. The list grows by one command and one reading at every step: a robot that reads its sensor 10 times a second holds 36,000 readings after an hour, and by its definition the belief must be recomputed from all of them. This Note adds the Bayes filter, which keeps only the last belief, and answers the question: how can a robot keep its belief up to date at every step, using only the newest command and the newest reading?

3. One reading at a time: Bayes’ theorem with past readings

Key point

Bayes’ theorem ()Bayes’ theorem: The rule that reverses a conditional probability: from P(B∣A) it gives P(A∣B), so a belief about A can be updated after seeing B; P(A∣B)=P(B∣A)P(A)/P(B). Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §4. The formula and its proof turns “how likely is this reading in each state” into “how likely is each state given the reading”. Applied again with the first answer as the new prior, it adds a second reading, provided the readings are independent once the state is known.

3.1 Why the robot needs Bayes’ theorem

First the readings alone: can the robot take in a new reading without going back to the old ones? For now, nobody touches the door; the robot only reads. Its table tells it p(z∣x): how often the sensor reads “open” for each kind of door. The robot needs the other direction, p(x∣z): given the reading, how likely is each kind of door? For one reading:

  • x is the door, open or closed;
  • z is the reading, here “sense open”;
  • p(x) is the prior ()Prior: The probability of an event before any evidence is seen. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §3. The names of the four parts, the belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition in each door before the reading, 0.5 for open and 0.5 for closed;
  • p(z∣x) is the chance of the reading given the door, from the table (0.6 for an open door, 0.2 for a closed one);
  • p(z) is the overall chance of the reading, 0.4 for “sense open” (found below);
  • p(x∣z) is the posterior ()Posterior: The probability of an event after the evidence is taken into account. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §3. The names of the four parts, the belief in each door after the reading (0.75 for open, found below).

Bayes’ theorem ()Bayes’ theorem: The rule that reverses a conditional probability: from P(B∣A) it gives P(A∣B), so a belief about A can be updated after seeing B; P(A∣B)=P(B∣A)P(A)/P(B). Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §4. The formula and its proof turns one into the other:

p(x∣z)=p(z)p(z∣x) p(x)​

First reading, “sense open”. Multiply each prior by how well that door explains the reading:

open: 0.6×0.5=0.3 closed: 0.2×0.5=0.1

The two products are not yet probabilities, because they add to 0.4, not 1. Their sum is the evidence ()Evidence: The overall chance of seeing what we observed, counted over every possible hypothesis, written P(E). Bayes’ theorem divides by it: posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Bayes’ Theorem, §3. The names of the four parts p(z), the overall chance of the reading, by the law of total probability ()Law of total probability: The rule for the overall probability of an event: add its probability under each case, weighted by how likely that case is, P(B)=∑i​P(B∣Ai​)P(Ai​), when the cases Ai​ are mutually exclusive and cover every possibility. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: A Bayes’ Theorem Problem, §1. Overview. Divide by it:

p(open∣z1​)=0.3/0.4=0.75 p(closed∣z1​)=0.1/0.4=0.25

Dividing by the sum is the same as multiplying by its inverse, written η (eta):

η=1/0.4=2.5

This number is the normaliser ()Normalizer η: The factor that rescales a set of numbers, or a density, so that they add up (or integrate) to 1; it is always one over the current total: the sum of the products in the Bayes filter (1/0.4 = 2.5 after a reading in the door world), the area under the cut curve in the beam model (1/0.655 = 1.53). Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §4.1 Measurement noise: a Gaussian around the expected range: whatever factor makes the products add to 1, the same job it does for the cut Gaussian of the beam modelExplained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §4.1 Measurement noise: a Gaussian around the expected range. We never need p(z) from anywhere else, because it is always the sum of the products (PR §2.2).

3.2 A second reading: Bayes’ theorem with background knowledge

One reading leaves a 0.25 chance of a closed door. To be surer, the robot reads again and again gets “open”. Now the belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition must depend on both readings. For the second reading:

  • x is the door, open or closed;
  • z is the new reading, here the second reading z2​, “sense open”;
  • C is the background knowledge, everything known before z, here the first reading z1​, “sense open”.

Bayes’ theorem still works if every term also carries the earlier readings C behind the bar:

p(x∣z,C)=p(z∣C)p(z∣x,C) p(x∣C)​

The formula is Bayes’ rule with background knowledge ()Bayes’ rule with background knowledge: Bayes’ theorem with an extra condition C in every term, p(x∣z,C)=p(z∣x,C) p(x∣C)/p(z∣C), such as earlier readings; it lets the posterior after one reading serve as the prior for the next. Explained in §3.2 A second reading: Bayes’ theorem with background knowledge. (PR §2.2). The rule holds for the same reason as the plain rule: the joint probability of x and z given C splits two ways by the multiplication rule ()Multiplication rule: The chance that both A and B happen is the chance of B times the chance of A given B: P(A∩B)=P(A∣B)×P(B). Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: Conditional Probability, §5. The multiplication rule. The door’s numbers for an open door, all found below: 0.6 for a second “sense open” given the door and the first reading, 0.75 before the second reading, 0.9 after it, and 0.5 for a second “sense open” given the first:

0.6×0.75=0.45=0.9×0.5 p(z∣x,C) p(x∣C)=p(x,z∣C) =p(x∣z,C) p(z∣C) 0.9=0.45/0.5 p(x∣z,C)=p(x,z∣C)/p(z∣C)

With z2​ in place of z and z1​ in place of C, two of the terms are known:

  • p(x∣z1​) is the answer of Section 3.1: the old posterior becomes the new prior;
  • p(z2​∣x,z1​) asks how likely the second reading is, given the door and the first reading.

Here the robot makes an assumption: once the state of the door is known, the first reading tells nothing more about the second. Each reading is the door plus its own independent sensor noise. So:

p(z2​∣x,z1​)=p(z2​∣x)

The assumption is conditional independence ()Conditional independence: Independence that holds once a third variable is known: given the class, knowing one feature tells nothing more about another. Naive Bayes assumes it so it can multiply one probability per feature. Explained in: Naive Bayes: The Mathematics, §5. Step 3: the naive assumption: independence that holds given the state. The assumption does not say that the two readings are independent on their own. They are not, as the robot’s own numbers show:

p(z2​=open)=0.6×0.5+0.2×0.5 =0.4 p(z2​=open∣z1​=open) =0.6×0.75+0.2×0.25=0.5

A first “open” makes a second “open” more likely (0.5 instead of 0.4), because it makes an open door more likely. Only when the door is known does that link disappear.

Second reading, “sense open”. Same recipe, with 0.75 and 0.25 as the prior:

open: 0.6×0.75=0.45 closed: 0.2×0.25=0.05 η=1/(0.45+0.05)=2 p(open∣z1​,z2​)=2×0.45=0.9 p(closed∣z1​,z2​)=2×0.05=0.1

Figure 2 plays both readings. For each one, watch the two bars shrink when multiplied by 0.6 and 0.2, then grow back when rescaled by η, one over their sum: the belief in an open door climbs from 0.5 to 0.75, then to 0.9.

The robot never went back to the first reading: it kept one number, 0.75, and reused it. That reuse is the whole idea of a filter. So far, though, nothing has changed the door between readings.

4. The Bayes filter: predict, then correct

Key point

Each step has two parts. Prediction: after the command, add up every way of reaching each state. Correction: after the reading, multiply by the measurement probability and rescale.

4.1 Why a moving world needs a second step

Section 3 kept only the last answer, but it assumed nothing changes the door. Once the robot pushes, the door may change between readings, and a robot that drives changes its own position. The belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.2 The definition, the robot’s probability for each door state, must now follow two kinds of event: commands, which change the state and usually add doubt, and readings, which add knowledge (see the predicted belief ())Predicted belief: The belief after the latest command but before the latest reading, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t−1​,u1:t​); it separates the doubt a move adds from the knowledge a reading brings. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.5 The predicted belief: after the move, before the reading.

The direct way to know the belief is to recompute it from the whole history at every step (Figure 3, the archive row). Recomputing from the history is an archive, not a filter: after an hour at 10 readings a second, it holds 36,000 readings and keeps growing. 4.2 The prediction step

After the command, before the reading, the robot wants its predicted belief ()Predicted belief: The belief after the latest command but before the latest reading, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t−1​,u1:t​); it separates the doubt a move adds from the knowledge a reading brings. Explained in: Belief: What the Robot Knows, §4.5 The predicted belief: after the move, before the reading bel(xt​). The robot does not know the previous state, so it considers every previous state, asks how likely the command takes it to xt​, and weights that by how much it believed in that previous state. At step t:

  • xt−1​ is the door before the command, open or closed;
  • xt​ is the door after the command, open or closed;
  • ut​ is the command, push at t=2;
  • bel(xt−1​) is the belief before the command, a number from 0 to 1 (0.75 for open after t=1);
  • p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​) is the state transition probability of Section 2, the chance that the command turns door xt−1​ into door xt​ (0.8 for a push that opens a closed door);
  • ∑xt−1​​ adds over every possible previous state, here open and closed;
  • bel(xt​) is the predicted belief, after the command and before the reading, a number from 0 to 1 (0.95 for open at t=2).

Adding up every way of reaching xt​ is the law of total probability ()Law of total probability: The rule for the overall probability of an event: add its probability under each case, weighted by how likely that case is, P(B)=∑i​P(B∣Ai​)P(Ai​), when the cases Ai​ are mutually exclusive and cover every possibility. Explained in: Naive Bayes Foundations: A Bayes’ Theorem Problem, §1. Overview:

bel(xt​)=xt−1​∑​p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​) × bel(xt−1​)

The formula is the prediction step ()Prediction step (motion update): The Bayes-filter step after a command: for each state, add up the probability of reaching it from every previous state, weighted by the belief in that state, bel(xt​)=∑p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​) bel(xt−1​); it moves the belief with the robot and usually widens it, because the motion is uncertain; a near-certain command, such as a push that opens a door, can make the belief sharper. Explained in §4.2 The prediction step., also called the motion update or time update (PR §2.4.1).

At t=2, the command is “push”, and the belief ()Belief: The robot’s probability for each possible state given all its commands and readings, bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); a short summary of the history that says how strongly the evidence points to each state.
