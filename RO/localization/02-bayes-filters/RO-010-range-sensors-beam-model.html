1. Overview

Key point

A robot judges a guess about its pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. The beam model gives that number for one reading as a mix of four causes, so that a person walking past does not make the right pose look impossible.

So far the robot has a grid map ()Location-based map (grid map): A map that splits space into cells and stores a value for every cell, occupied or free, free space included (2800 cells for a 7 m by 4 m room at 0.1 m); larger, but it answers at once whether a pose is free. 2. What a range sensor measures

Key point

A range sensor reports, for each direction, the distance to the first surface the beam meets. A measurement model scores a guessed pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading, a position and heading, by how likely those distances are from there.

2.1 Range finders and scans

The four numbers of Figure 1 come from a range finder ()Range finder: A sensor that measures the distance to the nearest surface in one direction, for example by timing a laser or sound pulse; robots use it to compare what they see with a map. 2.2 Why we need a measurement model

Explained in §2.2 Why we need a measurement model., written z∗.

2.3 Why a whole scan is a product of beams

A scan has many beams, and writing one density for all of them at once is hard. We split it into one density per beam and multiply. With the symbols of Section 2.1 and Section 2.2:

  • zk is the reading of beam k, in m;
  • K is the number of beams, 4;
  • x is the guessed pose, such as A;
  • m is the map;
  • ∏ means “multiply the terms for k=1 to K“.
p(z∣x,m)=k=1∏K​p(zk∣x,m)

Multiplying is allowed when the beams are independent: knowing one beam’s error tells us nothing about another’s. Independence does not hold in general, but it nearly holds once the pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading and the map are known (Thrun §6.3.1):

  • Without a map, beams are linked: if one beam hits something at 2 m, the beam half a degree away probably hits the same object at about 2 m.
  • With the map and the pose given, that shared object is already in the map, so each beam’s remaining error is its own sensor noise, and the beams are close to independent.

Independence that holds only once a third thing is known is conditional independence ()Conditional independence: Independence that holds once a third variable is known: given the class, knowing one feature tells nothing more about another. Naive Bayes assumes it so it can multiply one probability per feature. Explained in: Naive Bayes: The Mathematics, §5. Step 3: the naive assumption, the same assumption Naive Bayes makes for features given the class.

Extra

The assumption is not exact. A person blocks several neighbouring beams at once, and their errors are then linked. The product then counts that one person several times, which makes the model overconfident (Freiburg sensor-model slides, “Summary beam-based model”). Implementations therefore use only a subset of the beams of each scan: the ROS localization package AMCL uses 30 evenly spaced beams per scan by default (ROS amcl, laser_max_beams).

So scoring a pose comes down to one density per beam; what is still missing is the shape of that density.

3. Why a Gaussian alone fails

Key point

A narrow Gaussian around the expected range gives a reading caused by a passer-by a density of practically zero, and the product then rejects the true pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading, the robot’s real position and heading, outright.

The first rule that comes to mind fails on the person’s 1.00 m reading of Figure 1. The rule says the reading should be the expected range ()Expected range z∗: The distance a beam would read with a perfect sensor, if the robot were at a given pose in the map; the measurement model compares each real reading with it. Explained in §2.2 Why we need a measurement model., the distance the map predicts, plus a small sensor error, and small errors follow a Gaussian distribution ()Gaussian distribution: Another name for the normal distribution: the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution set by its mean and standard deviation, used to model many measurements. Explained in: The Normal Distribution, §2. What the normal distribution is. Because the scan density is a product, one zero beam makes the whole scan density zero, whatever the other beams say. So the true pose A gets 0. The wrong pose B fits the backward beam far worse but escapes the zero: its forward beam expects 2 m, and its backward beam expects 3 m but reads 2.04 m. Those two densities are tiny but not zero, and B ends up with a scan density of about 4×10−164. A Gaussian alone would prefer the wrong pose, only because a person stepped in front of the robot.

The cure is not a wider Gaussian: that would make every reading near the wall less informative too. Instead we list the other ways a reading can come about, and give each its own shape.

4.2 Unexpected objects: why the curve falls off, and why it stops

A person, a chair or an open door that is not in the map can block the beam first. The reading is then shorter than z∗. Such a blocker is an unexpected object ()Unexpected object (pshort​): Something not in the map, such as a person, that blocks the beam before the mapped surface and gives a short reading; modelled as an exponential from 0 up to z∗, because only the first blocker counts. Explained in §4.2 Unexpected objects: why the curve falls off, and why it stops., and its reading a short reading. Figure 5 shows its shape: high at 0, falling off, and zero after z∗. Both features have a reason.

Why it stops at the expected range. An object behind the wall cannot block the beam: the wall is hit first. So a short reading is always shorter than z∗, and the density is 0 beyond it.

The first cell is blocked in 8 of the 16 patterns, more than all the patterns that first stop further out:

1+2+4=7

The count halves with each cell, because each extra free cell in front is one more condition the pattern must meet.

The same holds for any blocking chance. Let each cell be blocked independently of the others:

  • b is the chance that one cell is blocked, a number from 0 to 1 (0.2 below);
  • j is a cell’s number along the beam, counted from the robot (1, 2, 3, …);
  • P(first stop at cell j) is the probability that the beam stops at cell j and not before.

For the beam to stop at cell j, the j−1 cells before it must be free and cell j blocked:

P(first stop at cell j)=(1−b)j−1 b

Each further cell multiplies the chance by the same factor 1−b. With cells of 0.1 m and b=0.2:

P(cell 1)=0.2 P(cell 2)=0.8×0.2=0.16 P(cell 3)=0.82×0.2=0.128

6. Learning the model’s numbers from data

Key point

Record many readings of a wall at a known distance and choose the weights and widths that make those readings most likely. The causes are hidden, so EM finds them by alternating “which cause made each reading?” with “refit each cause”.

6.1 Why the numbers must be learned

The answer of Section 5.2, the scan about 215 times more likely from pose ()Pose: Where a robot is and which way it faces: on a floor, its position (x,y) and heading θ, such as (2 m, 1 m, 30°); needed because a robot at one spot drives off differently depending on its heading. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading A (the robot’s true position and heading) than from pose B, rests on six numbers we chose: the four weights, the standard deviation of the hits σhit​ (0.05 m) and the rate of short readings λshort​ (2 per m). The six numbers are the model’s intrinsic parameters ()Intrinsic parameters (of a sensor model): The numbers that describe a sensor and its surroundings rather than the pose, such as the beam model’s four weights, σhit​ and λshort​; they are learned from recorded readings. Explained in §6.1 Why the numbers must be learned.: they belong to the sensor and its surroundings, not to the pose (Thrun §6.3.4). A sonar is noisier than a laser, and a busy hall has more short readings than an empty lab, so no textbook value fits every robot. We measure them.
