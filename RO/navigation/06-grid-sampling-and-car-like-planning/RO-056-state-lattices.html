1. Overview

Key point

A state lattice fixes the car’s states to whole-metre points and a few headings, and fixes its moves in advance so that every move ends exactly on another lattice state. Plain A* then finds the cheapest path the lattice holds, ends exactly on the goal, and checks each move by looking up the map cells it sweeps, listed before planning.

The car of Hybrid A* and path smoothingExplained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §1. Overview can already back into its bay. Hybrid A* ()Hybrid A:* A* over grid cells and heading ranges in which each state keeps the exact pose that reached it most cheaply and every move is an arc the car can drive, such as six 1.5 m arcs per pose; its path is drivable, but because it drops other poses in the same cell it is not guaranteed to be the cheapest. Explained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §2.1 Six arcs from every pose, A* over cells and heading ranges whose moves are arcs the car can drive, finds a 39.42 m path after 312 expansions. We keep the same lot and the same car: 4.0 m by 1.8 m, its tightest circle 5 m, starting at (2, 3) facing 0° and backing into the bay at (12, 23) facing −90°.

What still breaks shows in RO-055’s search:

  • The arcs never land on the goal. Each arc turns the heading by 17.2°, and no whole number of 17.2° steps from 0° makes −90°. Without the Reeds-Shepp shot the search expanded 1,949 states and stopped 0.54 m and 5° from the goal.
  • The cheapest path is not promised. Poses that fall in the same cell and heading range are merged and all but one dropped, so the cheapest path may be among the dropped ones (Dolgov et al. 2008).
  • Every move is worked out during the search. The search drove 1,866 arcs through the car’s motion equations and checked the body at 7 places along each.

This Note fixes all three at once by designing the moves before planning:

  • why moves that are not designed never land on the same point twice (Section 2);
  • moves that end exactly on lattice points, the control set (Section 3);
  • the cells each move sweeps, worked out once (Section 4);
  • A* on the lattice, and how its path compares with Hybrid A*‘s (Section 5).

2. Why fixed moves never close up

Key point

If the car repeats Hybrid A*‘s full-left arc of 1.5 m, it never returns to the same point: one lap of the 5 m circle is 20.94 arcs, not a whole number. Moves whose lengths do not fit the geometry make endless different states; moves designed to fit a grid make a finite graph.

RO-055’s first problem was that the arcs never land exactly on the goal. The cause is general (LaValle §14.2.2.3), and the simplest case shows it: the car steers full left and keeps driving arcs of 1.5 m round its 5 m circle. One lap is the circumference:

2π×5=31.416 m 31.416/1.5=20.94 arcs

Figure 1 plays it. Watch the end points of the arcs: after 21 arcs the car is round once more, but not on the start.

Figure 1: The car drives full-left arcs of 1.5 m round its 5 m circle, one arc per frame. Blue dots: ends of the arcs of lap 1; orange: lap 2; purple: lap 3. After 21 arcs (1.00 laps) the end of the 21st arc lies 0.084 m past the start; after 63 arcs (3.01 laps) there are 64 different end points, none repeated. Last frame: a quarter-turn move of the same circle, 7.85 m long (green squares), is back on the start after 4 moves

Twenty-one arcs turn the car by:

21×0.3=6.3 rad 6.3−2π=0.0168 rad

so the end of the 21st arc lies 0.0168 rad of the circle past the start, along the 5 m circle:

0.0168×5=0.084 m

Every lap shifts by the same amount, so the ends never meet: after 63 arcs there are 64 different points. Because the lap is not a whole number of arcs, the ends keep filling the circle, and the search graph they make has no end of states (LaValle §14.2.2.3). Hybrid A* copes by merging nearby poses into cells; that is exactly the merging that cost it the promise of the cheapest path.

The last frame of Figure 1 shows the other way out. A quarter turn of the same circle is 7.85 m:

2π​×5=7.854 m

Four such moves bring the car back exactly to the start. A move whose length is chosen to fit the geometry lands on the same few points over and over.

RO-054 met this good case in its straight drive: with speed changes held for a fixed time, positions and speeds fell on a regular grid, and the lattice search ()Lattice search: Graph search over states that the primitives keep on a regular grid, such as positions every 0.0625 m and speeds every 0.25 m/s when the robot accelerates, coasts or brakes every 0.5 s; any graph search then finds the best plan on that grid. Explained in: Planning with Motion Limits, §6. Searching the primitives systematically: lattice search searched it. For the car we can choose the grid first and then design the moves to fit it. The states are:

  • positions on whole metres, (x, y) = (2, 3), (2, 4), and so on;
  • headings in steps of 45°: 0°, 45°, 90°, up to 315°.

A regular grid of states, joined only by moves the vehicle can drive that end exactly on other states of the grid, is a state lattice ()State lattice: A regular grid of states, such as positions on whole metres and headings in steps of 45 deg, joined only by drivable moves that end exactly on other states of the grid; no states need merging, so plain A* finds the cheapest path the lattice holds. Explained in §2. Why fixed moves never close up. (Pivtoraiko, Knepper and Kelly 2009). Its states are exactly the lattice points, so nothing needs merging, and a search over it is plain A*. What remains is to find the moves.

3. Moves that end on the lattice: the control set

Key point

A turn on the 5 m circle alone ends between lattice points, but a short straight piece before and after the arc can be sized so that the move ends exactly on one. The moves from each heading, worked out once and copied to every state, are the lattice’s control set.

3.1 A 45° turn that lands on a lattice point

Section 2 asked for moves that land on lattice points. Start from (0, 0) facing 0° and turn left by 45° on the 5 m circle. The arc alone ends at (3.536, 1.464): between lattice points, as in Figure 2.

Figure 2: Building the 45° left turn. Grey dots: lattice points, 1 m apart. Red dashed: the arc alone, which ends at (3.536, 1.464), between points. Orange, blue, orange: a straight piece of 0.929 m, the 45° arc of the 5 m circle (3.927 m), and a straight piece of 0.757 m along 45°, which end exactly on (5, 2) facing 45°

Add a straight piece before the arc, along 0°, and one after it, along 45°, and choose their lengths so that the end is a lattice point. With:

  • ρ, the radius of the circle, 5 m;
  • ϕ, the turn, 45°;
  • a, the length of the straight piece before the arc, in m;
  • b, the length of the straight piece after it, in m;
  • X and Y, the lattice point we aim for, (5, 2) in m.

The arc alone ends at (3.5355, 1.4645), and driving along 45° adds the same amount to x and to y, 0.7071 m for every metre. The y coordinate gives b; the x coordinate, which gains the same 0.5355 m from the second straight piece, then gives a. Numbers first, then in symbols:

1.4645+0.7071b=2 b=0.5355/0.7071=0.757 m a+3.5355+0.5355=5 a=0.929 m Y=ρ(1−cosϕ)+bsinϕ X=a+ρsinϕ+bcosϕ

The move is 5.613 m long:

0.929+3.927+0.757=5.613 m

and every part of it is drivable: straight, then the 5 m circle, then straight. Pivtoraiko et al. solve the same problem with smoother curves whose steering changes gradually; straight-arc-straight moves are the simplest that land exactly.

3.2 The control set

The other moves are found the same way (notebook). From a state facing 0° (Figure 3, left):

  • straight 1 m, ending on (1, 0);
  • turn 45° left or right, ending on (5, 2) or (5, −2);
  • turn 90° left or right, a quarter of the 5 m circle, ending on (5, 5) or (5, −5) with no straight pieces needed.

From a state facing 45° (Figure 3, right), the straight move is a diagonal of 1.414 m to (1, 1), and the 45° turns end on (2, 5) and (5, 2). Each move also exists in reverse: the same shape mirrored, driven backwards, which also ends on a lattice point.

Figure 3: The control set. Left: the 10 moves from a state facing 0°: straight 1 m, 45° turns to (5, ±2) and 90° turns to (5, ±5), forward (blue) and in reverse (purple). Right: the 6 moves from a state facing 45°: the 1.414 m diagonal and 45° turns to (2, 5) and (5, 2), forward and in reverse. Every move ends on a lattice point (grey dots) with a heading that is a multiple of 45°

The moves work like toy bricks made to snap only onto studs: whatever order they are joined in, each ends on a stud, here a lattice point. The number of moves from a state is the search’s branching factor ()Branching factor b: The number of choices at each step of a search, such as 4 moves on a 4-connected grid or 18 turns of a Rubik’s cube; level d of a search tree holds up to bd nodes, so it sets how fast the tree grows. The diagonal headings get their own moves because a move designed for 0° does not stay on the lattice when turned by 45°: (5, 2) turned by 45° is not a pair of whole numbers. Turning by 90° does keep whole numbers, so the set for 0° serves 90°, 180° and 270°, and the set for 45° serves the other diagonals (Hauser ch. 11 makes the same split into axis-aligned and diagonal moves).

3.3 Copying a move to any state

The lattice repeats: a move that joins two lattice states also joins every other pair arranged the same way (Pivtoraiko et al., §2.1). So during the search a move is turned to the state’s heading and added to its position (Hauser ch. 11). With:

  • (x,y), the state’s position, in m;
  • θ, its heading;
  • (Δx,Δy), where the move ends when it starts from (0, 0) facing 0°, in m;
  • (x′,y′), where it ends from the state, in m.

The lattice path of Section 5 turns left by 45° from (17, 9) facing 90°; the 0° move ends on (5, 2); the cosine of 90° is 0 and its sine is 1:

x′=17+0×5−1×2=15 y′=9+1×5+0×2=14 x′=x+Δxcosθ−Δysinθ y′=y+Δxsinθ+Δycosθ

The move ends on (15, 14), facing 135°: a lattice state again. So each move is built once, as an “action template”, and replicated at every state the search reaches (CMU 16-350 slides). The moves are in place; each still has to be checked against the parked cars.

4. Checking a move by look-up: the swept cells

Key point

Because every move starts on a lattice point, the cells of a fine map that the car’s body sweeps during the move are the same at every state, shifted. The cells can be listed once per move, and checking a move during the search is looking up whether any of those cells is occupied.

Hybrid A* checked its body at 7 places along every arc, during the search. On the lattice that work can move to before the search. Take a fine map of the lot in cells of 0.1 m, each marked occupied if its centre lies inside a parked car or outside the lot. During a move, the car’s body (grown by RO-055’s safety margin of 0.1 m) passes over a set of these cells, the move’s swept cells (Pivtoraiko et al., §3.3; CMU 16-350 case study). Figure 4 drives the 90° left turn from (12, 3), the turn the lattice path takes round the end of the middle row; watch the blue cells pile up behind the body.

Figure 4: The 90° left turn from (12, 3) facing 0°, driven step by step: blue, the map cells of 0.1 m that the car’s body, grown by 0.1 m, has swept so far; grey, the map cells inside parked cars; green, the body. Last frame: all 3,260 swept cells; no blue cell is grey, so the move is free

Lattice points are whole metres, exactly 10 map cells apart, so a move’s swept cells at another state are the same list shifted by whole cells. The 64 lists, one per move and start heading, are worked out once; during the search, checking a move is reading the map at its listed cells. The whole search of Section 5 made 5,192 such look-ups and drove no arc through the motion equations, where Hybrid A* drove 1,866 arcs and checked each at 7 places. Pivtoraiko et al. write that adding up the cells a move sweeps “is typically much more efficient than simulating the motion of the system”. The moves and their checks are ready; what is left is the search itself.

5. Searching the lattice with A*

Key point

A* over lattice states (with RO-055’s costs and guess) finds a 42.93 m path to the exact goal pose after 629 expansions, and no other path in the lattice is cheaper. The path is longer than Hybrid A*‘s 39.42 m because 8 headings and fixed turns are coarse; finer lattices close the gap.

5.1 The search

With states, moves and checks in place, the search is A* ()A search:* Dijkstra’s algorithm with the open list sorted by f=g+h, the cost so far plus a heuristic guess of the cost left; with a consistent heuristic it returns the shortest route while expanding far fewer states (9 cells against 54 on the room grid). Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §4.5 The algorithm as in RO-034. Its costs and guess are RO-055’s: each metre forward costs 1 and each metre in reverse 2, each switch between them adds 2 m, and the guess h of the cost left is the larger of the Reeds-Shepp length and the grid distanceExplained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §3. Two guesses of the cost left. A state is a lattice point, a heading and the direction of the last move, so that a switch can be charged. Figure 5 runs it.

Figure 5: A* on the state lattice. Grey dots: lattice points. Each expansion takes the state with the smallest estimated total cost f, cost so far plus guess (orange arrow) and copies the control set there: moves whose swept cells touch a parked car or a wall red, moves to a state already reached more cheaply grey dotted, kept moves blue (forward) or purple (reverse). The path drives 10 m straight along the lower lane, turns 90° left round the end of the middle row, drives 1 m, makes two 45° left turns into the upper lane, drives 3 m past the bay, then backs in with a reverse 90° turn and 2 m reverse straight. Its forward and reverse lengths:

10+7.854+1+2×5.6133+3 =33.0806 m forward 7.854+2=9.854 m in reverse

Its length and its cost:

33.0806+9.854=42.93 m 33.0806+2×9.854+2=54.79

The path ends exactly on (12, 23) facing −90°, without any shot, because the goal is a lattice state. Its body stays at least 0.25 m from the parked cars.

The lattice path is 3.51 m longer:

42.93−39.42=3.51 m

The lattice is cheapest among its own moves, and its moves are coarse: only 8 headings, and turns of 45° or 90° with straight pieces attached. Hybrid A*‘s arcs can stop turning at any multiple of 17.2°, and its final Reeds-Shepp shot can take any shape. Lattices used in practice are finer: Nav2’s lattice planner ships a control set with 16 headings by default, and CMU’s car Boss planned its parking manoeuvres in the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge on a lattice with 32 headings and 36 moves per state (CMU 16-350 case study). Finer lattices find shorter paths but branch more, so their searches need the good guesses of RO-055: for Boss, 2,019 states expanded with the larger of two guesses, against 26,108 and 124,794 with either guess alone (CMU 16-350 case study).

The car can now plan into the bay with every move checked in advance and every state exact.
