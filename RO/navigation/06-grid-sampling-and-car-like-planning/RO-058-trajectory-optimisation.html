1. Overview

Key point

A global planner makes a route from the map, rarely; a local planner turns the next few metres of it into a timed trajectory, within the robot’s limits and around things the map does not have, by solving an optimisation problem every 0.1 s. When a person steps onto the route in front of the robot, the local planner bends round them, and the robot reaches the door at 8.0 s instead of 6.7 s.

2. Global plan and local planner

Key point

Planning is split into levels: a global plan over the whole map, made rarely, and a local planner that plans the next few metres or seconds many times a second, with the time, the motion limits and the obstacles the map lacks. The local planner’s trajectory comes from an optimisation that is either solved once from a search’s path or solved again every cycle from its own last answer.

The opening’s first problem is the person the map did not have: a route made from the map cannot include them.

2.1 Levels of planning

A car’s planning is split into levels, each working coarser than the one below it, with route planning ()Route planning (mission planning): Finding the best way from where a vehicle is to its destination on a map of the road network, by travel time or by distance, such as the 492.8 s route across the Chicago map; the top level of planning, which picks the roads and lanes and leaves the motion to the levels below. Explained in: Route Planning on Road and Route Graphs, §2. Why route planning is its own level, which picks the roads and lanes, at the top. Self-driving cars stack four levels (Paden et al. 2016 §II):

  1. route planning picks the way through the map to the destination;
  2. the behavioural layer picks the task for now: follow the lane, stop at the line, give way;
  3. local motion planning turns that task into a path or a trajectory for the next stretch, around what the sensors see;
  4. feedback control turns the trajectory into wheel or steering commands and corrects the robot as it drives.

A robot in a building splits its planning the same way (Figure 2). The planner that finds a route over the whole map, from the robot to the goal, and runs rarely is the global planner ()Global planner: The planner that finds a route over the whole map, from the robot to its goal, and runs rarely, such as A* on a room’s grid or a road network; its route, the global plan, guides the local planner but has no timing and includes only the obstacles on the map. Explained in §2.1 Levels of planning.; its route is the global plan. In the room it is A* on the 0.5 m grid, 3.414 m. 3. The trajectory optimisation problem

Key point

A trajectory gives the robot’s state at every moment and the controls that produce it. Trajectory optimisation chooses the numbers that describe it, the decision variables, to make a cost such as the time as small as possible while the motion rules, the start and the end, the limits and the clearance from obstacles hold, either exactly (hard constraints) or by paying a penalty (soft constraints).

The opening’s second problem was the clock: the route says where to drive, not when. This section says what the local planner must find.

3.1 What a trajectory is

The idea. A path is a line on the floor. A trajectory is the same line with a clock on it: where the robot is at each moment, how fast it goes and how it turns. To describe the robot at one moment we need four numbers, its state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.1 State: from one number to many, everything about the robot that matters for what happens next, and two commands, its control inputs ()Control input u: The numbers we choose to drive a robot, such as u=(v,ω); the model turns them into motion. 3.2 Decision variables, cost and constraints

The trajectory of Figure 4 was chosen among many; choosing needs three things: what we may change, what makes one answer better than another, and what every answer must obey.

What we choose. A computer cannot choose a function of time with a value at every moment, so the trajectory is described by a list of numbers: Figure 4’s band is 10 poses and the 9 time steps between them. The numbers an optimisation is free to choose are its decision variables ()Decision variables: The numbers an optimisation is free to choose, such as the poses and time steps of a band or the controls at each interval; the solver changes them to lower the cost while the constraints hold. Explained in §3.2 Decision variables, cost and constraints.. Section 6.1 shows the ways to pick them.

What it costs. “Best” needs a number, the cost. The local planner of Figure 1 and the drive of Section 9 ask for the least time, the duration of the drive. Another common cost is the least effort in a fixed time, a drive of exactly 8 s as gently as possible (Kelly 2017), which the next Note uses to compare solvers on the 8 s driveExplained in: Solving Trajectory Optimisation: Shooting, iLQR and DDP, §2.1 The 8 s drive in numbers.

What must hold. The rules every acceptable answer must meet are its constraints ()Constraint: A condition the solution must satisfy; in SVM, yi​(wTxi​+b)≥1 for every training point. Explained in: The Mathematics of SVM: Hard Margin, §4.2 One constraint for both classes:

  1. the motion rules of Section 3.1, at every moment;
  2. the start, where the robot is now, and the end, the local goal or the door pose;
  3. the limits at every moment: both wheels at most 0.6 m/s, the acceleration between −0.5 and 0.5 m/s², the speed at least 0;
  4. the clearance: the disk’s centre at least 0.25 m from the table, the walls and the person.

Finding the decision variables that make the cost as small as possible while every constraint holds is trajectory optimisation ()Trajectory optimisation: Choosing the numbers that describe a trajectory to make a cost such as the time or the effort as small as possible while the motion rules, the start and the end, the limits and the clearance from obstacles hold; it computes one good trajectory where a rule for every state would be far too large. Explained in §3.2 Decision variables, cost and constraints. (Kelly 2017; Lynch and Park §10.7). In symbols (Lynch and Park §10.7), with:

  • x, the state, the four numbers (x,y,θ,v);
  • u, the controls, the two numbers (a,ω);
  • f, the four motion rules of Section 3.1, which give how fast each state number changes;
  • T, the duration of the drive, in s, the cost.
find a(t),ω(t),T minimising T with x˙=f(x,u) x(0)=start x(T)=goal and the limits at every t

One trajectory, not a rule for every state. The best possible answer would be a policy ()Policy: The agent’s rules for which action to take. Explained in: Types of Machine Learning, §5.1 Agent, environment, policy and reward, the best controls from every state the robot could be in, but even a coarse grid of our 4-number state holds over 10 million states. Trajectory optimisation finds one good trajectory from this start instead, which needs far less computation, and needs a new one when the robot drifts or a person steps in (Kelly 2017); the local planner solves again every 0.1 s.

3.3 Hard and soft constraints

The local planner must solve its problem in a few milliseconds, and a constraint can be handled in two ways that differ in how strictly it holds.

The idea. A wall is a hard limit: the robot cannot be on the other side, at any price. A speed limit with a fine is a soft one: exceeding it is allowed, but it costs, and the larger the excess, the higher the cost. A solver can treat the 0.6 m/s wheel limit either way.

The standard terms. A constraint the answer must meet exactly, so that no answer that breaks it is accepted, is a hard constraint ()Hard constraint: A constraint the answer must meet exactly, so no answer that breaks it is accepted, such as a wheel speed that may never pass 0.6 m/s; it holds whatever it costs. Explained in §3.3 Hard and soft constraints.. A constraint replaced by an extra cost, a penalty that is 0 while the constraint holds and grows with the amount it is broken, so that the problem has no constraints left, is a soft constraint ()Soft constraint (penalty): A constraint replaced by an extra cost that is 0 while it holds and grows with how far it is broken, such as a weighted squared excess over a speed limit; the problem then has no constraints and is easy to solve, but the answer may exceed the limit slightly when that lowers the total cost. Explained in §3.3 Hard and soft constraints.; handling constraints this way is the penalty method (Paden et al. 2016 §IV-C). TEB turns every constraint into a squared penalty, so its problem becomes a sum of squares (Rösmann et al. 2017).

On one piece of the band. Take one straight piece of a band, 0.18 m long, with both wheels at the speed v. Its time cost is the time step squared; its penalty for the wheel limit, with the weight 2 that TEB gives its speed limit, starts 0.05 m/s inside the limit, at 0.55 m/s, a safety margin (teb_local_planner). The sum still falls at 0.6 m/s, so its lowest point lies above the limit: at 0.62 m/s (notebook). With a weight of 1000 instead of 2 the lowest point moves to 0.550 m/s, inside the limit. A soft constraint holds only as well as its weight makes it: the answer of the penalised problem equals the constrained one only when every weight grows without end, and very large weights make the problem hard to solve accurately, so TEB’s paper uses weight 1 for most penalties and about 1000 for the motion rule (Rösmann et al. 2017).

Penalties let a solver start from any guess, even one that breaks every limit, but the answer it settles on may still break some (Paden et al. 2016 §IV-C). So the robot keeps its own hard limits underneath: in the room, the wheels never pass 0.6 m/s and the speed never changes faster than 0.5 m/s², whatever the band asks for. The problem is stated; the first thing to optimise with it is the global plan’s shape.

4. Smoothing as optimisation: the elastic band

Key point

Smoothing a route can be written as one optimisation problem: move each point sideways, within a bound, to make the sum of squared second differences, how far each point lies from the middle of its neighbours, as small as possible. The cost is a quadratic program whose matrix has the same 1, 5, 6 pattern in Autoware’s path smoother and in Apollo’s reference-line smoother.

The opening’s third problem was the route’s corners: it turns 45° at (2.75, 1.75), (2.75, 3.25) and (3.25, 3.75), and a robot at speed cannot turn on a point.

Path smoothing ()Path smoothing: Moving the points of a found path, sweep after sweep, a little towards their found places and a little towards their neighbours, yi​←yi​+α(xi​−yi​)+β(yi+1​+yi−1​−2yi​); it rounds corners and removes needless steering, but on its own it ignores obstacles, the turning circle and the goal heading. Explained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §5.1 The smoothing rule pulled each point a little towards its neighbours, sweep after sweep. Real planners write the same aim as one cost, add bounds on how far each point may move, and hand the whole problem to a solver, so that every point moves at once to the best place the bounds allow (Autoware path smoother; Apollo planning).

4.1 One corner by hand

The idea. Three points are straight and evenly spaced when the middle one sits exactly halfway between the other two. How far it sits from that halfway point, doubled, measures how sharply the path bends there.

Take the first corner of the route and its two neighbours (Figure 6):

  • p0​, p1​, p2​, three route points, in m: (2.25, 1.25), (2.75, 1.75) and (2.75, 2.25).

5. The timed elastic band

Key point

A timed elastic band (TEB) is a list of poses with the time between neighbours. Speeds, turn rates and accelerations come from differences between neighbours; the cost is the sum of the squared time steps plus squared penalties for every broken rule, and Levenberg–Marquardt steps solve it fast because each term involves only a few neighbouring numbers.

The smoothed route of Section 4 still has no clock and runs through the person; the local planner of Figure 1 fixes both in one solve.

TEB is a local planner that plugs into the ROS navigation stack, and has been ported to Nav2 (teb_local_planner). The stack’s own classic local planner, the dynamic window approach (DWA), tries a few constant pairs of speed and turn rate (by default 3 speeds and 20 turn rates, each held for 1.7 s), scores where each would take the robot, and keeps the best; it searches among sampled commands, while TEB adjusts every pose of its trajectory with a numerical solver (DWAPlanner.cfg; Rösmann et al. 2021). Classical local planning, a later Note (RO-078), teaches DWA.

5.1 Poses with time steps

The idea. Lay beads along the next 1.5 m of the route, the first on the robot and the last on the local goal, the route’s pose 1.5 m ahead. Between each pair of neighbours, write how many seconds the robot takes. Now the beads say where, and the numbers between them say when: Figure 4 was such a band.

The standard term. The motion rule. 5.2 The cost: time plus penalties

The band must be fast, obey the arc rule, and keep every limit and the clearance. TEB adds all of these into one sum of squares (Rösmann et al. 2017; teb_local_planner), with the weights of its default settings:

  • the time: each time step squared, weight 1; the sum of squared time steps tends to make the steps equal (Rösmann et al. 2017);
  • the arc rule: each ek​ squared, weight 1000;
  • the wheels: each wheel’s excess over 0.55 m/s squared, weight 2 (our robot’s wheel limit in place of TEB’s separate speed and turn-rate limits);
  • the acceleration: the excess of ak​ over 0.45 m/s² squared, weight 1;
  • the direction: how far a piece runs backwards, squared, weight 1;
  • the clearance: at every pose and halfway between neighbours, how far the disk’s edge comes within 0.15 m of an obstacle, squared, weight 50.

The clearance is TEB’s min_obstacle_dist plus its margin; we set the distance to 0.1 m for the small room (TEB’s default is 0.5 m). TEB checks the clearance only at its poses, and its FAQ warns that poses can spread apart across a gap (TEB FAQ); our band also checks the point halfway between neighbours.

In the first cycle the cost of the first guess, the route’s poses spaced at full speed, is 21.38; after optimising it is 1.182 (notebook). The optimised band still asks for 0.649 m/s² at the start, above the 0.5 m/s² limit: the acceleration is a soft constraint of weight 1, cheaper to exceed slightly than to lose time (Section 3.3). The robot’s own limit holds it to 0.5 m/s², and the next cycle plans again from where the robot really is.

5.3 Solving it: sparse least squares

The cost is a sum of squares of numbers that each depend on a few neighbouring poses and time steps. Minimising such a sum is a least-squares problem, the kind ordinary least squares ()Ordinary least squares (OLS, least squares): The way linear regression finds its line with one formula (a closed-form method): the line with the smallest sum of squared errors. Explained in: Simple Linear Regression: The Mathematics from Scratch, §2. Two ways to find m and b solves for a line in one formula, here with curved terms that need steps.

The steps. At the current band, write each squared number as a straight-line guess in the decision variables, using its Jacobian ()Jacobian: The table (matrix) of all first partial derivatives of a function with several inputs and outputs, one row per output and one column per input, Jij​=∂fi​/∂xj​; it shows how every output changes with every input near a point. Explained in: The Jacobian and Gradients of Matrices, §4.2 The formula: every partial derivative in one grid, the table of how each number changes with each variable. The sum of the squared guesses is a bowl whose lowest point can be solved for exactly: that jump is the Gauss–Newton step. Far from the answer the straight-line guesses are poor, so the jump is shortened by blending it with a small step straight downhill, more when a step fails to lower the cost and less when it succeeds. Gauss–Newton steps blended this way are the Levenberg–Marquardt method ()Levenberg–Marquardt method: A solver for sums of squares that takes Gauss–Newton steps, jumping to the lowest point of a bowl built from straight-line guesses of each term, blended with a small step downhill that grows when a step fails and shrinks when it succeeds; it converges fast near the answer and safely far from it. Explained in §5.3 Solving it: sparse least squares. (Nocedal and Wright §10.3). TEB runs it in the graph-optimisation library g2o: 4 passes, each of which first resizes the band and then takes 5 Levenberg–Marquardt steps, with the clearance weight doubled after each pass (Rösmann et al. 2013; teb_local_planner).

6. Direct collocation

Key point

Turning a trajectory into a list of numbers is transcription. Direct collocation keeps the states and the controls at a row of points in time as unknowns and requires between neighbours that the change in state equals the trapezoid-rule area under its rate of change, so nothing is simulated and limits are simple; TEB and Apollo’s path optimiser are both of this kind.

TEB’s band of Section 5 turned the trajectory into poses and time steps, with the motion rule as a difference between neighbours. The refine-once drive of Section 9 needs the same step for any trajectory: a list of numbers that a solver can change.

Key point

A search, which explores every way round, gives a first guess on the right side; the optimiser then makes it smooth and fast.

Once the band is on one side, every small step keeps it there: moving it to the other side would mean passing through the person, where the cost is high.

Why the two methods need each other. Optimisation methods converge fast to a locally best answer, but they cannot find the best one overall unless they are given a suitable first guess; graph searches do not stop at local minima, but they choose only among the paths their graph contains; so a coarse search is used to give the optimiser its first guess (Paden et al. 2016 §IV-A). A search makes the discrete choice, which side of the person, and the optimiser makes the answer smooth and fast. TEB’s authors found the same: the band cannot cross to the other side of an obstacle, and which local optimum it reaches depends on how it starts (Rösmann et al. 2015; Rösmann et al. 2017). Real systems pair the two:

  • TEB, by default, also optimises up to 5 bands that start on different sides of the obstacles, found by a search, and keeps the cheapest (teb_local_planner; Rösmann et al. 2015);
  • Apollo’s EM planner first searches for a rough answer by dynamic programming, then smooths it with a quadratic program (Fan et al. 2018);
  • MoveIt can seed CHOMP with a sampling planner’s path; its guide suggests this because CHOMP “can fail if it gets stuck in local minima due to a bad initial guess” (MoveIt 2 docs).

8. The receding-horizon loop

Key point

Every 0.1 s the local planner measures the robot, shifts its last band to the robot and the new local goal, optimises it, and drives its first 0.1 s; the window slides forward with the robot. Each solve starts from the last answer, so it only has to correct it, and the person enters the plan in the cycle they appear.

The opening’s last problem was time: the person appeared 0.47 m in front of the robot, and the plan had to change within one cycle.

The idea. A driver does not plan the whole trip at once and then shut their eyes: they look a few seconds ahead, steer, and look again. The local planner does the same, every 0.1 s, on a receding horizon (Section 2.3). Each cycle has four steps (Figure 12):

  1. measure where the robot is and how fast it goes; it has driven 0.1 s along the last band;
  2. warm start: drop the poses the robot has passed, move the first pose to the robot and the last to the new local goal, 1.5 m further along the global plan (teb_local_planner);
  3. optimise: TEB’s 4 passes of 5 steps (Section 5.3);
  4. drive the band’s first 0.1 s, and start the next cycle.

Figure 12: The receding-horizon loop, cycle by cycle, from t = 2.2 s to 2.7 s; the person steps onto the route at 2.4 s. Each cycle has three frames. 1 measure: the robot has driven 0.1 s along last cycle’s band (pale). 2 warm start: the passed poses are dropped, the first pose is moved to the robot and the last to the new local goal (cross). 3 optimise: the new band (orange). Then the robot drives the band’s first 0.1 s

In the cycle at 2.4 s the warm start runs straight through the person, because it was planned before they appeared; one solve bends it 0.146 m clear (Figure 8). The next cycles start from that bent band and only adjust it. Over the whole drive the loop runs 81 cycles; the band holds between 3 and 12 poses, about 0.29 s apart, and shrinks to 3 poses as the robot reaches the door (notebook).

Why solve again every cycle. One trajectory answers for one start (Section 3.2). Each cycle starts from where the robot really is, so errors are corrected: the band of the first cycle asked for 0.649 m/s², the robot gave 0.5 m/s², and the next cycle planned from the slower robot (Section 5.2). Each cycle also uses the latest sensor data, so the person entered the plan in the cycle they appeared. TEB solves its problem “repeatedly at a rate faster than the robot control cycle” and feeds each answer back “to warm-start consecutive optimizations” (Rösmann et al. 2017). Solving an optimal control problem again at every step, from the measured state, and applying only the first part, is also the core of model predictive control, which the control chapters teach (Rösmann et al. 2021).

The loop keeps the robot moving with no plan longer than 1.5 m. When no new obstacle appears, one solve over the whole drive gives a faster drive.

9. Refine once: one solve for a whole drive

Key point

The same optimisation can run once, over a whole drive, started from a search’s path: from the A* route, collocation finds the least-time drive to the door pose, 6.18 s, against 11.81 s for driving the route by stopping and turning at each corner. A controller then follows it.

The opening’s route said where, not when; the local planner timed it 1.5 m at a time. With no person, one solve can time the whole route at once.

The drive. We take the A* route as the first guess, with the robot’s position in place of the start cell’s centre and the door pose in place of the goal cell’s centre, and ask trapezoidal collocation (Section 6) for the least time. For this problem we use a solver that keeps every constraint exactly, SciPy’s SLSQP, which solves a quadratic program at each step (Virtanen et al. 2020); methods of this kind find a locally best trajectory when the first guess is close to one (Lynch and Park §10.7). Every limit that can be used is used:

  • the acceleration sits at +0.5 m/s² until the speed reaches 0.6 m/s, and at −0.5 m/s² to brake;
  • the faster wheel sits at its 0.6 m/s limit while the robot cruises;
  • the disk passes the table’s corner at the clearance limit;
  • the turns happen at the two ends, where the speed is low and the wheels allow a fast turn.

A straight line from start to door pose, which ignores the table and every turn, could not be driven in less than 6.147 s; the refined drive needs only 0.034 s more (notebook). The local planner of Section 8, which plans only 1.5 m at a time, took 6.7 s with nobody in the way (notebook).

Checking between the knot points. The constraints hold at the knot points and halfway between them, and nowhere else is checked. Replaying the found controls in small simulation steps from the start, the drive ends 0.003 m from the door pose and its speed never passes 0.6 m/s (notebook). Collocation enforces the motion rules only at its points, so its error must be checked between them (Kelly 2017, §5.3).

After the solve. The robot then follows the drive with a controller that measures where it is and corrects the commands, the subject of feedback control; Apollo parks this way, from a Hybrid A* path, and MoveIt moves arms this way, from a sampling planner’s path (Section 10). The room now runs both loops of Section 2.3: re-planning every cycle round the person, and refining once over a whole drive. Real robots and cars run the same two loops at larger scale.

10. Real systems

Key point

TEB, Autoware, Apollo and MoveIt can all optimise trajectories with the ideas of this Note: TEB as one sparse least-squares band per cycle, Autoware and Apollo as a chain of quadratic programs, path first and speed second, and MoveIt as a sampling planner’s path refined once. Each pairs a search or a last answer with an optimiser.

The opening’s room is small; real robots and cars meet the same three problems, a plan without a clock, things the map lacks, and little time, at larger scale.

10.4 Apollo

Apollo’s public-road planner builds an 8 s trajectory in three steps (Apollo planning; Zhang et al. 2020; Fan et al. 2018):

  1. the reference line: the lane’s centre line, smoothed with the same second-difference cost, in its own loop every 50 ms;
  2. the path, a piecewise-jerk quadratic program (Section 6.3), every cycle;
  3. the speed, a search over station and time followed by a piecewise-jerk quadratic program, every cycle.

For parking it refines once: a Hybrid A* path is smoothed by quadratic programs and timed by a speed quadratic program, each step taking 0.035 s on average in its authors’ tests (Zhou et al. 2020).
