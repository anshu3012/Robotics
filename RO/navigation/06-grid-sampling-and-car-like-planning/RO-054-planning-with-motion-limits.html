1. Overview

Key point

The planners so far assume the robot can stop dead and turn on the spot at any moment, but at speed it can do neither: from 0.6 m/s it needs 0.36 m to stop, and at 0.5 m/s its tightest turn is a circle of 0.5 m. Planning with motion limits puts the speed into the state and builds every path from motions the robot can really drive.

All three join straight pieces at corners, and each assumes the robot can stop at any point and turn there on the spot. The assumption held while we only asked where the robot could be. The assumption breaks once the robot moves at a useful speed, because its motors have limits. We give our robot two:

  • each wheel turns at most fast enough for 0.6 m/s over the floor;
  • the forward speed changes by at most 0.5 m/s every second, the acceleration limit.

The opening question of this Note: how does the robot plan a drive to the door that it can follow at speed, stopping in time and turning only as tightly as its wheels allow? We answer it in two parts:

  • stopping: put the speed into what the planner tracks (Section 2), find the states from which a crash can no longer be avoided (Section 3), and search the fastest safe drive (Section 6);
  • turning: find where the robot can get to in a short time (Section 4), build paths from a few drivable motions (Section 5), learn the shortest drivable paths (Section 7), and grow an RRT out of drivable motions (Section 8).

2.2 The state, its motion equations and the stopping distance

For the drive along the line we track two numbers, and the robot changes them through one input:

  • s, the position along the line from the start (2, 1), in m;
  • v, the speed along the line, in m/s, from 0 to 0.6 m/s;
  • u, the acceleration the robot commands, in m/s², from −0.5 to 0.5.

The pair (s,v) is the state of the drive. A space of configurations and their speeds, such as these pairs, is the phase space ()Phase space: The space of a robot’s configurations together with their speeds, such as the pairs (position, speed) of a robot driving along a line; planning there lets a planner tell a robot that can still stop from one that cannot. Explained in §2.2 The state, its motion equations and the stopping distance. (Hauser ch.11; LaValle §14.1.3). The picture on the right of Figure 2 is the phase space of the straight drive, also called the phase plane because it has two numbers.

Speed grows at the rate u, and position at the rate v. In the dot notation ()Dot notation x˙: A dot over a quantity means its rate of change per second (its derivative with respect to time), such as x˙=0.433 m/s; models of motion are written with it. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model of RO-001, where a dot means “rate of change per second”:

s˙=v v˙=u

Equations like these, which give the rate of change of each number of the state from the state and the input, are called differential equations. For any robot we write them in one line (Hauser ch.11; MR §10.5), with:

  • x, the state, such as (s,v), in bold so that it is not confused with the x coordinate;
  • u, the input, such as the acceleration;
  • f, the function that gives the rates from the state and the input, here (v,u).
x˙=f(x,u)

The stopping distance. How much room does braking need? Braking at the full 0.5 m/s², the speed falls steadily to 0, so its average over the stop is half the starting speed. For robot B:

  • v, the speed when braking starts, 0.6 m/s;
  • a, the braking rate, the acceleration limit, 0.5 m/s²;
  • t, the time to stop, in s;
  • d, the distance travelled while stopping, in m.

Our numbers first, then the same steps in symbols:

t=0.6/0.5=1.2 s t=v/a d=(0.6/2)×1.2 d=(v/2)×t d=0.3×1.2=0.36 m d=v2/(2a)

Robot B has this much room left before the door:

3.117−2.8=0.317 m

less than the 0.36 m it needs (notebook). Robot A, at 0.3 m/s, needs only 0.09 m of the same 0.317 m.

2.3 Kinodynamic planning

The planners of chapter RO-04 searched the configuration space ()Configuration space (C-space): The set of all configurations of a robot, one point per placement of the whole robot, such as a box of (x,y,θ) whose top and bottom faces are the same headings for a floor robot; motion planning looks for paths through it. Explained in: Configuration Space and Degrees of Freedom, §2.2 The configuration space of the floor robot: they asked only where the robot could be, and assumed it could move between any two nearby configurations. With motion limits, the planner searches the state space ()State space: All the states a problem can be in, joined by the moves between them, such as the robot’s 76 cells or the arrangements of a Rubik’s cube; it is a graph, so planning becomes finding a path from the start state to a goal state. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §2.2 Any problem of states and moves is a graph, all the states the problem can be in, here configurations with their speeds, and may only use motions that the motion equations produce with allowed inputs. Planning a path that respects both the obstacles and these limits on how the state can change is kinodynamic planning ()Kinodynamic planning: Planning a motion that avoids obstacles and also obeys limits on how the state can change, x˙=f(x,u) with bounded inputs, such as a top speed and an acceleration limit; its plans can be driven as they are, without stops the robot cannot make. Explained in §2.3 Kinodynamic planning. (Hauser ch.11). The name was first used for planning with bounds on velocity and acceleration, as on our straight drive, and now covers any planning with such limits on how the state changes (LaValle §14.1.2.1).

Formally, with:

  • x(t), the state at time t, such as (s,v) for the straight drive;
  • u(t), the input at time t, inside its allowed set, such as u between −0.5 and 0.5 m/s²;
  • Xfree​, the states that touch no obstacle;
  • xI​, the start state, such as rest at the start: (0 m, 0 m/s);
  • XG​, the set of goal states, such as rest in front of the door;
  • T, the time the drive takes, in s.

a kinodynamic plan is a pair of curves x(t) and u(t), for t from 0 to T, such that the four lines below hold (Hauser ch.11). The sign ∈ reads “is in”; for example, robot A’s state, (2.8 m, 0.3 m/s), is in Xfree​.

x(t)∈Xfree​ for every t x˙(t)=f(x(t),u(t)) x(0)=xI​ x(T)∈XG​

The first line keeps the robot off obstacles; the second allows only motions the robot can make. The rest of the Note builds such plans. First we need to know which states Xfree​ really leaves out: robot B at 2.8 m touches nothing yet, but it is already lost.

Key point

Explained in §3.2 The region of inevitable collision.. The shape of the fan explains the planners’ trouble. A point 0.3 m to the robot’s left is near in distance but not in the fan at all: to reach it the robot must drive a loop. With a reverse gear, a car can also reach points beside it by shuffling forward and back, as in parallel parking, but even then it cannot move straight sideways (MR §13.3.2; LaValle §14.2.1.2). So a planner must build paths only from motions that stay inside such fans. The next section picks a few of them.

6. Searching the primitives systematically: lattice search

Key point

When every primitive changes the state by whole multiples of a fixed step, all reachable states fall on a regular grid, a lattice, and any graph search can explore it. On the straight drive, breadth-first search finds the fastest safe drive to the door: 7.0 s on a lattice with 0.5 s steps, against 6.2 s for the true best.

Section 3 told us where braking must start; it did not give the fastest drive that stops in front of the door. We search for it with primitives of the straight drive: every 0.5 s the robot accelerates at 0.5 m/s², coasts, or brakes at 0.5 m/s².

7.1 Dubins paths: forward only

The robot at (2, 1) facing 30° at 0.5 m/s must reach the pose in front of the door, (3.6, 3.5) facing 90°, without stopping. What is the shortest path it can drive, if we forget the furniture for a moment?

When our robot keeps moving forward at 0.5 m/s, it moves like a car that drives forward only and cannot turn tighter than a circle of radius 0.5 m. At rest our robot could spin on the spot instead, and its shortest path would then be turn, drive straight, turn (MR §13.3.3); at speed it cannot.

Dubins proved in 1957 that the shortest path is made of at most three pieces, each an arc of the tightest circle or a straight line (MR §13.3.3, Theorem 13.11). Write L for a full left turn, R for a full right turn and S for straight; a path is then a three-letter word (MR §13.3.3). Why only six words?

  1. Three letters from L, S and R give 27 words.
  2. Two equal letters in a row are one piece, so we drop them, which leaves 12.
  3. A word that starts or ends with S cannot be shortest: turning first and then going straight is never longer than going straight and turning later. Dubins’s theorem lists only words that turn at both ends (MR §13.3.3, Theorem 13.11). Six words are left: LSL, RSR, LSR, RSL, LRL and RLR.

8. Growing a tree of drivable motions: kinodynamic RRT

Key point

The kinodynamic RRT grows a tree whose every edge is a primitive run through the simulator: it samples a state, takes the nearest tree node, tries the primitives from it and keeps the one that comes closest, if its motion is free. Every path in the tree is drivable by construction; in the room it reaches the goal region in front of the door with 8 primitives, 3.0 m, at 0.5 m/s without a stop.

Three details explain the method’s behaviour:

  • A goal region, not a goal pose. Chains of fixed primitives almost never land exactly on one pose, so the tree aims at a small region around it (Hauser ch.11). A drivable steer, such as the Reeds-Shepp path of Section 7.3, can then join the last node to the exact pose.
  • Many throw-aways. Choosing the primitive that comes closest often points it into an obstacle; 147 of 221 tries were thrown away. The Modern Robotics book keeps instead the closest free one (MR §10.5.1.3).
  • The distance misleads. RO-038’s distance prefers a node beside the sample, which, as Section 7.2 showed, may be a whole loop away for a robot that cannot reverse. A distance closer to the true driving distance makes the tree grow better, and finding one is a known difficulty of kinodynamic RRT (MR §10.5; Hauser ch.11).

The kinodynamic RRT answers the turning part of the opening question: it plans in the room with only motions the robot can drive at speed. Its path is not the shortest, and random sampling gives a different path each run.
