1. Overview

Key point

A route from A* serves one start, and a robot that slips off it must search again. Numbering every free cell with its distance to the door, in one pass from the door, gives the robot a move from any cell: walking downhill on those numbers reaches the door by the shortest route, wherever the robot finds itself.

So far the robot can find its shortest route to the door on its map, the room’s occupancy grid ()Occupancy grid: A grid map that stores in each cell a number between 0 and 1, such as the probability that the cell is occupied, instead of a hard 0 or 1; the standard grid map of robotics, since sensor readings are uncertain. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §5.2 Bitmaps and occupancy grids of 0.5 m cells, 76 of them free. With moves to any of the 8 neighbouring cells, A* search ()A search:* Dijkstra’s algorithm with the open list sorted by f=g+h, the cost so far plus a heuristic guess of the cost left; with a consistent heuristic it returns the shortest route while expanding far fewer states (9 cells against 54 on the room grid). Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §4.5 The algorithm finds the 3.414 m route from the start cell S to the door’s cell G after taking 9 cells off its open list.

2. Numbering every cell from the door: the wavefront

Key point

Give the door’s cell 0, its free neighbours 1, their unnumbered neighbours 2, and so on. One breadth-first pass from the door numbers all 76 free cells with their fewest moves to the door; S gets 8.

The idea. The opening problem needs, for every cell the robot might land in, its distance to the door. Drop a stone into a pond at the door: the ripple reaches the cells next to the door first, then the cells next to those, ring after ring. If each cell writes down the ring that reached it, every cell knows how many moves it is from the door.

The standard term. Numbering the cells ring by ring outwards from the goal is wavefront propagation ()Wavefront propagation (wavefront planner): Numbering the free cells of a grid ring by ring outwards from the goal, each ring one move further, so every cell gets its fewest moves to the goal in one pass (8 for the room’s start cell); a breadth-first search from the goal, used when every move costs the same. Explained in §2. Numbering every cell from the door: the wavefront., and the program that does it is a wavefront planner (Choset slides, “The Wave-front Planner”; LaValle §8.2.3). Each ring is a wavefront: the cells first reached at the same number of moves.

The mechanism on our grid. We start with moves left, down, up and right only, the 4-connected grid ()4-connected grid: A grid made into a graph by joining each cell to the up to four cells that share a side with it (left, down, up, right); the simplest graph of a grid map, with every move the same length. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §2.1 Cells and moves: vertices and edges, whose cells are joined to the at most four cells that share a side with them. The steps (Choset slides; LaValle §8.2.3):

  1. Give G the number 0 and put it in a list.
  2. Take the oldest cell off the list.
  3. Give each free neighbour without a number the taken cell’s number plus 1, and add it to the end of the list.
  4. Go back to step 2 until the list is empty.

The list is a queue ()Queue (first in, first out): A list served in arrival order: new items join the back and the next item leaves from the front; as a search’s frontier it gives breadth-first search. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring, so these steps are breadth-first search ()Breadth-first search (BFS): A search whose frontier is a queue, so it expands vertices in rings of equal moves from the start; the first route it finds has the fewest moves, which is the shortest only when every move costs the same. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring run as a backward search ()Backward search: A search that starts at the goal and follows every move backwards until it reaches the start; it is cheaper when the goal’s side branches less, and run to the end it gives every state’s cost to the goal. Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §8.1 Backward search, from the door, and run until every cell is reached instead of stopping at S.

3. Which moves count: four or eight neighbours, moves or metres

Key point

The numbers mean what the moves and their costs say: on the 4-connected grid S is 8 moves (4.0 m) from the door, and on the 8-connected grid 6 moves, but walking down those numbers can take 3.828 m. Counting metres instead, with Dijkstra’s algorithm run from the door, gives S its true 3.414 m.

The wavefront of Section 2 numbers each cell with its moves to the door, but the robot drives in metres. Two choices fix what a number means:

  • which cells are neighbours: the 4-connected grid, or the 8-connected grid ()8-connected grid: A grid made into a graph by joining each cell to all 8 cells around it: 4 straight moves of one cell side and 4 diagonal moves of 1.414 cell sides (0.707 m for 0.5 m cells), usually without cutting the corner of a blocked cell; it gives shorter, less jagged routes than a 4-connected grid. Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §2.1 Diagonal moves: the 8-connected grid, which joins each cell to all 8 cells around it (MR §10.4) and, on our grid, never lets a diagonal move cut the corner of a blocked cell;
  • what a move costs: one move each, or its length, 0.5 m straight and 0.707 m diagonal.

Counting metres. To count metres, the pass must settle cells in order of their distance to the door, not of their moves, and that is Dijkstra’s algorithm ()Dijkstra’s algorithm: A search that always settles the vertex with the smallest distance from the start found so far, which is then final, and offers each of its neighbours the route through it, keeping the shorter; with every edge cost at least 0 it finds the shortest route to every vertex (3.414 m to the door on the room grid with diagonals). Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §3.2 The algorithm: settle the nearest, update its neighbours run from the door until its priority queue is empty. In the bottom panel of Figure 3, S’s number is 3.414 m, and the walk down the numbers is the 3.414 m route of A*.

The standard term. A cell’s number in metres, the length of the shortest route from that cell to the goal, is its cost-to-go ()Cost-to-go: The length of the shortest route from a state to the goal, such as 3.414 m from the room’s start cell to the door; stored for every state, it tells the robot how far it still has to go from wherever it is. Explained in §3. Which moves count: four or eight neighbours, moves or metres. (LaValle §2.3.1, §8.2.2). From here on we write it V(x):

  • x is a free cell;
  • V(x) is its cost-to-go, in m, such as 3.414 for S and 0 for G.

The cost-to-go of a cell is the true distance left that A*‘s heuristic ()Heuristic h: A cheap guess of the cost still to go from a state to the goal, such as 3.121 m from the start cell to the door; a search uses it to prefer states that look nearer the goal. Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §2. A guess of the distance left: the heuristic only guesses: for S, the octile guess is 3.121 m and the cost-to-go 3.414 m. Every free cell now has its distance to the door; the robot still needs a rule that turns the numbers into moves.

4. Walking downhill: the navigation function

Key point

From any cell, step to the neighbour with the smallest move plus cost-to-go V, the cell’s shortest distance to the door; each step lowers V by exactly the move’s length, so the walk is as long as the start cell’s number: 3.414 m from S, 3.207 m from the cell the slip left the robot in. A number map with this property is a navigation function.

4.1 The numbers as a valley

The opening problem asks for a move from every cell. Picture each cell’s cost-to-go as a height: the door’s cell at height 0, S at 3.414, the corner by the cabinet at 11.121. The room becomes a valley whose lowest point is the door, and a ball dropped anywhere rolls to the door.

The rule. A robot in a cell looks at its neighbours and steps to the one for which the move plus that neighbour’s number is smallest (LaValle §8.2.2, eq. 8.5). From S, with each move’s length and the neighbour’s cost-to-go (notebook):

The smallest, 3.414, is S’s own number, reached by two moves; we break the tie towards the neighbour with the smaller number, diagonally up and right.

Why the walk is the shortest route. S’s number is the length of its shortest route, and that route’s first move leads to a neighbour whose shortest route is the rest of it. With:

  • n is a neighbour of the cell x;
  • c(x,n) is the length of the move from x to n, 0.5 or 0.707 m;
  • min means the smallest of the numbers that follow, here one for every neighbour n;
  • n∗ is the neighbour the rule picks, diagonally up and right for S.

Our numbers first, then the same in symbols:

V(S)=min(3.414,3.414,3.828,…) V(x)=nmin​(c(x,n)+V(n)) V(S)=0.707+2.707=3.414 V(x)=c(x,n∗)+V(n∗)

So each step lowers the number by exactly the move just made. The walk from S passes through cells numbered 3.414, 2.707, 2.207, 1.707, 1.207, 0.5 and 0, and its moves add up to the drop from the first number to the last:

walk=3.414−0=3.414 m walk=V(start)−V(G)

The same telescoping holds from every cell, because every cell’s number came from such a neighbour. When every move costs the same, as on the 4-connected grid of Section 2, the move adds the same amount everywhere, and the rule becomes “step to the neighbour with the lowest number” (Choset slides, “The Wavefront, Now What?”; LaValle §8.2.2). On our 8-connected grid the lowest-number rule happens to give shortest routes from all 75 cells (notebook), but only the rule with the move added is guaranteed to.

The standard terms. A number map that guides the robot this way is a navigation function ()Navigation function: A number for every state that is 0 at the goal, infinite only where the goal cannot be reached, and lower at some neighbour of every other state, so it has one minimum, the goal; stepping to the neighbour with the smallest move plus number from any state leads to the goal, by the shortest route when the numbers are the cost-to-go. Explained in §4.2 One step at a time, from any cell. (LaValle §8.2.2). LaValle’s three conditions, on our map:

  1. the goal has the value 0: V(G)=0;
  2. a cell from which the goal cannot be reached has the value infinity, and only such a cell: our room has none;
  3. from every other cell, some move leads to a cell with a strictly lower value: true for all 75 cells (notebook), because every cell’s number was made from a neighbour’s number plus a move longer than 0.

The third condition says the valley of Figure 4 has one lowest point, the door, and no local minimum ()Local minimum: A point lower than everything around it, but not the lowest overall. Explained in: Gradient Descent from Scratch, §8. The shape of the loss function matters: no dent other than the goal where a walker could stop. A height map made by hand, such as a potential fieldPotential field (artificial potential field): A height over the robot’s C-space that is lowest at the goal and rises near obstacles, built as an attractive bowl plus a repulsive hill round each obstacle; the robot reaches the goal by stepping downhill from wherever it is, with no grid and no search, but can stop in a local minimum. Explained in: Potential Fields, §4. Why the robot stops: local minima, can have such dents. Walking downhill on a smooth valley is gradient descent ()Gradient descent: Finding the lowest point of a function by repeated small steps downhill. Explained in: Gradient Descent from Scratch, §1. Overview; the grid’s arrows are its version on cells (LaValle §8.2.2).

A navigation function with its downhill rule is a feedback plan ()Feedback plan: A plan that names an action for every state the robot can be in, not one sequence for one start, so the robot’s measured state picks the action and a slip only changes the input; a navigation function with its downhill rule is one. Explained in §4.2 One step at a time, from any cell.: a plan that names an action for every state the robot can be in, instead of one sequence of actions for one start (LaValle §8.2.2). The plan is called feedback because the robot’s measured cell, fed back from its sensors, picks the action, so a slip changes the input, not the plan. A route from A* is not a feedback plan: off the route it names no action.

A drawback: shortest routes hug the obstacles. A shortest route bends round every corner as tightly as the grid allows, so a robot that follows it passes close to the furniture (Freiburg path planning, slide 42). Our grid already blocks every cell that furniture touches, which keeps the robot’s centre a quarter of a cell from it along the grid lines. Two common remedies take more room, both by treating the obstacles as bigger than they are (Freiburg path planning, slide 43): drop the free cells next to obstacles before numbering, or give those cells a higher move cost, so the numbers prefer open floor.

The numbers so far came from Dijkstra’s algorithm, which settles cells strictly in order of distance using a priority queue. The next section computes the same numbers with no order at all, which is the form that carries over to robots between cells.

5. Value iteration: the same numbers without a queue

Key point

Start with 0 at the door and “unknown” everywhere else, then sweep over all cells, each taking the smallest move plus neighbour’s value, until a whole sweep changes nothing. On the room grid 8 sweeps give exactly Dijkstra’s numbers; no priority queue and no order are needed.

Why we need it. The numbers that sent the slipped robot to the door came from Dijkstra’s algorithm, which computes the cost-to-go, each cell’s shortest distance to the door, correctly because it settles cells in the right order, the nearest first. Keeping that order costs a priority queue, and some problems have no simple order at all, such as values stored between cells (Section 6) or moves whose result is uncertain. LaValle (§2.3.3) puts it this way: value iteration “extends easily to a much broader class of problems”, including continuous state spaces and planning with uncertain outcomes, while a Dijkstra-like order “may be inefficient or impossible in practice to maintain”.

The idea. Section 4.2 showed that each cell’s number equals the smallest move plus neighbour’s number. Turn that equation into a rule for fixing numbers: start from wrong numbers and let every cell repeatedly copy the best offer from its neighbours. Wrong numbers improve with every sweep, and once no cell can find a better offer, the numbers are the cost-to-go.

The standard term. Repeating, over all states, the update “value = smallest move cost plus the next state’s value” until the values stop changing is value iteration ()Value iteration: Computing every state’s cost-to-go by repeating, over all states, the update “value = smallest move cost plus the next state’s value” until no value changes; it needs no priority queue and no order, and it carries over to values between grid cells and to uncertain moves. Explained in §5. Value iteration: the same numbers without a queue. (LaValle §2.3.1, §2.3.2; Freiburg path planning, slide 40). The Reinforcement Learning (RL) Notes use the same update with rewards and with probabilities for where a move ends. With V(x), the cost-to-go of cell x in m, and the move lengths c(x,n) of Section 4.2:

V(x)←nmin​(c(x,n)+V(n))

The arrow means: replace the value on the left by the right side. Before the first sweep V(G)=0 and every other value is infinity, written ∞: not yet known.

The mechanism on our grid. One sweep visits the free cells row by row, from the door’s wall down, left to right in each row, and updates each in place, so a cell already uses the values its neighbours received earlier in the same sweep (Sutton and Barto §4.1, §4.4). Figure 6 shows the values after each sweep. Watch two things:

  • frame “sweep 1”: 49 cells get a value, S already its final 3.414, because each row passes its values to the row below within the same sweep;
  • frame “sweep 3”: four values drop (orange), such as the cell x 2.0 to 2.5 m under the top wall, from 1.914 to 1.500.

Why did the cell under the top wall first get 1.914? In sweep 2 it was visited before its right-hand neighbour had a value, so its best offer came diagonally, through a cell with 1.207:

0.707+1.207=1.914

In sweep 3 its right-hand neighbour already had 1.0, a shorter way in:

0.5+1.0=1.5

Sweep 8 changes nothing, and the 76 values equal Dijkstra’s (notebook).

Why the sweeps end at the right numbers. Two facts (LaValle §2.3.2):

  1. A value is never too small. Every finite value is the length of some real route to the door, built move by move from G’s 0, and no route is shorter than the shortest.
  2. After sweep k, every cell whose shortest route has at most k moves holds its exact value. The second cell of such a route has at most k−1 moves left, so it was exact after sweep k−1, and sweep k offers its value plus the first move.

The longest of the room’s shortest routes has 21 moves (notebook), so 21 sweeps always suffice and a 22nd confirms that nothing changes. Sweeps that each use only the values of the last sweep need exactly 22.

With updates in place, a value can travel many cells in one sweep, and here 8 sweeps suffice. According to Sutton and Barto (§4.1), the in-place version “usually converges faster”, and the order of the sweep matters.

The price. Value iteration does more work than Dijkstra’s algorithm: 8 sweeps of 75 cells are 600 updates, against 76 cells settled once each (notebook). Dijkstra’s algorithm is value iteration that only touches the cells whose values can still change, in the order that makes each touch final (LaValle §2.3.3).

Value iteration pays for doing without that order. The next section needs exactly that freedom, because its values sit between the cells.

6. Between the cells: dynamic programming with interpolation

Key point

The robot stands at (2, 1), not at a cell centre, and can drive at any angle. Storing values at the cell centres, blending the four around any point, and running value iteration with moves in 16 directions gives a cost-to-go between the cells; following it, the robot reaches the door’s cell after 3.564 m instead of 3.768 m by the grid’s cells.

6.1 The robot is between the cell centres

The problem, in numbers. The navigation function of Section 4, the door’s numbers the robot walks down, has a value at each cell centre and moves in 8 directions. But the robot stands at (2 m, 1 m), the corner of S, and can turn to any headingExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.4 Four ways to drive. To use the grid it must first drive 0.354 m to S’s centre and then follow 45° steps:

0.354+3.414=3.768 m

To plan from where the robot really is, we need values at points between the centres.

The idea. A point between four cell centres takes a blend of their four values, each counted more the closer the point is to it. Draw the four centres as the corners of a square and the point inside: each corner’s share is the area of the rectangle on the far side of the point from that corner, so the nearest corner, with the largest far rectangle, counts most.

The standard term. The blend is bilinear interpolation ()Bilinear interpolation: Estimating a value at a point between four grid points as their weighted average, each weight the area of the rectangle on the far side of the point (linear interpolation along x, then along y); it fills in values between cell centres. Explained in §6.1 The robot is between the cell centres. (LaValle §8.5.2.1, eq. 8.59): linear interpolation ()Linear interpolation: Placing a percentile between two neighbouring sorted values, in proportion to its position; the pandas default. Explained in: Outlier Detection with the Percentile Method and Winsorization, §2.2 From percentiles to limits, placing a value between two neighbours in proportion to the distance, done once along x and once along y.

The mechanism on our numbers. Figure 7 takes the end of one 0.5 m move of the robot, at 67.5° from (2, 1): the point p = (2.191, 1.462). The formal version. With:

  • α is how far across the square the point lies, from 0 at the left centres to 1 at the right ones, 0.8827 for p;
  • β is how far up, from 0 at the lower centres to 1 at the upper ones, 0.4239 for p;
  • V00​, V10​, V01​ and V11​ are the values at the bottom-left, bottom-right, top-left and top-right centres: 3.557, 3.338, 3.122 and 2.862.

Why it holds: checks at easy points. At the bottom-left centre itself, α=β=0, and the first weight is 1 and the others 0, so the blend gives that centre’s own value. At the middle of the square, α=β=0.5, and every weight is 0.25, so the blend is the plain average. And the four weights always add up to 1, so a blend of four equal values gives that value back:

0.0676+0.5085+0.0497+0.3742=1 (1−α+α)(1−β+β)=1

The robot can now read a value anywhere between four free centres. Where one of the four centres is a blocked cell, LaValle (§8.5.2.2) gives the point the value infinity, so no move ends there.

6.2 Value iteration with interpolation

The idea. Run the value iteration of Section 5 on the cell centres, but let each centre try moves in more than 8 directions. Each move ends between centres, and the bilinear blend of Section 6.1 gives the value at its end. The update is unchanged: the smallest move length plus value at the move’s end.

The standard term. Value iteration with values stored at sample points and blended in between is dynamic programming with interpolation ()Dynamic programming with interpolation: Value iteration with values stored only at sample points, such as cell centres, and blended in between, with moves in many directions; it gives a cost-to-go at every point, so a robot can plan from where it really stands and drive at any angle. Explained in §6.2 Value iteration with interpolation. (LaValle §8.5.2). The method is dynamic programming ()Dynamic programming: Solving a problem by storing the answer to each overlapping sub-problem the first time it is computed and looking it up afterwards, so no work is repeated; memoization is one form of it. Explained in: Memoization in Backpropagation, §3.3 The memoized version, reusing each stored value instead of recomputing routes, on a continuous floor instead of a list of cells.

The mechanism on our grid. Every free centre tries 16 moves of 0.5 m, one every 22.5°; a centre within 0.5 m of the door’s centre may also go straight there (notebook). With:

  • x is a cell centre, or later any point of the floor;
  • u is one of the 16 directions;
  • x+0.5u is the end of a 0.5 m move in direction u;
  • V(x+0.5u) is the bilinear blend there.
V(x)←umin​(0.5+V(x+0.5u))

The sweeps continue until no value changes by more than a millionth of a metre: 17 sweeps (notebook). S’s centre gets 3.338 m, below the grid’s 3.414 m, because S’s best move now leaves at 67.5°, between the grid’s 45° and 90°.

Why not Dijkstra’s algorithm here? A move’s value now depends on four stored values at once, and on where exactly the move ends, so no single neighbour settles a centre the way Dijkstra’s algorithm needs. LaValle (§8.5.2.3) shows a Dijkstra-like version can be built, with more bookkeeping; value iteration needs none.

7. A front that spreads in every direction: fast marching

Key point

Dijkstra’s distances on a grid follow only the 8 grid directions, so in some directions they come out about 8 percent too long, however small the cells. The fast marching method lets a front spread over the floor at 1 m/s and takes each cell’s arrival time from two neighbours at once, so the front can cross the grid at any angle; its error shrinks as the cells shrink.

7.1 Why the grid’s distances are too long

The cost-to-go of Section 3, each cell’s shortest distance to the door, counts only routes made of the 8 grid moves. An open floor shows the error without obstacles in the way: there, a point 4 m across and 2 m up from the start is, in a straight line:

42+22​=4.472 m

The 8-connected grid must reach it by diagonals and straight moves: diagonals that cover 2 m across and 2 m up, 2.828 m long, then 2 m straight:

2.828+2.0=4.828 m

The grid’s route is 8.0 percent too long. Smaller cells do not help: the same mix of diagonal and straight moves is needed at any cell size, so the error stays 8.0 percent (notebook). Sethian puts it this way: with Dijkstra’s method on a grid, “no matter how much you refine the mesh, you will still get stairstep patterns: you can never find the diagonal”.

In the room, on cells of 0.1 m, the 8-connected distance from the door’s cell centre to S’s centre is 3.180 m, while the shortest way round the blocked cells, a straight line to the corner of the blocked cells at (3, 3) and on to S’s centre, is 2.965 m (notebook).

7.2 Fast marching

The idea. Put a person with a watch at the centre of every cell, and let a front leave the door’s cell at 1 m/s in every direction, like a ripple on open water (Sethian). Each person writes down the time the front passes. The recorded time is the cell’s cost-to-go in seconds, and because the front moves through the open floor rather than along grid moves, it can arrive from any angle.

A program cannot let a front move, so it builds the times outwards from the door, smallest first, like the oil-depot fire of Dijkstra’s algorithmDijkstra’s algorithm: A search that always settles the vertex with the smallest distance from the start found so far, which is then final, and offers each of its neighbours the route through it, keeping the shorter; with every edge cost at least 0 it finds the shortest route to every vertex (3.414 m to the door on the room grid with diagonals). Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §3.1 The fire picture (Sethian: “very closely related to Dijkstra’s method”). The one change: a cell’s time is worked out from two known neighbours at once, one across and one above or below, so the front can arrive between them on a slant.

The standard term. The method is the fast marching method ()Fast marching method: Dijkstra’s loop run on a grid with an update that takes a cell’s arrival time from two neighbours at once, one across and one up or down, so a front spreading from the goal can cross the grid at any angle; its distances get closer to the true ones as the cells shrink, unlike 8-connected Dijkstra’s. Explained in §7.2 Fast marching. (Sethian 1996). Its loop is Dijkstra’s: the cell with the smallest tentative time is accepted and never changed, and its neighbours’ tentative times are recomputed (Wikipedia, Fast marching method). Only the update differs.

T=2a+b+2h2−(a−b)2​​

7.3 The Eikonal equation

What equation does the update solve? Picture the arrival time T, the cost-to-go of Figure 4 measured in seconds, as a height over the floor: the valley of Figure 4, made smooth. The fronts of Figure 9 are its level lines, the points at one height, seen from above, as the rings of Figure 2 were: Figure 9 is a contour plot ()Contour plot: A map of a surface seen from above, with lines joining points of equal height, so a 3D shape such as a loss bowl can be drawn and read on flat paper. Explained in: Partial Derivatives and Gradients, §1.1 Reading a contour map of the arrival-time surface.

Why the steepest slope is 1/F. The left half of Figure 10 shows the front at time T and a short time Δt later. The front moves at speed F, straight across itself, so the two lines are FΔt apart. Crossing that gap, the arrival time climbs by Δt:

slope across the front=FΔtΔt​ =F1​

Across the front is the steepest way up, because along the front T does not change at all. The steepest slope of a function and its direction make its gradientExplained in: Partial Derivatives and Gradients, §4.3 The slope in any direction ∇T ()Nabla (∇): The upside-down triangle ∇, read “nabla” or “grad”; ∇f means the gradient of f, the vector that collects all its partial derivatives. Explained in: Partial Derivatives and Gradients, §4.1 Collecting the partial derivatives: the arrow of its slopes along x and along y, whose length, written with bars, ∣∇T∣, is the steepest slope ()Steepest ascent: The direction in which f increases fastest: the direction of the gradient; gradient descent steps the opposite way to go downhill fastest. Explained in: Partial Derivatives and Gradients, §4.3 The slope in any direction. So:

∣∇T∣=F1​

The standard term. The arrival time’s steepest slope equal to one over the front’s speed everywhere, with T=0 on the goal, is the Eikonal equation ()Eikonal equation: The rule that the arrival time T of a front moving at speed F has steepest slope 1/F everywhere, written as the length of its gradient equals 1/F, with T = 0 where the front starts, the goal; its solution with F = 1 is the shortest distance, and fast marching solves its grid form. Explained in §7.3 The Eikonal equation. (Wikipedia, Eikonal equation; Sethian). With F=1 m/s, T in seconds equals the distance to the goal in metres, so the Eikonal equation describes the true shortest distance.

A check on the open floor. From the start of Section 7.1, the arrival time at a point x m across and y m up is its straight-line distance, divided by F=1. At the point (4, 2), T=4.472 s, the square root of 20, and the gradient points straight away from the start, with slope 4 / 4.472 along x and 2 / 4.472 along y:

∣∇T∣2=2042​+2022​ =0.8+0.2=1

On the grid. The right half of Figure 10 shows the update’s version: the slope along x is (T−a)/h, from the neighbour across; the slope along y is (T−b)/h, from the neighbour above or below. By Pythagoras’ rule, their squares add up to the square of the steepest slope:

(hT−a​)2+(hT−b​)2=F21​

Solving this for T gives the update of Section 7.2, and the 45° check there is this equation with a=b.

Fast marching on a 4-connected stencil, using only the cells left, right, above and below, thus gives distances in every direction: the grid’s connectivity no longer limits the directions the front can take.
