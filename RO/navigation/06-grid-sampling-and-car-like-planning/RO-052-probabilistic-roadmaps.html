1. Overview

Key point

A probabilistic roadmap scatters random free configurations over C-space, joins nearby ones by checked straight edges, and searches the resulting graph. Built once, it answers every trip in an unchanged room for a few hundred checks each; the price is that a missed route is only unlikely, not impossible, and tight gaps need smarter sampling.

  • tight gaps, and two ways of sampling that find them (Section 6);
  • a smaller graph that covers the room just as well (Section 7);

2. Building a roadmap from random samples

Key point

Build a graph of the free space once, in two phases: draw random configurations and keep the free ones as milestones, then join each milestone to its 10 nearest by straight edges that pass a collision check. A trip only joins its two ends to the graph and runs A*.

2.1 Why sample instead of building the free space

The four trips share one room, so the robot should describe the room once and reuse the description. Describing it exactly means building the obstacle region, which is costly for real robots (why planners do not build the obstacle regionExplained in: Obstacles in C-space, §4.3 Why planners do not build the obstacle region). A grid avoids that, but its size grows with the number of joints. With 10 values for each joint:

  • d is the number of joints, the dimension of the C-space.
cells=10d d=2:102=100 d=3:103=1,000 d=7:107=10,000,000

(KAIST PRM slides). Ten million cells for a 7-joint arm, each needing a collision check, is the curse of dimensionality ()Curse of dimensionality: The problems that appear when data has too many dimensions: lower performance and more computation. Explained in: Curse of Dimensionality, §2. What the curse of dimensionality is, the work and data that grow with the number of dimensions, here in C-space (MR §10.5).

The fix is the idea a road map uses. A road atlas does not draw every field of the country; it draws a network of roads, and any trip goes from home to the nearest road, along roads, and off at the nearest road to the destination (MR §10.3). We build such a network inside the free space from random configurations, checked one at a time by collision detection ()Collision detection: Deciding, yes or no, whether two bodies overlap, such as the robot’s disk and the table; planners call it thousands of times instead of building the obstacle region. Explained in: Collision Checking and Nearest Neighbours in C-space, §2.1 The yes-or-no question and what it costs, so the obstacle region is never built (MR §10.5.2). Like the RRT, the method is a sampling-based planner ()Sampling-based planner: A motion planner that never builds the obstacle region or a grid, but draws random configurations and tests them with collision checks, joining the free ones into a tree or graph; it works in C-spaces with many dimensions, where grids are far too large. Explained in: Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees (RRT), §2.1 One step: sample, nearest node, steer, check: random configurations and collision checks take the place of a grid (MR §10.5). How many samples it needs depends on how much of the free space one configuration can see along straight lines, and that share does not usually fall with every added dimension the way a grid’s cell count rises (MR §10.5.2).

kN=10×100=1,000 tries

A pair in which each milestone picks the other is tried only once. If every pair were picked both ways, the count would halve:

kN/2=1,000/2=500

So the distinct edges tried lie between 500 and 1,000; our room gave 573, of which 525 were free (notebook). The pseudocode (MR §10.5.2, Algorithm 10.4), with R the roadmap:

  1. for each of N times: draw a free configuration q and add it to R;
  2. for each milestone q: for each q′ among the k nearest milestones of q: if the local planner finds the segment from q to q′ free and the edge is not in R yet, add it to R.

3. Many trips on one roadmap: single-query and multi-query planners

Key point

A roadmap built once answers every trip in an unchanged room: the four trips cost 7,934 checks for the build plus 842 for the trips, against 32,578 if a new roadmap were built for each. A planner that builds such a reusable graph is a multi-query planner; one that solves each trip from nothing, like the RRT, is a single-query planner.

The opening problem was trips 2 to 4: a tree grown for trip 1 serves no other trip. The roadmap of Section 2, its milestones (the kept random configurations) and checked edges, holds no start or goal, so any trip can use it. When the room changes. The investment pays only while the room stays the same. 4. What makes a graph a roadmap: accessibility and connectivity

Key point

A graph serves every trip only if two things hold. Accessibility: from any free configuration, a simple free path reaches the graph. Connectivity: two configurations joined in free space are joined in the graph, so the graph has one piece for each piece of free space. With 10 milestones our roadmap breaks the second rule and trips 2 and 4 fail; from 20 milestones on it is one piece and every trip is found.

With both, every trip is three short steps: from the start onto the roadmap, along the roadmap, and off to the goal (MR §10.3). In Figure 4, from 20 milestones on, the roadmap is one piece and 99.9 percent or more of the free floor reaches it, and all four trips are found.

A probabilistic roadmap is called probabilistic because random milestones meet the two conditions only with some probability: as more milestones are added, the chance that the roadmap joins every start and goal that the free space joins goes to 1 (Hauser ch.10, “Analysis of visibility and performance”). With 20 milestones, seed 0 met them; another seed might not. How likely a wrong “no route” is, is the subject of the next section.

5. Can a planner miss a route? Completeness

Key point

A complete planner always finds a route if one exists and says so if none does; a resolution-complete planner does this down to the size of its grid; a probabilistically complete planner, such as the PRM, finds an existing route with a chance that rises to 1 as it samples more. A route the PRM returns is always free; its “no route” can be wrong.

5.1 Complete and resolution complete

Section 4 ended with the roadmap of 10 milestones answering “no route” for trip 2, which has one. So “no route” from a planner may be false, and the planners we know differ in how false it can be. Three grades are used (MR §10.1; KAIST PRM slides):

  1. A planner is complete ()Complete planner: A planner that always finds a route when one exists and reports failure when none exists; it needs an exact description of the free space, so it is practical only for simple problems. Explained in §5.1 Complete and resolution complete. if it always finds a route when one exists and reports failure when none exists (MR §10.1).
  2. A planner is resolution complete ()Resolution complete: Complete down to the resolution of a grid: the planner finds every route that exists through its grid, but may answer no route where only a finer grid would find one. Explained in §5.1 Complete and resolution complete. if it does so at the resolution of its grid: it finds every route that exists through the grid, and may miss a route that needs a finer grid (MR §10.1).
  3. A planner is probabilistically complete ()Probabilistically complete: Finding an existing route with a chance that goes to 1 as the planner samples more; a returned route is always free, but an answer of no route can be wrong. Explained in §5.2 Probabilistically complete. if the chance that it finds an existing route goes to 1 as its planning time, here its number of samples, grows (MR §10.1).

Complete. 6. Narrow passages and smarter sampling

Key point

A narrow passage is a thin part of free space that a route must cross; uniform samples rarely land in it, so the roadmap splits on either side. Gaussian sampling keeps samples next to obstacles, and the bridge test keeps the midpoint of two colliding samples, which lands in thin gaps; with 50 milestones they find the tray robot’s trip in 86 and 93 percent of runs, against 32 percent for uniform sampling.

6.2 Gaussian sampling and the bridge test

Narrow passages always lie next to obstacles, and the thinnest lie between two obstacles close together (Hauser ch.10; LaValle §5.6.3). Two ways of drawing samples use these facts, each from a pair of draws (Hauser ch.10; LaValle §5.6.3; WPI L11):

  1. Gaussian sampling ()Gaussian sampling: Drawing a uniform configuration and a second one near it (normal offsets) and keeping the free one only if exactly one of the two is free, so milestones gather next to obstacles, where narrow passages lie.   2. The bridge test ()Bridge test: Keeping the midpoint of two nearby configurations that both collide, if the midpoint is free, so milestones land in thin gaps between obstacles, where narrow passages are. Why they land in the right places. A Gaussian pair with one end free and one in collision straddles the edge of an obstacle, so the kept end lies within about 0.1 m of an obstacle (Hauser ch.10). 7. The visibility roadmap: guards and connectors

Key point

The visibility roadmap keeps a sample only if it sees no kept guard (then it becomes a guard) or it sees guards of two pieces of the roadmap (then it becomes a connector joining them). In our room, 5 guards and 4 connectors, 9 vertices and 8 edges, cover the free floor that took 100 milestones and 525 edges before.

The roadmap of Section 3 has 100 milestones (kept random configurations) and 525 checked edges, and most of them repeat each other: a milestone in the middle of the open floor sees the same floor as its neighbours. A smaller roadmap is quicker to search and to store, which matters when it is built once and queried many times (LaValle §5.6.2).

The visibility roadmap ()Visibility roadmap: A probabilistic roadmap that keeps a sample only if it sees no guard (it becomes a guard) or sees guards of two pieces (it becomes a connector), giving a small roadmap that covers the free space. Explained in §7. The visibility roadmap: guards and connectors. keeps a free sample only if it adds something, by one of two rules, and discards the rest (LaValle §5.6.2; KAIST PRM slides):

  1. a sample that sees no guard ()Guard (visibility roadmap): A vertex of a visibility roadmap that sees no other guard along a free straight segment; guards are added to cover free space no guard sees yet. Explained in §7. The visibility roadmap: guards and connectors., no kept vertex of the first kind, along a free straight segment becomes a guard: it adds floor that no guard sees yet;
  2. a sample that sees guards from two different pieces of the roadmap becomes a connector ()Connector (visibility roadmap): A vertex of a visibility roadmap that sees guards of two different pieces of the roadmap and is joined to both, so it merges the pieces. Explained in §7. The visibility roadmap: guards and connectors., joined by edges to one guard of each piece: it joins the pieces;
  3. any other free sample sees guards of one piece only, adds nothing, and is discarded.

Every sample is tested against every guard, however far away, because a guard’s view is everything it sees (LaValle §5.6.2). Figure 10 runs the rules on 300 draws, seed 0. The small roadmap has two costs (LaValle §5.6.2; KAIST PRM slides):

  • Building it costs more per sample: each sample is tested against every guard, so 300 draws took 10,513 configuration checks, against 7,934 for the 100-milestone roadmap.
  • Its routes can be long: trip 2 is 9.047 m here against 3.587 m on the 100-milestone roadmap, because only 9 vertices are there to route through.

Narrow passages stay as hard for it as for the basic roadmap, since a guard or connector must still land there (KAIST PRM slides). One name clash: some books call the graph of obstacle corners that see each other a visibility roadmap too (MR §10.3, Exercise 10.7); the visibility roadmap of this section is the guard-and-connector graph of LaValle §5.6.2. Both roadmaps have spent most of their checks on edges, which the last section avoids.

8. Lazy collision checking

Key point

Most edges of a roadmap are never used by any route, yet each costs dozens of configuration checks. Lazy collision checking joins milestones without checking the edges, searches, and checks only the edges of the route found, deleting any that collide and searching again: trip 2 needs 11 edge checks, 129 configuration checks, against 7,954 when every edge is checked first.

The visibility roadmap saved milestones; the edges still cost the most. Building the roadmap of Section 2 took 7,745 configuration checks for its edges, against 189 for its milestones (its random free configurations), yet trip 2’s route uses 3 of its 525 edges. Checking an edge is the main cost of a roadmap, especially for detailed robots, where every configuration check tests many bodies and pairs of links (Hauser ch.10, “Lazy collision checking in PRMs”).

Lazy collision checking ()Lazy collision checking: Delaying an edge’s collision check until a candidate route uses it, deleting edges found to collide and searching again; it saves the checks of the many edges no route uses. Explained in §8. Lazy collision checking. delays an edge’s check until a route wants to use it (Hauser ch.10):

  1. join every milestone, the start and the goal to their 10 nearest, assuming every edge is free;
  2. search for a route with A*; if there is none, report failure;
  3. check the route’s edges, in order from the start;
  4. if an edge collides, delete it from the graph and go back to step 2;
  5. if every edge is free, return the route.

The bet is that an edge between two free milestones is usually free too (Hauser ch.10). Figure 11 runs trip 2 on the 100 milestones of Section 2. Watch the orange candidate routes: an edge that passes the check turns green; an edge that cuts the grown table turns red and is deleted, and A* searches again.

Lazy checking needed 5 searches and 11 edge checks, of which 4 collided; it checked 129 configurations and found a route of 3.579 m (notebook). Checking every edge first, as in Section 2, costs 7,745 checks for the edges plus 209 for trip 2’s ends:

7,745+209=7,954 129/7,954=0.016

So the lazy route cost 1.6 percent of the checks. The price is extra searches, cheap in a graph of 100 milestones (Hauser ch.10).
