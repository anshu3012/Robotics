1. Overview

Key point

Across a city, a robot or a car plans on a graph of roads whose edges cost travel time, and the search loop is A* as before. What decides how much of the map the search looks at is the heuristic: on a Chicago street graph of 1628 intersections, one trip takes 1526 intersections off the open list with no heuristic, 1229 with the straight line at the top speed and 108 with 16 landmarks, while a contraction hierarchy, which adds shortcuts before any search, takes 159.

The robot can already plan its way to the door of its room. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list, the cells reached but not yet expanded, where Dijkstra’s algorithm takes 54, because a heuristic ()Heuristic h: A cheap guess of the cost still to go from a state to the goal, such as 3.121 m from the start cell to the door; a search uses it to prefer states that look nearer the goal. Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §2. A guess of the distance left: the heuristic, a cheap guess of the distance left, points it at the door.

Past the door, the trips get longer. A delivery robot, or a car, must cross a city: from a street corner in the south-west of the map of Figure 1 to one near the lake shore in the north-east, 4.6 km apart as the crow flies. Three things break:

  • Size. The map covers 4.9 km by 5.0 km; cut into 0.5 m cells like the room, it would be 96 million cells, against the room’s 112.
  • Roads. A car may not drive across a block. The car keeps to the streets, in the allowed direction, and in the right lane.
  • Time. A trip is judged by how long it takes, not how long it is: the fastest route is 7047 m long and takes 492.8 s, while the shortest, 6717 m, takes 573.1 s (notebook).

Roads fix the size: the map shrinks to its 1628 intersections and the streets between them. The search effort is what is left. The fourth adds shortcuts to the graph before any search and takes 159. This Note builds the four, on a small town first and then on the Chicago map:

  • why route planning is a level of its own, above the planners of the room (Section 2);
  • the road network as a graph of intersections and one-way edges, and of lanes (Section 3);
  • travel time as the cost of a road (Section 4);
  • the first heuristic in seconds, the straight line at the top speed, and why it is loose (Section 5);
  • a tight heuristic from stored travel times to a few landmarks (Section 6);

2. Why route planning is its own level

Key point

A car’s drive cannot be planned as one problem from the destination down to the steering angle. Planning is split into levels; the top one, route planning, picks the roads and lanes on a map of the road network and leaves every detail of the motion to the levels below.

The first thing that breaks is size. The room’s planners worked on every free cell and every heading; across the city, the same detail means 96 million cells, other cars, traffic lights and lane markings, for a drive of 8 minutes.

The idea. A taxi driver does not plan every turn of the steering wheel before leaving. First the driver decides the roads: west on this street, onto the expressway, off at the river. Only while driving does the driver decide when to change lane, whether to stop at a light, and how hard to brake. Each decision uses the one above it and adds detail.

Planning for self-driving cars is split the same way, because driving is hard to model as a single optimisation problem (Tübingen L12 slides; Paden et al. 2016). Figure 2 shows the levels.

Figure 2: The planning levels of a self-driving car, from the destination at the top to the steering, throttle and brake at the bottom. The route planner turns the destination and a map of the road network into a route; the behaviour layer picks a manoeuvre (follow the lane, stop, yield, change lane) from what the car sees; the motion planner turns the manoeuvre into a path or trajectory; the controller follows it. Each level works coarser than the one below it

The standard term. Finding the best way from where the vehicle is to the destination on a map of the road network, by time or by distance, is route planning ()Route planning (mission planning): Finding the best way from where a vehicle is to its destination on a map of the road network, by travel time or by distance, such as the 492.8 s route across the Chicago map; the top level of planning, which picks the roads and lanes and leaves the motion to the levels below. Explained in §2. Why route planning is its own level., also called mission planning (Tübingen L12 slides). Route planning is the top level of Figure 2:

  1. the route planner chooses the roads and lanes;
  2. the behaviour layer chooses manoeuvres, such as stopping at a stop line or changing lane;
  3. the motion planner finds a path or a trajectory that carries out the manoeuvre, with the planners of this chapter;
  4. the controller turns it into steering, throttle and brake.

Earlier levels work with coarser pictures of the world (Tübingen L12 slides). A route planner never sees a parked car; the motion planner never sees the whole city.

Robots do the same. A delivery robot in a building can plan on a fixed graph of waypoints, such as corridor junctions and doors, the room’s door among them, and leave the last few metres to a free-space planner such as A* on the room’s grid (Nav2 Route Server). A fixed graph of points a robot may travel between, made by hand or by a program from the map, is a route graph ()Route graph: A fixed graph of points a robot may travel between, such as corridor junctions and doors, made by hand or by a program from the map; a robot plans its long trips on it and leaves the last metres to a free-space planner. Explained in §2. Why route planning is its own level. (Nav2 Route Server). On route graphs, Dijkstra’s algorithm alone is fast enough: 0.232 ms on average on a graph of 10,000 nodes and 44 ms on a million (Nav2 Route Server). A road graph of Western Europe has 18 million intersections (Geisberger et al. 2008), and there the search effort matters.

The route planner needs the road network as a graph; the next section builds one.

3. Roads as a graph

Key point

Intersections are the vertices and road segments the edges of a road graph, and a one-way street is an edge that goes one way only, so road graphs are directed. When the route must say which lane to be in, each lane segment becomes a vertex of its own, joined to the next segment and to the lanes beside it.

Section 2 left the route planner a map of the road network; this section turns it into the graph ()Graph: A set of vertices (points) joined by edges (lines), such as the room’s 76 free cells joined by 114 moves; it keeps only which places connect directly, so one search method works for maps, puzzles and any problem of states and moves. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §2.1 Cells and moves: vertices and edges, points joined by lines, that a search needs. A road map is the next step up from the room, with roads instead of free floor, so we leave the room for a town.

3.2 Lane graphs

A road graph has one vertex per intersection, so it cannot say which lane the car should be in. The lane matters before a turn. In Figure 5 a car in the right lane that must turn left at the junction has to change to the left lane first, and it cannot do so at the last metre.

The idea. Cut every lane into short pieces and make each piece a vertex. Join a piece to the piece that follows it in the same lane, and to the pieces beside it that the car may change into.

Figure 5: A lane graph on a two-lane road heading east. Each lane is cut into pieces, the vertices a1 and a2 (right lane), b1 and b2 (left lane), s (straight on after the junction) and l (the left turn). Blue arrows join a piece to the next piece in the same lane; orange dashed arrows join pieces a car may change between, at a penalty. To turn left from a1, the route must cross to the left lane: a1, b1, b2, l or a1, a2, b2, l

The standard terms. One piece of one lane, with its left and right borders, is a lanelet ()Lanelet: One short piece of one lane, given by its left and right borders, as used by the Lanelet2 map format; the vertex of a lane graph. Explained in §3.2 Lane graphs. (Lanelet2 routing). A graph whose vertices are lanelets is a lane graph ()Lane graph: A graph whose vertices are lane segments (lanelets), joined to the next segment in the same lane and to the neighbouring lanes a vehicle may change into; it lets a route say which lane to be in, for example before a left turn. Explained in §3.2 Lane graphs.. Its edges are of two kinds (Lanelet2 routing):

  • succeeding: from a lanelet to the next one in the same lane, such as a1 to a2;
  • left and right: to a neighbouring lanelet the car may change into, such as a1 to b1; a lane change adds a penalty to the cost, so a route changes lane only when that pays.

Lanes beside each other that may not be changed between, such as two lanes with a solid line between them, get no lane-change edge. The graph is also built for one kind of road user: a bicycle’s lane graph includes cycle lanes that a car’s does not (Lanelet2 routing).

A route planner that runs on a lane graph returns a list of lane IDs; the Autoware software for self-driving cars groups them into sections of road, each with the lanes the car may change between (Autoware mission planner).

The graph is ready, at the level of roads or of lanes. Its edges still need a cost, and length is not the one a driver cares about.

4. Travel time as the edge cost

Key point

The cost of a road is the time it takes: its length divided by its speed limit. With that cost, the fastest route in the town runs 3300 m by the bypass in 197.1 s, while the shortest, 2500 m through the old town, takes 252.0 s.

Section 3 left the edges of the road graph without a cost. In the room, a move cost its length, and the weighted graph ()Weighted graph (edge cost): A graph in which every edge carries a number, its cost, such as a move’s length in metres (0.5 m straight, 0.707 m diagonal); the cost of a path is the sum of its edge costs, so the cheapest route need not have the fewest edges. Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §2.2 Edge costs: the fewest moves is not the shortest of the room’s grid gave each edge its length in metres. On roads, the same choice picks the wrong route.

The problem on numbers. From S to T, the straight way through the old town is 2500 m; the way round by the bypass is 3300 m. By length, the old town wins. But the old town’s streets allow 30 km/h and the bypass 80 km/h.

The idea. A driver judges a route by the time it takes. Driving a street at its speed limit takes its length divided by that speed, so each edge gets that time as its cost (Rhodes COMP 372; Tübingen L12 slides). A speed in km/h becomes m/s by dividing by 3.6, because 1 km is 1000 m and 1 h is 3600 s. For the bypass from N to M, 1500 m at 80 km/h:

80 km/h=80/3.6 =22.22 m/s time=1500/22.22 =67.5 s

With:

  • ℓ, the length of the street, in m (1500 m from N to M);
  • v, its speed limit, in m/s (22.22 m/s);
  • c, the cost of its edge, in s (67.5 s).
c=ℓ/v

5. A heuristic in seconds: the straight line at the top speed

Key point

Divide the straight-line distance to the goal by the highest speed limit on the map: no route can be shorter than the straight line or faster than the top speed, so the guess never overestimates. But most roads are much slower than the top speed, so the guess is loose: in Chicago it averages 0.41 of the true time, and A* still takes off 1229 of the trip’s intersections.

Section 4 left A* without a heuristic for travel time. The room’s straight-line and octile distances are in metres; the costs are now in seconds, and A* adds the two, so a heuristic in metres cannot be used.

5.1 The bound and why it holds

The idea. The time left from an intersection is at least the time it would take to drive the straight line to the goal at the fastest speed anywhere on the map. For the town, the fastest speed is the bypass’s 80 km/h, 22.22 m/s. From S, 2500 m from T as the crow flies:

h(S)=2500/22.22 =112.5 s

With:

  • u, an intersection;
  • δ(u), the straight-line distance from u to the goal T, in m (2500 m from S);
  • vmax​, the highest speed limit on the map, in m/s (22.22 m/s in the town);
  • h(u), the heuristic at u, in s (112.5 s at S).
h(u)=δ(u)/vmax​

This heuristic is the straight-line distance over the top speed (Freiburg L3; Rhodes COMP 372).

Why it never overestimates. Take the fastest route from S, five streets of 500, 400, 1500, 400 and 500 m. Driving each street at the top speed instead of its own limit can only make it faster, and no route is shorter than the straight line:

time=36+28.8+67.5+28.8+36 =197.1 ≥3300/22.22 =148.5 ≥2500/22.22 =112.5 =h(S)

The same steps for any route from u to the goal, with:

  • ℓi​, the length of street i of the route, in m;
  • vi​, its speed limit, in m/s, never above vmax​;
  • ∑i​, the sum over the streets of the route (five terms for the route from S, one per street).
time=i∑​ℓi​/vi​ ≥i∑​ℓi​/vmax​ ≥δ(u)/vmax​ =h(u)

The second line holds because every vi​ is at most vmax​; the third because the streets of a route together are never shorter than the straight line between its ends. So the heuristic is admissible ()Admissible heuristic: A heuristic that is never more than the true cost left from any state, h≤h∗; it makes f a lower bound on every route through a state, so the first route A* takes off the open list is the shortest. Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §5.1 A guess that never overestimates: it never overestimates, and A* returns the fastest route. Rhodes College’s Memphis exercise uses the same bound with 65 miles per hour, the highest speed limit of any Memphis road (Rhodes COMP 372).

6. Landmarks: lower bounds from stored travel times

Key point

Store, once, the exact travel times between a few landmark vertices and every other vertex; since a detour through the goal is never faster than the fastest way to a landmark, the stored times give a lower bound on the time left that knows about the roads. With 16 landmarks, A* takes off 108 of the Chicago trip’s intersections instead of 1229.

The straight line of Section 5 knows nothing about the roads: at B it guessed 67.5 s where the true time is 156.0 s. A better guess needs the roads, but the exact time left differs for every goal: computing it before each search is too slow, and storing it for every pair of vertices takes too much memory (Red Blob, differential heuristics).

6.1 The triangle inequality on travel times

The idea. Pick one vertex, L at the east edge of the town, and compute the fastest time from every vertex to it, once, with one Dijkstra run backwards from L. Then use it for any goal. From B, one way to reach L is to drive to T first and then on to L. That way can never beat the fastest way from B to L (Figure 9). With the stored times, 193.8 s from B to L and 46.8 s from T to L (notebook):

time(B,T)+46.8≥193.8 time(B,T)≥193.8−46.8 =147.0 s

Figure 9: The landmark bound at B. The fastest times from B to the landmark L, 193.8 s, and from T to L, 46.8 s, are stored (purple). The fastest time from B to the goal T is what the search does not know (blue, dashed; the true value is 156.0 s). Driving B to T to L is one way of reaching L, so it is never faster than the fastest way: time(B, T) + 46.8 ≥ 193.8, so time(B, T) ≥ 147.0 s

The guess at B rises from 67.5 s to 147.0 s; the truth is 156.0 s. Explained in: Collision Checking and Nearest Neighbours in C-space, §7.1 The rules a distance must follow, a detour through a third point is never shorter than going straight, here for fastest travel times instead of distances (Red Blob, differential heuristics; Freiburg L3).

The formal version. With:

  • u, the vertex the search is at (B above);
  • t, the goal (T);
  • L, a landmark (L);
  • time(x,y), the fastest travel time from x to y, in s;
  • hL​(u), the heuristic at u from landmark L, in s (147.0 s at B).

The steps, as above with numbers:

time(u,t)+time(t,L)≥time(u,L) time(u,t)≥time(u,L)−time(t,L) hL​(u)=time(u,L)−time(t,L)

On a directed graph the trip into a landmark and the trip out of it can differ, so each landmark stores both, and a second bound comes from driving L to u to t (Freiburg L3):

time(u,t)≥time(L,t)−time(L,u)

The heuristic takes the larger of the two bounds, and 0 if both are negative. Both lines are lower bounds, so the heuristic is admissible. The landmark heuristic is also consistent (Freiburg L3). On the edge from A to N, 28.8 s, the stored times to L are 141.3 s from A and 112.5 s from N:

141.3≤28.8+112.5 141.3−46.8≤28.8+112.5−46.8 94.5≤28.8+65.7

The same steps for any edge from u to w with travel time c:

time(u,L)≤c+time(w,L) hL​(u)≤c+hL​(w)

The first line is the triangle inequality again; the second subtracts time(t,L) from both sides.

7. Contraction hierarchies: shortcuts over unimportant roads

Key point

Rank the vertices from least to most important and remove them in that order, adding a shortcut whenever a removed vertex lay on the only fastest way between two of its neighbours. A query then runs two searches, from the start and from the goal, that only climb to higher ranks and meet at the top, which the shortcuts make enough: 67 + 92 intersections on the Chicago trip.

ALT of Section 6 still takes off its open list every intersection of the route and some around it: 108 on the Chicago trip, whose route has 42, and the route grows with the trip. Long trips need a search that skips most of the small roads.

7.1 Why skip small roads

The idea. Drivers use small streets near the start and the goal, bigger roads farther away, and motorways in between (Freiburg L6). On the town trip, only S and T are on small roads; the middle of the route is the bypass. A search would be faster if, away from the ends, it looked only at important roads.

A rule such as “far from the ends, use only motorways” is fast but can miss the fastest route; navigation devices used rules of that kind for years (Freiburg L6). Contraction hierarchies keep the idea and lose nothing: they add shortcuts so that skipping a vertex never loses a route.

7.2 Contracting a vertex: shortcuts

The idea. Put the vertices in an order, least important first: in the town, the old town (E, F, B, C), then the ends (S, T, L), the junctions (A, D) and the bypass last (N, M). Remove them one at a time. Before removing a vertex, look at every way through it, from a neighbour u in to a neighbour w out:

  • if the way through it is the only fastest way from u to w, add a direct edge from u to w with the same time;
  • if another way from u to w, avoiding the vertex, is no slower, add nothing.

The standard terms. Removing a vertex while keeping every fastest time between the vertices left is node contraction ()Node contraction: Removing a vertex from a graph while keeping every fastest time between the vertices left, by adding a shortcut for each way through it that nothing else matches; the step that builds a contraction hierarchy. Explained in §7.2 Contracting a vertex: shortcuts. (Geisberger et al. 2008). The direct edge it adds is a shortcut ()Shortcut (contraction hierarchies): A direct edge from u to w added when a contracted vertex v lay on the only fastest way u → v → w, with that way’s time, such as A → C, 120 s, for A → B → C; it lets a search skip v and is unpacked back into the original edges. Explained in §7.2 Contracting a vertex: shortcuts.. The other way is found by a Dijkstra run from u that may not use the vertex being contracted (Freiburg L6; Geisberger et al. 2008). Why contraction keeps every fastest time. Each contraction keeps every fastest time between the vertices that remain: a fastest route through the removed vertex either has a shortcut of the same time or another way that is no slower (Freiburg L7). In the town, after B is gone, A to C still takes 120 s, by the shortcut. Applying this once per vertex, the times in the final graph equal those of the original (Freiburg L7).

The order decides the speed, not the answer. Any order gives correct routes; a good order adds few shortcuts and gives short searches (Freiburg L6; mjt CH). A common order prefers vertices whose contraction adds fewer edges than it removes, and spreads the contractions evenly over the map by counting neighbours already contracted (Geisberger et al. 2008). On a road graph of Western Europe, 18,029,721 vertices, the first rule alone gave queries that took 1791 vertices off; adding the count of contracted neighbours cut that to 403 (Geisberger et al. 2008). The Chicago hierarchy uses those two rules and adds 5509 shortcuts to its 3857 edges (notebook).

7.3 The query: two searches that only climb

The idea. Search from the start and, backwards, from the goal, as in bidirectional search ()Bidirectional search: A forward search from the start and a backward search from the goal run in turn until some state has been expanded by both; the route is the smallest sum of the two costs over all states both reached, since the meeting state can be off the shortest route. Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §8.2 Bidirectional search, but let each search follow only edges that lead to a higher rank. Each climbs towards the important roads, and they meet near the top (Geisberger et al. 2008). Figure 13 runs it on the town; the bottom of each frame redraws the vertices at the height of their rank.

On the town trip, the search from S climbs S, A, N, M, and the search back from T climbs T, D, L, M. M is taken off by both:

132.3+64.8=197.1 s

the fastest time, after 8 take-offs, against Dijkstra’s 10. Each search stops once the smallest cost on its open list is at least the best route found so far, as no later vertex can give a faster one (Geisberger et al. 2008). The answer is the smallest sum over all vertices taken off by both (Freiburg L6), as in bidirectional search: for S to C three vertices qualify, A with 156 s, N with 213.6 s and M with 221.1 s, and A wins (Figure 13, last frame).

A route whose ranks dip in the middle is also covered: the dip’s vertex was contracted before its two neighbours, and a shortcut, or another way of the same time, bridges it. On S to C, B (rank 2) lies between A (rank 7) and C (rank 3), and the shortcut A → C bridges it; unpacking the shortcut gives back A, B, C (Freiburg L7; Geisberger et al. 2008).

On real maps. On the Chicago trip the two searches take off 67 and 92 intersections, 159 in all, and over 200 random trips 90.0 on average, against 861.1 for Dijkstra and 51.6 for 16 landmarks (notebook). On a city map, the hierarchy and the landmarks are close. The hierarchy pays on large maps: on Western Europe’s 18 million vertices, a query took 403 vertices off and ran in 183 µs (Geisberger et al. 2008), and on the United Kingdom’s 3,478,051 vertices a query visited 3968 + 3113 of them (mjt CH).

7.4 CH-Potentials: the hierarchy as a heuristic

A contraction hierarchy is built for one set of costs. Traffic changes the times, and forbidden turns or other rules change which routes are allowed; A* handles such changes easily, because only its costs and moves change, but it is only as fast as its heuristic (Strasser and Zeitz 2021). CH-Potentials ()CH-Potentials: A heuristic for A* taken from a contraction hierarchy, which estimates the time left tightly; it keeps A*‘s flexibility for traffic and turn rules while using the hierarchy’s speed. Explained in §7.4 CH-Potentials: the hierarchy as a heuristic. take the heuristic for A* from a contraction hierarchy: the hierarchy’s fast queries give A* tight estimates of the time left, while A* keeps its flexibility (Strasser and Zeitz 2021). Here the heuristic is built from the road network’s own hierarchy.

From no heuristic to the straight line to landmarks, the A* loop never changed; only the heuristic did, and with it the work, from 1526 intersections to 108. CH-Potentials take the same step once more: the hierarchy itself becomes the heuristic.
