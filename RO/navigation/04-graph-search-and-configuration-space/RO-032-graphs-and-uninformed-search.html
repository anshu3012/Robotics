1. Overview

Key point

A map says where the furniture is, not which way to go. Treating the free cells as places joined by moves turns the map into a graph, and searching the graph finds a route: breadth-first search finds the fewest moves to the door, 8 moves of 0.5 m.

The robot can now hold its room as a map ()Map: A robot’s list of the objects in its surroundings that matter, with their positions and properties, such as the pink pole at (1, 3); readings only give positions relative to the robot, and the map turns them into a pose. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §5.2 Bitmaps and occupancy grids, the room cut into 0.5 m cells with a number in each cell for how much of it furniture covers. 2. The map as a graph

Key point

To choose a route, the robot first needs to know which cell it can step to from which. A graph says exactly that: the free cells are its vertices and the moves between neighbouring cells are its edges.

2.1 Cells and moves: vertices and edges

To pick a route to the door, the robot first needs to know which cell it can step to from which. A metro map gives that kind of knowledge: stations are dots, and a line joins two dots when a train runs directly between them. The map leaves out everything else, the streets and the distances, because the question “can I get from here to there?” needs only the dots and the lines.

We do the same with the room. Each free cell becomes a dot, and two dots are joined when the robot can move directly between the cells: when the cells share a side, so one 0.5 m move left, down, up or right takes the robot across.

Figure 2 shows the result for our room. Look at the start cell S: four orange lines leave it, one for each move, because all four cells beside it are free.

Figure 2: The room’s 0.5 m grid with a blue dot at the centre of each of the 76 free cells and a blue line between every two free cells that share a side, 114 lines in all; the four lines from the start cell S are orange; the grey blocked cells have no dot

Dots joined by lines are a graph ()Graph: A set of vertices (points) joined by edges (lines), such as the room’s 76 free cells joined by 114 moves; it keeps only which places connect directly, so one search method works for maps, puzzles and any problem of states and moves. Explained in §2.1 Cells and moves: vertices and edges., the standard model of places and the direct steps between them (LaValle §2.1). Its parts have names:

  • a vertex ()Vertex (graph): One point of a graph, such as one free cell of the room or one waypoint; the places a search moves between. Explained in §2.1 Cells and moves: vertices and edges. is one dot, here one free cell, such as the start cell S;
  • an edge ()Edge (graph): A link between two vertices of a graph, such as one 0.5 m move between two free cells that share a side; it says the search can go directly from one vertex to the other. Joining each cell to the cells that share a side with it, at most four, gives a 4-connected grid ()4-connected grid: A grid made into a graph by joining each cell to the up to four cells that share a side with it (left, down, up, right); the simplest graph of a grid map, with every move the same length. Formally, a graph is written as a pair of sets:

  • G is the graph;
  • V is its set of vertices, here the 76 free cells;
  • E is its set of edges, here the 114 pairs of free cells that share a side;
  • a set is a collection with no repeats, and curly brackets list its members: {1,2,3} is the set of the numbers 1, 2 and 3.
G=(V,E)

The graph says which moves exist, but not which ones to take; finding those is the search of Section 3 onwards.

2.2 Any problem of states and moves is a graph

The graph of Figure 2 came from a map, but the idea does not need a map. Anything that is in one situation at a time, and changes situation by a move, fits the same picture: each situation is a vertex, each move an edge (CS188 §1.2).

  • Our robot: the situation is its cell; a move is a 0.5 m step. The graph has 76 vertices.
  • A Rubik’s cube: the situation is the arrangement of the coloured squares; a move is one turn of a face. Each of the 6 faces can be turned 3 ways: a quarter turn clockwise, a quarter turn anticlockwise or a half turn. Each arrangement therefore has 18 possible turns, so 18 edges leave every vertex (Korf 1997).

The situation a problem can be in is its state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.1 State: from one number to many, the quantities that decide what happens next. All the states together, with the moves between them, form the state space ()State space: All the states a problem can be in, joined by the moves between them, such as the robot’s 76 cells or the arrangements of a Rubik’s cube; it is a graph, so planning becomes finding a path from the start state to a goal state. Explained in §2.2 Any problem of states and moves is a graph.: a graph whose vertices are states and whose edges are the moves (CS188 §1.2.2).

Searching a state space means finding a path from the start state to a goal state, whatever the states stand for. Later Notes search a state space of facts, such as “the cup is on the table” (symbolic task planningSymbolic task planning: Choosing a sequence of actions that reaches a goal, from a description of the world in true-or-false facts such as “the cup is on the table”; it decides the order of steps (go to the table, pick up, go to the door, put down) that a route search alone cannot. Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §1. Overview).

With either graph in hand, the question is how to search it, and the simplest idea is to list every path.

3. The search tree: every path from the start

Key point

Listing every path from the start that never revisits a vertex finds every route to the goal, but the list grows fast and contains the same vertex many times: 40 paths for the 7-waypoint graph.

Which of the routes from S reaches the door G? A program has no eyes to spot a good route on a picture, so the plainest method is to try them all: from S, write down each way to go on; from each of those, each way to go on again; and never let a path visit a waypoint it has already visited (CS188 §1.2.2). A path that reaches G is not extended.

The list of paths is the search tree ()Search tree: The tree of paths from the start: each node is a whole path, and its children extend it by one edge; listing it finds every route, but it repeats vertices and grows fast (40 paths for 7 waypoints). Explained in §3. The search tree: every path from the start.: each node is a whole path from the start, the top node, at level 0, is the path that has not moved yet, and the nodes on level 2 are the paths of 2 edges (CS188 §1.2.2). For the waypoint graph:

  • levels 0 to 6 hold 1, 3, 8, 11, 10, 5 and 2 paths, 40 in all;
  • 14 of the 40 paths reach G; the first ones, on level 2, are S–D–G and S–P–G (notebook).

The tree is wasteful in two ways. First, it repeats work: waypoint D ends 3 nodes on levels 1 and 2 (S–D, S–A–D, S–P–D, the purple nodes in the last frame of Figure 4), and the tree grows everything below D three times. A state space graph holds each state once; a search tree can hold it many times, because each node carries the whole path that led to it (CS188 §1.2.2).

Second, the tree grows fast: on the grid of 76 cells, its level 8 alone holds 1,447 paths (Section 7). We need to grow only part of the tree, and never the same vertex twice.

4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list

Key point

To reach the door without exploring any place twice, keep two lists: the vertices waiting to be explored (the frontier) and those already explored (the visited list). Taking one vertex off the frontier and adding its new neighbours, over and over, reaches the door in 7 expansions on the waypoint graph, against 40 paths in the tree.

The tree of Section 3 grew the paths below D three times. The fix is the one we use for errands: a to-do list and a done list. The to-do list holds the places reached but not yet explored; the done list holds the places already explored, so they are never explored again.

The to-do list is the frontier ()Frontier (open list): The vertices a search has reached but not yet expanded, waiting to be taken off one at a time; which one comes off next is what makes one search differ from another. Explained in §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list., also called the open list: the vertices the search has reached but not yet expanded. Expanding a vertex means taking it off the frontier and looking at its neighbours. The done list is the visited list ()Visited list (closed list): The vertices a search has already expanded; a search never expands them again, so it explores each vertex once (7 expansions on the waypoint graph instead of 40 paths). Explained in §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list., also called the closed list or extended list: the vertices already expanded (CS188 §1.3, §1.4; LaValle §2.2.1; MIT 6.034).

For each vertex the search also records its parent ()Parent (back pointer): The vertex from which a search reached a vertex, stored with it during the search; following parents back from the goal gives the route. In A*, a cell’s parent changes whenever a shorter way in lowers its cost so far. Explained in §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list., also called its back pointer: the vertex it was reached from, so the route can be read back at the end by following parents from the goal (LaValle §2.2.1). The loop (LaValle §2.2.1; CS188 §1.3):

  1. Put the start S on the frontier.
  2. Take a vertex off the frontier. If it is the goal G, stop, and read the route back from G along the parents.
  3. Mark the vertex visited. Add each of its neighbours that is neither visited nor already on the frontier, and record the vertex as their parent.
  4. Go back to step 2. If the frontier is empty, there is no route.

The loop leaves one choice open: which vertex in step 2 comes off the frontier next. Every search in this Note and the next is this loop with a different answer to that one question (LaValle §2.2.2).

A search that makes this choice only from the order in which vertices arrived, knowing nothing about where the goal lies, is an uninformed search ()Uninformed search (blind search): A search that chooses which vertex to expand next only by the order vertices were reached, knowing nothing about where the goal lies, such as breadth-first and depth-first search. Explained in §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list., also called blind search (CS188 §1.3; NPTEL AI search, lecture 7). Sections 5 and 6 take the two simplest orders.

5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring

Key point

Taking the vertex that has waited longest explores the grid in rings of equal moves from the start, so the first time the goal comes off the frontier, it is by the fewest moves: 8 moves, 4.0 m, after 57 expansions.

The loop of Section 4 needs an order in which to take vertices off the frontier, the vertices waiting their turn. The first answer is the one a ticket counter uses: serve whoever has waited longest. A frontier served this way, new arrivals joining the back and the next vertex leaving from the front, is a queue ()Queue (first in, first out): A list served in arrival order: new items join the back and the next item leaves from the front; as a search’s frontier it gives breadth-first search. Explained in §5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring., “first in, first out”.

The loop with a queue is breadth-first search ()Breadth-first search (BFS): A search whose frontier is a queue, so it expands vertices in rings of equal moves from the start; the first route it finds has the fewest moves, which is the shortest only when every move costs the same. Explained in §5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring., BFS: it finishes every vertex one move from the start before any vertex two moves away (LaValle §2.2.2; CS188 §1.3.2). The run of Figure 5 was breadth-first.

8×0.5=4.0 m

The rings hold 1, 4, 7, 9, 10, 9, 7, 6 and 5 cells for 0 to 8 moves. Without the stop at G, the search would go on to reach all 76 free cells, the farthest 18 moves from S (notebook).

Why the first route found has the fewest moves. Three facts about the queue give it:

  1. Cells two moves from S join the queue only while cells one move away are being expanded, so they wait behind every cell one move away. The same holds for every ring: all of ring k, the cells k moves from S, leave the queue before any of ring k+1.
  2. A cell gets its parent the first time any neighbour reaches it. Rings are expanded in order, so that neighbour is in the lowest ring next to it, and the cell lands in the ring one further out.
  3. So each cell’s ring is its fewest number of moves from S. G is in ring 8: no route reaches it in 7 moves.

The order in which a cell’s neighbours are tried can change which 8-move route is found, but not its length: with each of the 24 possible orders, the route has 8 moves (notebook). Fewest moves means shortest only because every move is 0.5 m long. Breadth-first search finds the cheapest route only when every edge costs the same (CS188 §1.3.2); the next Note lets the robot also move diagonally, and that condition fails.

Breadth-first search pays for its guarantee by finishing each whole ring before going one move further: 57 expansions to reach the door. The opposite order goes straight for depth.

6. Depth-first search: dive, then back up

Key point

Taking the vertex that arrived last dives along one path until it is stuck, then backs up to the newest choice not yet tried. Depth-first search finds a route, but which one depends on the order the moves are tried: 22 moves, 11.0 m, on our grid, where breadth-first search found 8.

The second answer to the choice in Section 4 is the opposite of a queue: take the waiting vertex that arrived last. A pile of plates works this way: the plate put on top last is taken first. A frontier served from the end where vertices arrive is a stack ()Stack (last in, first out): A list served from the end where items arrive, like a pile of plates: the item put on last comes off first; as a search’s frontier it gives depth-first search. Explained in §6. Depth-first search: dive, then back up., “last in, first out”.

The loop with a stack is depth-first search ()Depth-first search (DFS): A search whose frontier is a stack, so it always extends the newest path and dives deep before backing up; it backs up to its last untried choice at dead ends and finds a route, but which one, and how long, depends on the order the moves are tried (22 moves where breadth-first search found 8). Explained in §6. Depth-first search: dive, then back up., DFS: it always extends the newest path, so it plunges deeper and deeper along one line (LaValle §2.2.2; CS188 §1.3.1).

When the search reaches a cell with no new neighbour, a dead end, the next cell off the stack hangs off an earlier cell. The search has gone back to the most recent choice it has not tried and taken the other branch.

With an unlucky one it wanders for 65 expansions and returns a route of 26 moves, over three times as long as needed. Python: one loop gives both searches; only the end of the frontier the next cell comes from differs. A deque (double-ended queue) can give its first item, popleft(), or its last, pop(). The stack takes new cells in reverse so that the first move tried ends on top.

Both searches expand each cell at most once, thanks to the visited list (the cells already expanded), so on our grid both stop after at most 76 expansions. The tree of every path had 1,447 paths on level 8 alone. How the work grows as problems get bigger decides which searches are usable at all.

7. How fast the work grows: Big-O and exponential time

Key point

Listing every path to the door multiplies the work by the number of choices at every level, 65,536 move sequences of 8 moves, while a search with a visited list touches each vertex and edge a fixed number of times, 304 steps on our grid. Big-O notation names such growth rates, ignoring constant factors.

7.1 Counting the work

7.2 Big-O notation

To compare searches we do not need the exact step counts, which depend on the computer and the code; we need how the count grows with the problem. So we keep only the fastest-growing part and drop constant factors: breadth-first search’s 304 steps grow in step with the number of vertices plus the number of edges, and listing every path grows like bd (USACO Guide, time complexity).

Writing a growth rate this way is Big-O notation ()Big-O notation: A way to write how fast an algorithm’s work grows with the problem’s size, keeping only the fastest-growing part and dropping constant factors, such as O(V+E) for breadth-first search and O(bd) for listing every path; it lets us compare methods without timing them. Explained in §7.2 Big-O notation.. With V now standing for the number of vertices, 76 on our grid, and E for the number of edges, 114, “breadth-first search is O(V+E)” reads “its work grows at most in proportion to the number of vertices plus the number of edges” (LaValle §2.2.2; USACO Guide, time complexity).

The formal version, with:

  • n is the size of the problem, such as the depth d;
  • f(n) is the exact work for size n, such as the tree size N;
  • g(n) is a simpler function, such as bd;
  • c is a constant number, the same for every n;
  • the sign ≤ means “is at most”.

f(n) is O(g(n)) when, for all large enough n:

f(n)≤cg(n)

The tree size is O(bd) with c equal to 2, because the fraction of Section 7.1 is at most twice its last level when b is at least 2. With our numbers, b of 4 and d of 8:

4−149−1​=87,381 2×48=131,072 87,381≤131,072

So, in Big-O terms:

  • listing every path takes O(bd): exponential in the depth d;
  • breadth-first and depth-first search with a visited list take O(V+E): in proportion to the size of the graph (LaValle §2.2.2).

A search with a visited list is cheap in the size of its graph. The trouble is that for many robots the graph itself is enormous.

8. Why exact planning is hard: NP-hard and PSPACE-hard

Key point

Each number a robot’s state needs multiplies the number of grid cells, so grids blow up exponentially with the number of numbers. Complexity theory says the general planning problem has no shortcut around this: it is PSPACE-hard, and even shortest paths among obstacles in 3D are NP-hard.

Our grid needs two numbers per state, x and y, and has 112 cells. Each further number multiplies the cells by its number of values:

  • x and y in 0.5 m cells: 14 times 8 is 112 cells;
  • add the heading, in 8 steps of 45°: 112 times 8 is 896 cells;
  • a robot arm with 6 joints, each angle in 100 steps: 100 to the power 6 is 1012 cells.

At 108 cells per second (USACO Guide, time complexity), only touching each of the arm’s cells once takes:

1012/108=104 s 104 s=2.8 hours

The search of Sections 4 to 6 is O(V+E), but V, the number of vertices, is itself exponential in the number of numbers per state. Is there a cleverer exact method that avoids this blow-up? Complexity theory sorts problems by how fast the best possible method’s work must grow, and its answer for planning is: almost certainly not.

The classes, in plain words. Two groups of problems matter first, and together they are P and NP ()P and NP: Two classes of problems: P, those a computer can solve with work that grows like a power of the input size (polynomial time), such as breadth-first search on a grid; NP, those whose “yes” answers can be checked in polynomial time once shown, such as surviving a known sequence of Tetris pieces. Whether every NP problem is also in P is unproved. Explained in §8. Why exact planning is hard: NP-hard and PSPACE-hard. (MIT 6.006 L23 notes):

  • P is the set of problems a computer can solve with work that grows like a power of the input size, n, n2 or n3, called polynomial time. Breadth-first search on a grid is in P: its O(V+E) grows like the first power of the grid’s size.

9. Explicit and implicit graphs

Key point

A graph can be built in full before the search (explicit) or made as the search goes, by a function that returns a vertex’s neighbours when asked (implicit). An implicit graph never makes the vertices the search does not reach, which is what makes large state spaces searchable.

Section 8 multiplied the cells by each extra number in the state. A planner for a robot’s position and heading shows what that does to the graph (CMU 16-350):

  • a 20 m by 20 m area in 0.25 m cells with 8 headings has 80 times 80 times 8, that is 51,200 states;
  • a 200 m by 200 m area in 0.25 m cells with 16 headings has 800 times 800 times 16, that is 10,240,000 states.

Building and storing all 51,200 states with their edges before the search is quick. Building 10 million, most of which the search never reaches, wastes time and memory.

So there are two ways to hand a graph to a search (CMU 16-350; CS188 §1.2.2):

  • an implicit graph ()Implicit graph: A graph never built in full: the search starts from the start vertex and calls a function that returns a vertex’s neighbours when it is expanded, so only the vertices the search reaches are made; this is what makes state spaces of millions of states searchable. Explained in §9. Explicit and implicit graphs. is never built in full: the search starts from the start vertex and calls a function that returns a vertex’s neighbours when it is expanded, so only the vertices the search reaches are ever made.

A Rubik’s cube can only be searched this way, because its state space is never written down; the search applies the 18 turns to the arrangement it is expanding (LaValle §2.2.2). Explicit graphs are typical for 2D problems in robotics and implicit graphs for problems with more dimensions (CMU 16-350).

An implicit graph is defined entirely by what a state holds and by the neighbours function, so choosing the state is the last decision left.

10. Choosing the search state: the Markov rule

Key point

The search state must hold everything that decides which moves are allowed and what they cost. Leave out the robot’s battery charge, and a depth-first search finds no route to a door 8 moves away; put it in, and it finds one.

The implicit graph of Section 9 is only as good as its states. Suppose the robot’s battery holds enough for 12 moves, and each 0.5 m move uses one unit.

Breadth-first search found an 8-move route in Section 5, so the battery is enough. Yet depth-first search with the cell as its state, run in the order of Figure 7, reports “no route” after 42 expansions.

Figure 11 shows why. Each visited cell, a cell already expanded, shows the charge left when the search first reached it.

From there the door is 6 moves away, so every path through it runs flat. Later the search comes back to the cell below it with 11 units, but the purple cell is already visited, so it is never tried again.

The fault is in the state, not in the search. With the cell alone as the state, the moves allowed from a cell depend on the charge left, and the charge depends on the path that led there: on the history.

The search assumes the opposite, that a vertex’s neighbours and their costs depend only on the vertex itself (CMU 16-350). The assumption that only the present state matters is the Markov property ()Markov property: The property of a sequence of states that the future depends on the past only through the present state; it lets a filter forget the history and work from the latest state alone. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.3 Complete state and the Markov property, the future depending on the past only through the present state, applied to search.

Explained in §10. Choosing the search state: the Markov rule.: the quantities that decide which moves are allowed and what they cost, and nothing more (CS188 §1.2; CMU 16-350). Here the fix is to add the charge to the state: the same cell with 10 units left and with 2 units left are now different states. The last frame of Figure 11 runs the same depth-first search with (cell, charge left) as the state and finds a 12-move route after 182 expansions. The price is more states, one per cell and charge from 0 to 12:

76×13=988 states

Breadth-first search happens to survive the cell-only state in this example, because it reaches every cell first by the fewest moves, with the most charge left: it reaches the door with 4 units to spare (notebook). The rule does not depend on luck: a search state must hold what decides the next moves and costs, and every extra quantity multiplies the states, as in Section 8.

A Pac-Man that only has to reach a square needs its square as the state; one that must eat every dot needs its square plus a yes or no for each dot (CS188 §1.2). With the state chosen, the graph, the search loop and its cost are all in place for a room whose moves are all the same length.
