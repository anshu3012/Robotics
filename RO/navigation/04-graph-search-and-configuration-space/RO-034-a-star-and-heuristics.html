1. Overview

Key point

Dijkstra’s algorithm finds the robot’s shortest route to the door, but it searches away from the door as much as towards it. Adding a guess of the distance left that never overestimates makes the search head for the door: A* finds the same 3.414 m route after 9 cells instead of 54.

The robot at (2 m, 1 m) can already find a route to the door on its map of the room, cut into 0.5 m cells, 76 of them free (occupancy grid ())Occupancy grid: A grid map that stores in each cell a number between 0 and 1, such as the probability that the cell is occupied, instead of a hard 0 or 1; the standard grid map of robotics, since sensors are never sure. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §5.2 Bitmaps and occupancy grids. The robot moves from a free cell to any of its 8 neighbours: 0.5 m straight or 0.707 m diagonally, and a diagonal move never cuts the corner of a blocked cell. The robot stands on the corner of the start cell S, which runs from 2.0 to 2.5 m along x and 1.0 to 1.5 m along y; the goal cell G sits just under the door, from 3.5 to 4.0 m along x and 3.5 to 4.0 m along y.

Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §3.2 The algorithm: settle the nearest, update its neighbours finds the shortest route from S to G: 3.414 m. Dijkstra keeps an open list of the cells it has reached, the frontier ()Frontier (open list): The vertices a search has reached but not yet expanded, waiting to be taken off one at a time; which one comes off next is what makes one search differ from another. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list, and always takes off the cell nearest to S.

2. A guess of the distance left: the heuristic

Key point

Dijkstra cannot tell a cell near the door from a cell far from it. A cheap guess of the distance left from each cell, worked out as if there were no obstacles, gives it that sense of direction: from S the guess is 3.121 m, the true distance 3.414 m.

The idea. Dijkstra ranks the cells on its open list by the distance driven from S, so a cell 1 m behind the robot ranks with a cell 1 m towards the door. To prefer the second, the search needs, for each cell, some idea of how far the door still is. The idea has to be cheap: the exact distance left is what we are searching for.

A traveller does this with a map: before knowing the roads, the distance as the crow flies already tells which town is nearer. The robot can do the same on its grid: count the moves to G as if the table, the cabinet and the poles were not there.

The standard term. A function that guesses the cost still to go from a cell to the goal is a heuristic ()Heuristic h: A cheap guess of the cost still to go from a state to the goal, such as 3.121 m from the start cell to the door; a search uses it to prefer states that look nearer the goal. Explained in §2. A guess of the distance left: the heuristic., written h (CS188 §1.4; LaValle §2.2.2). Figure 2 writes the guess for our room in every free cell.

Figure 2: The heuristic h in every free cell of the left part of the room (x 0 to 5.5 m): the octile distance to G, in m, the length of the shortest 8-connected route if there were no obstacles. From S it is 3.121 m, the dotted route of 3 diagonal and 2 straight moves, which runs through the table’s cells; the dashed line is the straight-line distance, 2.915 m. The true shortest route from S is 3.414 m

The mechanism on our numbers. On an empty 8-connected grid the shortest route makes diagonal moves while both the column count and the row count still have to shrink, and straight moves for what is left (Patel, Heuristics). S is 3 columns left of G and 5 rows below it, so with:

  • Δx, the number of columns between the cell and G, 3 for S;
  • Δy, the number of rows between the cell and G, 5 for S;
  • m, the smaller of Δx and Δy, 3 for S;
  • M, the larger of them, 5 for S;
  • a, the length of a straight move, 0.5 m;
  • ℓ, the length of a diagonal move, 0.707 m.

The route takes m diagonal moves, which cover the 3 columns and 3 of the rows, and then M−m straight moves for the 2 rows left:

h(S)=3×0.707+2×0.5 =2.121+1.0 =3.121 m

The same in symbols:

h=ℓ×m+a×(M−m)

This heuristic is the octile distance ()Octile distance: The length of the shortest route between two cells of an 8-connected grid with no obstacles: diagonal moves while both the column and the row count shrink, then straight moves; with straight moves of length a and diagonals of length ℓ it is ℓmin+a(max−min) of the column and row counts; a heuristic for 8-connected grids that never overestimates. Explained in §2. A guess of the distance left: the heuristic. (Patel, Heuristics). The octile distance is the exact answer to an easier problem, the same grid with every obstacle removed; dropping a rule of the real problem in this way is a common source of heuristics (CS188 §1.4).

A second heuristic is the straight line from the centre of S to the centre of G, the Euclidean distance ()Euclidean distance: The straight-line distance between two points. Explained in: Handling Missing Data: KNN Imputer, §4.1 The Euclidean distance, which drops one more rule: that moves follow the grid. With 3 columns and 5 rows of 0.5 m:

straight line=0.5×32+52​ =0.5×34​ =0.5×5.83095 =2.915 m

Both heuristics cost a few multiplications per cell, and both fall short of the true 3.414 m, because the table makes the real route bend. The heuristic is ready; the next question is how the search should use it.

3. Following the guess alone: greedy best-first search

Key point

Taking off the open list the cell whose heuristic h, the guess of the distance left, is smallest heads straight for the goal and looks at few cells, but it never counts the distance already driven, so it can return a long route: 9.536 m where 8.000 m exists.

The idea. The simplest use of the heuristic from Section 2: keep the same open list, but always take off the cell that looks nearest to the goal, the one with the smallest h. On the door trip this works: it reaches G after 7 cells with the 3.414 m route (notebook).

The standard term. Expanding, at every step, the open cell with the smallest heuristic value is greedy best-first search ()Greedy best-first search: A search that always expands the open state with the smallest heuristic value, ignoring the cost already paid; it heads straight for the goal and looks at few states, but can return a long route. Explained in §3. Following the guess alone: greedy best-first search. (CS188 §1.4). “Best-first” names any search that takes off the open list the cell that scores best; “greedy” says the score is the heuristic alone.

Where it goes wrong. The door trip is too short to show the flaw, so we give the robot a longer trip in the same room: from the bottom-left corner cell (x 0 to 0.5 m, y 0 to 0.5 m) to a cell at the right wall (x 6.5 to 7.0 m, y 1.5 to 2.0 m), behind the table. Figure 3 runs greedy best-first search on it: watch the red cells head up and to the right, straight at the goal, until the table’s left side stops them, and then slide down the table to the bottom of the room.

Figure 3: Greedy best-first search on the long trip, from S in the bottom-left corner cell to G at the right wall, one cell per frame: it always takes off the open list the cell with the smallest heuristic h, the octile distance to G (7.121 m at S), and never looks at the distance already driven. The search heads up and right, meets the table, and slides down its left side. Last frame: its route, 9.536 m after 21 cells (blue), and the shortest route, 8.000 m along the bottom wall (green, dashed)

Greedy best-first search returns 9.536 m after 21 cells; the shortest route, along the bottom wall, is 8.000 m (notebook). The climb towards the goal’s height looked good to the heuristic, and nothing in the score counted what the climb and the way back down cost:

extra=9.536−8.000 =1.536 m

Greedy search can go straight to the goal or wander like a badly guided depth-first search, depending on the obstacles (CS188 §1.4). The fix is to score each cell by both what it has cost and what it still promises.

4. A*: cost so far plus the guess

Key point

A* scores every cell on the open list by f=g+h: the length of the best route found so far from S to the cell, plus the heuristic, the guess of the length left. A* takes off the cell with the smallest f, so it heads for the door like greedy search but drops a route as soon as its distance driven makes it look worse than another.

Greedy search of Section 3 forgot the distance driven; Dijkstra forgot the distance left. A* adds the two. We walk it through on the door trip, where every step can be checked by hand (CMU 16-350, A*; Hart et al. 1968).

4.1 g, h and f on the first step

For every cell the search has reached, it keeps three numbers:

  • g, the length of the best route found so far from S to the cell, in m: the cost so far ()Cost so far g (g-value): The length of the best route found so far from the start to a state; A* lowers it whenever a shorter way into the state turns up. Explained in §4.1 g, h and f on the first step., also called the g-value;
  • h, the heuristic: the octile distance, a guess of the length left from the cell to G, in m (Section 2);
  • f, their sum, in m: the estimated total cost ()Estimated total cost f (f-value): The cost so far plus the heuristic, f=g+h: the length of the best route through a state if the guess were exact; A* expands the open state with the smallest f. Explained in §4.1 g, h and f on the first step., also called the f-value, the length of the best route through the cell if the heuristic were exact.
f=g+h

At the start only S is on the open list, with g=0 and h=3.121 m, so f=3.121 m. A* takes S off and expands it, as in breadth-first search ()Breadth-first search (BFS): A search whose frontier is a queue, so it expands vertices in rings of equal moves from the start; the first route it finds has the fewest moves, which is the shortest only when every move costs the same. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §5. Breadth-first search: ring by ring: it puts each of its 8 neighbours on the open list, here with its own g, h and f. For the cell diagonally up and to the right of S, 2 columns left of G and 4 rows below it:

g=0.707 h=2×0.707+2×0.5 =2.414 f=0.707+2.414 =3.121 m

For the cell straight above S, 3 columns and 4 rows from G:

g=0.5 h=3×0.707+1×0.5 =2.621 f=0.5+2.621 =3.121 m

4.2 The smallest f first, ties to the smaller h

A* now takes off the open list the cell with the smallest f, as Dijkstra takes the smallest g. Two cells tie at 3.121 m, the cell up and to the right of S and the cell above S. A tie is broken by the smaller heuristic, which means the cell nearer to G by the heuristic (Patel, Heuristics): 2.414 m beats 2.621 m, so the cell up-right of S goes first (frame “2”).

Frames “2” to “4” show why the smallest f is a good choice. While the search heads straight for G, each move adds to g exactly what it takes off h, so f stays at 3.121 m. Moving up from the cell up-right of S:

g=0.707+0.5=1.207 h=2.414−0.5=1.914 f=1.207+1.914=3.121 m

At the cell below the table’s corner (frame “4”) the diagonal towards G would cut the corner of a blocked table cell, which is not allowed. The cell above it gets f=3.414 m: the detour round the corner costs what the heuristic did not see,

3.414−3.121=0.293 m

4.3 A shorter way in lowers g

The cells straight up from the cell up-right of S are used up: the next one has f=3.414 m. So A* goes back to the other cell with f=3.121 m, the one straight above S (frame “5”). From there it reaches the cell two above S again. That cell already had g=1.414 m from the first time it was reached, by two diagonal moves; the new way in is two straight moves:

gold​=0.707+0.707=1.414 m gnew​=0.5+0.5=1.0 m

The new way is shorter, so A* lowers the cell’s g to 1.0 m and its f from 3.536 to 3.121 m (CMU 16-350, A*). Frame “6” does the same one cell higher: 1.914 m falls to 1.5 m. A cell on the open list keeps the best g found so far, never the first.

4.4 Parent arrows give the route

Now every f left is 3.414 m or more. Three cells tie at 3.414 m, and the smallest heuristic, 1.207 m, wins (frame “7”); then the cell left of G (frame “8”), then G itself (frame “9”). G comes off the open list with h=0, so its f is its g: 3.414 m, the route length.

To find the route itself, each cell also stores the cell its best g came from: its parent ()Parent (back pointer): The vertex from which a search reached a vertex, stored with it during the search; following parents back from the goal gives the route. In A*, a cell’s parent changes whenever a shorter way in lowers its cost so far. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list, the back pointer breadth-first search keeps too, here changed whenever g drops (CMU 16-350, A*). The last frame of Figure 4 draws the parents as arrows. Following them back from G to S gives the route: up-right from S, three cells up, past the table’s corner, and right into G.

4.5 The algorithm

The steps of Sections 4.1 to 4.4, with the open list kept as a priority queue ()Priority queue: A collection with two operations, insert an entry with a priority and remove the entry with the best priority (for Dijkstra’s algorithm, the smallest distance); it is what lets a search always pick its most promising vertex next. Explained in: Dijkstra’s Algorithm and Priority Queues, §5.1 Why a plain list is slow ordered by f and the closed list as the visited list ()Visited list (closed list): The vertices a search has already expanded; a search never expands them again, so it explores each vertex once (7 expansions on the waypoint graph instead of 40 paths). Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §4. Searching with a frontier and a visited list of cells already expanded (CMU 16-350, A*; LaValle §2.2.2):

  1. Put S on the open list with g=0 and f=h(S).
  2. Take off the open list the cell with the smallest f (ties: the smallest h) and put it on the closed list.
  3. If that cell is G, stop: follow the parents back to S.
  4. Otherwise, for each neighbour that is free and not on the closed list, work out g through the current cell. If the neighbour is new, or this g is smaller than its stored g, store the new g, f and parent and put it on the open list.
  5. Go to step 2.

The loop above is A* ()A search:* Dijkstra’s algorithm with the open list sorted by f=g+h, the cost so far plus a heuristic guess of the cost left; with a consistent heuristic it returns the shortest route while expanding far fewer states (9 cells against 54 on the room grid). Explained in §4.5 The algorithm., “A star” (Hart et al. 1968): Dijkstra’s algorithm with the open list sorted by f=g+h instead of g. With h=0 everywhere, f is g and A* is Dijkstra (LaValle §2.2.2; Patel, Heuristics).

Figure 1 compared exactly these two, top and bottom: 54 cells with h=0 against 9 with the octile heuristic, for the same 3.414 m. A* returned the shortest route here, but nothing so far says it always will.

5. Why A* returns the shortest route: admissible heuristics

Key point

If the heuristic h is never more than the true distance left, f, the cost so far plus the heuristic, is a lower bound on any route through a cell, so the first route to G off the open list is the shortest. The heuristic is the one free choice in A*: among heuristics that never overestimate, a closer one takes fewer cells, 54, 11 and 9 for h=0, the straight line and the octile distance, all with the 3.414 m route.

A* stopped at 3.414 m after only 9 of the 76 cells, without looking at most of the room. Section 5 checks why the cells it never expanded cannot hide a shorter route.

5.1 A guess that never overestimates

A trip by road is never shorter than the distance as the crow flies, so “crow-flight distance so far plus crow-flight distance left” can never overshoot the trip (Wikipedia, A* search algorithm). A* relies on the same property. A heuristic that is never more than the true cost left from any cell is an admissible heuristic ()Admissible heuristic: A heuristic that is never more than the true cost left from any state, h≤h∗; it makes f a lower bound on every route through a state, so the first route A* takes off the open list is the shortest. Explained in §5.1 A guess that never overestimates. (CS188 §1.4). With:

  • h(n), the heuristic at a cell n, in m (3.121 m at S);
  • h∗(n), the true shortest distance left from n to G, in m (3.414 m at S).

The rule is, for every cell:

h(n)≤h∗(n)

The octile heuristic is admissible because it is the length of the shortest route in the room with its obstacles removed (Section 2), and putting obstacles back can only remove moves, never add a shorter one (LaValle §2.2.2). Figure 5 checks this on every free cell: the true distances come from one Dijkstra run backwards from G, and every dot lies on or below the line where guess and truth are equal.

Figure 5: Every free cell, heuristic against the true distance left to G: across, the true shortest distance, from one Dijkstra run backwards from G; up, the heuristic h, the octile distance (blue dots) and the straight-line distance (orange diamonds). Every dot lies on or below the dashed line h = true distance, so neither guess overestimates; the octile dot of each cell is on or above its straight-line dot. S: octile 3.121 m, true 3.414 m. The cells far below the line lie behind the table, where the true route goes all the way round

5.2 Why the first route out is the shortest

When G comes off the open list, every other route from S to G is still unfinished. Any such route starts in a closed cell, S, and must leave the closed cells somewhere: the first cell it reaches outside them is on the open list. Take the cell left of S, which is on the open list when G comes off (Figure 4, frame “9”), with:

  • n, that open cell;
  • g(n), its cost so far, 0.5 m, one straight move from S, already the shortest way to it;
  • h(n), its heuristic, 3.328 m;
  • f(n), its estimated total cost, 3.828 m;
  • L(n), the length of the shortest route from S to G through n, in m, unknown;
  • g(G), the length A* returns, 3.414 m; since h(G)=0, it is also f(G).
L(n)=0.5+h∗(n) ≥0.5+3.328 =3.828 ≥3.414 =g(G)

The same steps in symbols:

L(n)=g(n)+h∗(n) ≥g(n)+h(n) =f(n) ≥f(G) =g(G)

The second line is the admissible rule; the fourth holds because A* took G off before n, so f(G) was the smallest f on the open list. Every open cell gives the same chain, so no route is shorter than the one A* returned (CS188 §1.4). The first line needs g(n) to be the shortest way to n; it is here, and Section 6 shows when the closed list breaks it.

The chain also shows what a heuristic that overestimates does. If h could be larger than h∗, the second line would fail, f(n) could exceed the length of the best route through n, and A* could take G off before ever expanding n: it might return a longer route, though faster (Patel, Heuristics). Section 10 uses that trade on purpose.

Any admissible heuristic keeps the answer right; the closer it is to the true distance left, the fewer cells A* takes off the open list. 6. When admissible is not enough: consistent heuristics

Key point

A* never expands a cell twice, so a cell closed by a long way in keeps that wrong cost so far g. A heuristic can be admissible and still let that happen. The fix is a consistent heuristic: along any move it never falls by more than the move’s length, and then every cell leaves the open list with its shortest g.

Section 5.2 needed the cost so far g of each open cell to be the shortest way in. On the grid that held, because the octile heuristic changes by no more than the move between two neighbouring cells; Section 6.2 shows why that matters. Here is a heuristic that is admissible and still breaks it.

6.3 Why consistency keeps g right

The consistent rule makes f never fall along a route. Take a move from the cell below the table’s corner, n, to the cell above it, n′, with:

  • g(n), the cost so far at n, 1.707 m;
  • h(n), its heuristic, 1.414 m, so f(n)=3.121 m;
  • c, the move, 0.5 m;
  • h(n′), the heuristic at n′, 1.207 m.
f(n′)=1.707+0.5+1.207 =3.414 ≥1.707+1.414 =3.121 =f(n)

The same steps in symbols:

f(n′)=g(n)+c+h(n′) ≥g(n)+h(n) =f(n)

The second line is the consistent rule with g(n) added to both sides. So the f values along any route never decrease, and A* takes cells off the open list in order of f. A cell reached first by a long way, as C by way of B, has a larger f than every cell on its short way in, so those cells come off first and lower its g before it is closed (CMU 16-350, A*; Wikipedia, A* search algorithm).

On the roadmap, run 1’s f falls from 3.653 at A to 2.487 at C by way of A: that fall is what let C close early.

A second fix keeps any admissible heuristic: reopen a closed cell when a shorter way into it turns up, at the price of expanding some cells more than once (CS188 §1.4). The octile heuristic is consistent, so on the grid A* expands each cell once and returns the shortest route. What it still costs is memory: it keeps every cell it has reached.

7. Saving memory: iterative deepening and IDA*

Key point

A* stores every cell it has reached; a depth-first search stores only its current route. Iterative deepening repeats a depth-first search with a growing limit; IDA* puts the limit on f, the cost so far plus the heuristic, so it returns the shortest route while storing at most one route: on the door trip, 17 visits with at most 7 cells in memory.

When A* stopped on the door trip it held 23 cells: 9 on the closed list and 14 on the open list (notebook). The room is small, but a planner over position and heading, or over the joints of an arm, has far more states, and A*‘s lists grow with the number of states it reaches (CMU 16-350, heuristics).

The idea. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §6. 8. Searching from the door: backward and bidirectional search

Key point

A search can run from the goal back to the start, or from both ends until they meet. From the door, which stands against the wall, Dijkstra needs 31 cells instead of 54; from both ends, 38. Two searches that meet must still compare every cell both have reached, because the cell where they meet need not lie on the shortest route.

Every search so far started at S. But the door never moves while robots, and the same robot later, start from many places; and the door’s side of the room is narrow, against the top wall.

8.1 Backward search

The idea. Run the search from G, following each move backwards, until it reaches S. Our moves cost the same in both directions, so the backward search uses the same moves (LaValle §2.2.3). Its cost so far g at a cell is then the distance left from that cell to G, the true distance left h∗ of Section 5.1.

Run to the end, one backward search gives every cell’s distance to the door at once: a perfect heuristic for later searches, and the route for any start (CMU 16-350, heuristics). Many robots sharing one destination can use one search from the destination instead of one A* each (Patel, Variants).

The standard term. Searching from the goal towards the start, with every move reversed, is backward search ()Backward search: A search that starts at the goal and follows every move backwards until it reaches the start; it is cheaper when the goal’s side branches less, and run to the end it gives every state’s cost to the goal. Explained in §8.1 Backward search. (LaValle §2.2.3). Figure 9 (top) runs Dijkstra backwards from G: it reaches S after 31 cells, against 54 forwards (notebook).

Only 30 free cells lie within 3.414 m of G, against 53 within 3.414 m of S, because the door stands against the wall and much of the circle round it lies outside the room. A backward search pays when the goal’s side branches less than the start’s (LaValle §2.2.3).

The idea. Grow two searches at once, one from S and one from G, a step of each in turn; a route exists as soon as a cell has been reached by both. Each search then has to cover only about half the distance.

10. Trading length for time: weighted A* and anytime A*

Key point

Multiplying the heuristic by a weight ε above 1 makes A* rush towards the goal: fewer cells, and a route at most ε times the shortest. Anytime A* runs weighted A* with falling weights and always has a route ready: on the long trip, 9.243 m at once, then 8.414 m, then the shortest, 8.000 m.

A robot must often start moving before an exact search finishes, and a slightly longer route found at once is better than the best route found too late (CMU 16-350, anytime). The door trip is too easy to show the trade: every weight there returns the same 3.414 m. So we use the long trip of Section 3, where A* takes 43 cells to find 8.000 m.

11. Skipping equal routes: jump point search

Key point

On a grid where every move of one kind costs the same, many routes tie, and A* puts cells of all of them on its open list. Jump point search scans along straight lines and diagonals and puts on the open list only the cells where a route may have to turn: on the door trip, 7 cells ever enter its open list, against 23 for A*, for the same 3.414 m route.

In Figure 1, Dijkstra and A* returned two different routes of the same 3.414 m. On a grid with no obstacles in the way, the number of such equal routes between two cells grows fast, and A* treats each one as a separate possibility, putting its cells on the open list and keeping their numbers.

The idea. Equal routes differ only in the order of the same moves, such as up then up-right against up-right then up (zerowidth JPS). So from a cell, keep going in one direction for as long as nothing could make a turn necessary, without putting the cells passed on the open list. Stop only at the goal, or next to an obstacle’s corner, where a cell beside the line has just opened up and a route may have to turn into it.

The standard terms. A neighbour that the cell before could normally reach by an equally short route of its own, but which an obstacle beside the line leaves reachable by a shortest route only by turning at the current cell, is a forced neighbour ()Forced neighbour: A neighbour that the cell before could normally reach by an equally short route of its own, but which an obstacle beside the line of travel leaves reachable by a shortest route only by turning at the current cell; it makes that cell a jump point. Explained in §11. Skipping equal routes: jump point search.: the search is forced to look at it there.

A cell where a scan stops, because it is the goal or has a forced neighbour, is a jump point ()Jump point: A cell where a scan of jump point search stops because it is the goal or has a forced neighbour, a place where a route may have to turn; only jump points go on the open list. Explained in §11. Skipping equal routes: jump point search., and A* that puts only jump points on its open list is jump point search ()Jump point search (JPS): A* for grids with equal move costs that scans along straight lines and diagonals and puts only jump points on the open list; it skips equal routes that differ only in the order of their moves, and returns the same shortest route with far fewer open-list operations. Explained in §11. Skipping equal routes: jump point search., JPS (Harabor and Grastien 2011; zerowidth JPS).

A diagonal scan checks a straight scan in both of its directions at every step, and stops where one of them finds a jump point (zerowidth JPS). Our grid forbids cutting corners, so we use the version for that rule (Harabor and Grastien 2012). Figure 14 runs it on the door trip.

12. Routes at any angle: Theta*

Key point

A grid route can only turn by multiples of 45°, so it is longer than a straight line through free space. Theta* lets a cell take its parent’s parent as its own parent whenever the straight line between them is clear, which shortens the door route from 3.414 m to 3.193 m.

Every route so far, from Dijkstra’s to JPS’s, is made of moves along the grid: straight or at 45°. The 3.414 m route of Section 4 goes up-right, up, up, up, up-right, right, while a robot could drive one straight line from S to beside the door and then turn.

The idea. A* builds every route from parent links between neighbouring cells. If a cell’s parent can see the cell’s neighbour directly, along a straight line that crosses no blocked cell, the neighbour can link to that parent instead, and its route becomes one straight segment instead of a bend (Daniel et al. 2010).

The standard terms. Two cells can see each other when the straight segment between their centres crosses no blocked cell. Search whose routes may run at any angle between cell centres, not just along grid moves, is any-angle search ()Any-angle search: Search on a grid whose routes may run at any angle between cell centres instead of only along grid moves at 0° and 45°, so they are shorter and need fewer turns. Explained in §12.
