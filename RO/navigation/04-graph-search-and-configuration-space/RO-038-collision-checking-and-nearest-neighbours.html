1. Overview

Key point

A planner that never builds the obstacle region asks three questions thousands of times: is this configuration free, is this straight segment free, and which stored configuration is nearest? Cheap box tests first, a tree of boxes, well-ordered checks along a segment and a distance that knows headings wrap answer them fast and correctly.

In it, the robot’s disk with its centre at (3.5, 3.2) touches the table, and the straight drive from the start (2, 1) to the doorway centre (3.6, 4.0) is free. Three questions come back thousands of times, and on our room each has a trap (Figure 1):

  1. Is this configuration free? Testing the robot exactly against every obstacle for every configuration is slow: with 10,000 bodies there are 49,995,000 pairs to test (Müller SAP slides).
  2. Is this straight segment free? A segment holds infinitely many configurations. The segment from (2, 2) to (4, 3.6) is free at both ends and in the middle, yet the robot at (3.5, 3.2), three quarters of the way along, hits the table.
  3. Which stored configuration is nearest? Headings wrap round: a stored heading of 15° is 25° from a query at 350°, but plain subtraction says 335°, so a plain search returns the wrong neighbour.

This Note builds the tools that answer them, on the same room and the same 0.25 m disk:

  • cheap box tests before exact ones (Section 2), a tree of boxes (Section 3) and links of one robot that may hit each other (Section 4);
  • how far a robot is from an obstacle (Section 5) and how to check a whole segment (Section 6);
  • a distance for configurations with headings (Section 7), a nearest-neighbour search that knows headings wrap (Section 8), and random configurations that favour no direction (Section 9).

The first question of the opening problem: is the configuration with centre (3.5, 3.2) free? 2.2 Broad phase and narrow phase

Key point

Wrap each body in its smallest box with sides along x and y. Two bodies can only touch if their boxes overlap, and two boxes are compared with four number comparisons.

A box round a body is a quick stand-in for it: if two boxes do not overlap, the bodies inside cannot overlap either. Comparing two boxes takes four comparisons of numbers, far fewer steps than an exact test. So collision detection runs in two phases (Müller SAP slides; LaValle §5.3.1):

  1. the broad phase ()Broad phase: The first, cheap step of collision detection: wrap each body in a simple shape such as a box and keep only the pairs whose shapes overlap, so the costly exact test runs on few pairs. Explained in §2.2 Broad phase and narrow phase.: wrap every body in a simple shape and keep only the pairs whose shapes overlap;
  2. the narrow phase ()Narrow phase: The second step of collision detection: the exact overlap test on the pairs the broad phase kept, such as the distance from the robot’s centre to the table compared with its 0.25 m radius. Explained in §2.2 Broad phase and narrow phase.: test the kept pairs exactly.

A simple shape that encloses a body for such a quick test is a bounding volume ()Bounding volume: A simple shape that encloses a body, such as a sphere or a box; if two bounding volumes do not overlap, neither do the bodies, so it gives a quick test before an exact one. Explained in §2.2 Broad phase and narrow phase.: a sphere, a box, or a box turned with the body (Hauser ch.7). The commonest is the smallest box with sides along the x and y axes that holds the body, which we call the body’s box. Two such boxes overlap exactly when their x-intervals overlap and their y-intervals overlap (Hauser ch.7).

2.3 Sweep and prune

Testing the robot’s box against every other box is still a test per pair, 10 for our 5 bodies. Sweep and prune ()Sweep and prune: A broad phase that sorts the boxes by their left edge and sweeps along x, comparing each box only with the boxes it overlaps along x; it cuts the pairs tested, though long boxes that span the axis defeat it. Explained in §2.3 Sweep and prune. cuts that number by sorting (Müller SAP slides):

  1. sort the boxes by their left edge;
  2. sweep a vertical line from left to right: a box joins the list of active boxes when the line reaches its left edge and leaves at its right edge;
  3. a box that joins is compared only with the boxes already active, the ones it overlaps along x;
  4. for those pairs, a full box test checks y as well; a pair that passes goes to the narrow phase.

Sorting puts the boxes that might overlap next to each other, so each box looks only at its neighbours along x. The sort takes far fewer steps than testing all pairs (Müller SAP slides). With:

  • n the number of bodies, each in its box, now 10,000;
  • log2​n the number of times n can be halved before reaching 1, 13.3 for 10,000.
sort steps≈nlog2​n log2​10,000=13.3 10,000×13.3=133,000

The sort’s 133,000 steps are far below the 49,995,000 pairs, though how many pairs the sweep then compares depends on how the boxes lie (Müller SAP slides). Sweep and prune looks along one axis only, and a long wall’s box overlaps almost every other box along that axis.

3. Bounding-volume hierarchies

Key point

A bounding-volume hierarchy ()Bounding-volume hierarchy (BVH): A tree of nested bounding volumes, each holding its two children’s down to one per body; a query skips every body inside a box that misses, so it needs about log2​n box tests for n bodies (9 among the room’s 25). Explained in §3.1 Why a tree of boxes. is a tree of boxes: each box holds two smaller ones, down to one box per body. A query opens a box only if it overlaps the robot’s box, so for n bodies it reaches the few near the robot after about log2​n levels: 9 box tests among the room’s 25 bodies.

3.1 Why a tree of boxes

The walls were left out of the sweep. Put them in, and each wall’s box stretches along a whole side of the room, overlapping almost every box along x, so sorting along one axis separates little (Müller BVH slides). With many bodies spread in two or three dimensions the problem grows: for 100 × 100 objects in a plane, sweep and prune still tests about 100 objects for each one (Müller BVH slides).

The fix is to group nearby bodies, and groups of groups, each group in one box. A query starts at the box that holds everything; if the robot’s box misses a group’s box, every body inside is ruled out at once. A tree of nested bounding volumes, each holding the boxes of its two children down to one box per body, is a bounding-volume hierarchy ()Bounding-volume hierarchy (BVH): A tree of nested bounding volumes, each holding its two children’s down to one per body; a query skips every body inside a box that misses, so it needs about log2​n box tests for n bodies (9 among the room’s 25). Explained in §3.1 Why a tree of boxes., BVH for short (Hauser ch.7; Müller BVH slides).

So that each wall takes up only a small box, we store it as panels about 1 m long, as a triangle mesh ()Triangle mesh: A set of triangles, each given by three corner points in space, that together cover an object’s surface (a table as 12 triangles on 8 corners); fast to draw in 3D, but it does not say what is inside. Explained in: Maps and Landmarks, §5.1 Triangle meshes stores a surface in small pieces: 7 panels along the bottom, 4 up each side and 3 on each side of the doorway, 21 in all. With the 4 pieces of furniture, the room has 25 bodies.

3.2 Building and searching the tree

The tree is built from the top down (Müller BVH slides; Hauser ch.7):

  1. one box round all 25 bodies is the root;
  2. sort the bodies of a box by the centres of their boxes along x and split them into two halves of equal count (13 and 12 here); each half gets its own box;
  3. repeat inside each half, sorting along y, then x again, and so on, until each box holds one body.

A query for the robot’s box descends from the root (Hauser ch.7, “BVH queries”):

  1. test the box of the current node against the robot’s box;
  2. if they miss, skip the node and everything inside it;
  3. if they overlap and the node holds one body, pass that body to the narrow phase; otherwise test both children.

Figure 3 builds the tree level by level, then runs the query for the robot at (3.5, 3.2). Watch the room on the left and the tree on the right: a red box is a miss, and every body under it is skipped without a test. The stage “query, test 4” is the first miss: the box of the room’s lower-left walls, which the robot’s box does not reach. The last frame shows the 9 tests that leave one body, the table.

The query needed 9 box tests instead of 25 (notebook). From the start (2, 1), where nothing is close, it needs 11, and over 2,000 random centres in the room it averages 12.0. The tree has 5 levels below the root, because halving 25 bodies 4 times still leaves boxes with two bodies, and 5 times leaves one:

24=16<25≤32=25

In general, for a balanced tree:

  • n is the number of bodies, such as 32;
  • L is the number of levels below the root, 5 for 32 bodies.
n=2L L=log2​n

so a query that goes down one branch makes about log2​n box tests (Müller BVH slides). With 10,000 objects in a plane that is about 13 tests per object, against about 100 for sweep and prune and 10,000 for brute force (13.3 from Section 2.3); with 1,000,000 objects in 3D, about 20 against about 10,000 and 1,000,000 (Müller BVH slides):

log2​1,000,000=19.9

The count grows when the robot is close to many bodies at once, since then several branches must be opened (Hauser ch.7, “Performance”). A BVH is built once for bodies that do not move and reused for every query, which is why planners use it for the room’s furniture and walls. The robot itself, though, may be more than one body.

4. Self-collision

Key point

A robot made of several links can hit itself. A self-collision ()Self-collision: Two links of the same robot overlapping, such as an arm’s third link folding back across its first; a configuration with a self-collision is not free, so planners test the robot’s own link pairs too. Explained in §4. Self-collision. check tests the pairs of links that can meet; links joined by a joint touch all the time and are left out.

The first question, is this configuration free, has one more part for robots with several links. Our disk robot is one rigid body, so it cannot hit itself. An arm can: its links move relative to each other, and a far link can fold back onto a near one. We switch to a new example for this one section: a planar arm with three links, 1.0 m, 0.8 m and 0.6 m long, drawn as line segments, like the 2R armExplained in: Configuration Space and Degrees of Freedom, §3.2 Degrees of freedom of a robot made of links, the arm with two joints on the robot’s top plate, with a third link added. Its configuration is three joint angles, each measured from the direction of the link before it.

Figure 4 shows two configurations. At joint angles (0°, 90°, 90°) the arm folds into a hook and link 3 stays clear of link 1. At (0°, 150°, 150°) link 3 folds back across link 1, crossing it at (0.54, 0): a self-collision ()Self-collision: Two links of the same robot overlapping, such as an arm’s third link folding back across its first; a configuration with a self-collision is not free, so planners test the robot’s own link pairs too. Explained in §4. Self-collision., the robot’s own links overlapping (LaValle §4.3.1, “Obstacle region for multiple bodies”).

Figure 4: A planar arm with three links (1.0 m blue, 0.8 m orange, 0.6 m purple) on a base at the origin. Left: joint angles (0, 90, 90) degrees; links 1 and 3 do not meet. Right: joint angles (0, 150, 150) degrees; link 3 folds back across link 1 and crosses it at (0.54, 0), marked red: a self-collision

Which pairs to test. The pairs to test are the pairs of links that must not overlap (LaValle §4.3.1). Links next to each other share a joint, so they touch all the time and are left out (LaValle §4.3.1). For a chain of m links, the count of pairs is the one we used for bodies:

all pairs=2m(m−1)​ pairs joined by a joint=m−1

For our arm, with 3 links:

23×2​−2=1 pair: links 1 and 3

For an arm with 7 links:

27×6​−6=15 pairs

Some of the remaining pairs can never meet whatever the joint angles, and some always overlap; testing them wastes time. So robot software such as MoveIt’s Setup Assistant samples 10,000 random configurations by default and turns off checking for the pairs that are never in collision, always in collision, in collision in the robot’s default pose, or joined by a joint (MoveIt docs). For our arm, links 1 and 3 cross in 735 of 10,000 random configurations, 7.35 percent (notebook), so the pair stays on the list.

The self-collision pairs join the broad phase and the narrow phase like any other pair: a configuration is free only if no link hits an obstacle and no pair on the list overlaps (LaValle §4.3.1, eq. 4.36). Every check so far answers yes or no; a planner often needs to know by how much.

5.1 How far, not only whether

The second question, whole segments, needs more than yes or no. At the start (2, 1) the robot is free, but how much room does it have? A planner uses the answer to move fast where there is room and carefully near obstacles, and Section 6 uses it to clear whole segments at once. 5.2 The Minkowski difference and GJK

Right: the Minkowski difference robot minus table (orange), every robot point minus every table point; it does not contain the origin, and its nearest point to the origin is (−0.75, 0), 0.75 m away. For a disk the distance was one subtraction. For two bodies of any convex shape, the Minkowski difference ()Minkowski difference: The set of all differences of a point of one shape and a point of the other, A⊖B, which equals A⊕(−B); obstacle minus robot gives the obstacle region, and two convex bodies overlap exactly when their difference contains the origin. Explained in: Obstacles in C-space, §3.2 Minkowski sum and difference, every point of one shape minus every point of the other, gives a single rule. Take every point of the robot minus every point of the table:

  • if the two share a point, some difference is (0,0), so the difference contains the origin and the bodies overlap;
  • otherwise the distance between the bodies is the smallest length of a difference, which is the distance from the origin to the Minkowski difference (dyn4j GJK distance).

In Figure 5 (right), the orange shape is the robot at (2, 1) minus the table. The shape does not contain the origin, and its nearest point to the origin is (−0.75, 0): 0.75 m, the clearance found above.

Farthest points. Building the whole difference costs as much as building the obstacle regionExplained in: Obstacles in C-space, §4.3 Why planners do not build the obstacle region. The GJK algorithm ()GJK algorithm: The Gilbert-Johnson-Keerthi algorithm: it decides whether two convex shapes overlap, or how far apart they are, by searching their Minkowski difference for the origin using each shape’s farthest point in chosen directions, without building the difference. Explained in §5.2 The Minkowski difference and GJK., named after Gilbert, Johnson and Keerthi, never builds it (dyn4j GJK). GJK works with one point at a time: the point of a shape farthest in a chosen direction, found as the point with the largest dot product ()Dot product: Multiplying two vectors entry by entry and adding the products, uTx, which gives one number. It is large when the vectors point the same way, so it measures similarity and gives projections. Explained in: Dot Product and Cosine Similarity, §3. Computing the dot product with that direction, each coordinate times the direction’s, added (dyn4j GJK). With:

  • v the chosen direction, (1,0), from the robot towards the table;
  • −v the opposite direction, (−1,0).

The dot products of the table’s four corners (3, 1), (5, 1), (5.5, 2.5) and (3.5, 3) with −v are:

−3.0, −5.0, −5.5, −3.5

The largest is −3.0, so the table’s farthest point in the direction −v is the corner (3.0, 1.0). The robot’s farthest point in the direction v is its rim point straight ahead of the centre:

(2+0.25, 1)=(2.25, 1)

The farthest point of the difference in the direction v is the robot’s farthest point in v minus the table’s farthest point in −v (dyn4j GJK), because the dot product of a difference with v is the robot point’s dot product minus the table point’s, which is largest when the first is largest and the second smallest:

(2.25, 1)−(3.0, 1.0)=(−0.75, 0)

The point (−0.75, 0) is already the nearest point of the difference to the origin. In general GJK picks a direction towards the origin, adds the farthest point found there, and repeats with the triangle (in 3D, the tetrahedron) of points found so far, until a triangle encloses the origin (overlap) or no new point gets past the origin (apart); each step costs one farthest point per shape (dyn4j GJK). A bounding-volume hierarchy speeds up distance queries as it speeds up yes-or-no checks, as in the collision libraries PQP and FCL (Hauser ch.7). With distances in hand, the second question of the opening problem, whole segments, can be answered.

6. Checking a path segment

Key point

A segment cannot be checked point by point, so we check points at most a step apart: too large a step misses thin collisions, and checking in halving order (ends, middle, quarter points, …) finds a collision sooner. With clearances, a piece of segment is free once the free circles at its two ends meet.

6.1 A segment holds infinitely many configurations

The second question of the opening problem: is the straight segment from a = (2, 2) to b = (4, 3.6) free? Both ends are free and so is its middle, (3, 2.8). A planner cannot test every point, because a segment holds infinitely many configurations (LaValle §5.3.4), so it tests some points and hopes the rest are free. Checking a segment by testing points along it is edge resolution ()Edge resolution (segment checking): Checking a straight path segment in C-space for collisions by testing points along it, spaced at most a chosen step apart; too long a step can miss a thin collision, too short a step wastes time. Explained in §6.1 A segment holds infinitely many configurations., also called dynamic collision checking (Hauser ch.10).

The segment is 2.561 m long. The notebook finds its colliding stretch: from 0.620 to 0.777 of the way along, 0.403 m round the table’s corner, (3.5, 3.2) among it.

6.2 Step size and order

Step size. Split the segment into k equal steps no longer than a chosen step ε (Hauser ch.10, “Dynamic collision checking”):

  • d(a,b) is the segment’s length, 2.561 m;
  • ε is the longest step allowed, in m;
  • ⌈⋅⌉ rounds up to a whole number;
  • k is the number of steps; the checks are its k+1 end points.
k=⌈εd(a,b)​⌉

With ε=0.6 m:

k=⌈2.561/0.6⌉=⌈4.27⌉=5 step=2.561/5=0.512 m

The checks sit at 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1 of the way. The colliding stretch runs from 0.620 to 0.777, between the checks at 0.6 and 0.8, so all six checks are free and the segment wrongly passes. A step shorter than the thinnest obstacle a collision can hide in is needed; too short a step wastes time (Hauser ch.10; LaValle §5.3.4). 6.3 Checking with clearance

A step of 0.1 m is a guess: a thinner obstacle could still hide between two checks. Clearances remove the guess (Hauser ch.10). Around a configuration with clearance c, the robot can move up to c in any direction without touching anything, so the circle of radius c round it is free. If the free circles at the two ends of a piece of segment reach each other, the piece is covered and free. With:

  • a and b the two ends of a piece of segment;
  • d(a,b) its length, in m;
  • c(a) and c(b) the clearances at its ends, in m.
d(a,b)≤c(a)+c(b)

If the length passes this test, the piece is free. Otherwise, check its middle and repeat on both halves (Hauser ch.10, “Visible-Exact1”; KAIST proximity slides). 8. Nearest neighbours when headings wrap

Key point

A k-d tree search skips a cell when the cell is farther than the best point found so far. If headings are treated as numbers from 0 to 360, a cell just across 0° looks a long way off and is skipped; measuring the cell’s distance the short way round fixes the search.

The opening problem’s third trap, in a search tree. A k-d tree stores points in cells split one coordinate at a time, so a nearest-neighbour search can skip every cell that lies farther away than the best point found so far (Hauser ch.10, “k-d trees”; LaValle §5.5.2). The query is at x = 3.55 m, heading 350°. Distances use the weight of Section 7.3, 0.25 m per radian, and the heading axis is drawn in those metres, so a circle of equal distance is round.

Plain search. The search goes first into the cell that holds the query (Hauser ch.10). The search finds C and then D, at 0.371 m, and then reaches the cell of headings 0° to 175° beside it. Measured as plain numbers, that cell is 175° below the query:

350−175=175∘=3.054 rad 0.25×3.054=0.764 m

The gap of 0.764 m is more than the best so far, 0.371 m, so the cell is skipped, and with it E. The search ends at H (3.40 m, 320°), 0.199 m away. Wrong: E is nearer, at 0.120 m (Section 7.3).

Search with wrap-around. The heading 0° is the same as 360°, so the cell of headings 0° to 175° starts 10° above the query, across the top edge:

360−350=10∘=0.175 rad 0.25×0.175=0.044 m

Less than 0.371 m, so the cell is visited, and E, at 0.120 m, becomes the best. 9. Uniform random rotations and directions

Key point

A random heading must favour no direction: drawn evenly from 0° to 360°, every arc of 30° gets the same share, 1/12, wherever it lies. Directions and rotations in 3D need care: a uniform latitude angle crowds the poles, a point in a cube favours the corners; a normal sample scaled to length 1, the bread-slicer recipe and Shoemake’s recipe for 3D rotations are uniform.

9.1 What uniform means for headings

Sampling planners draw random configurations, so the random headings must not favour any direction. For the floor robot this is easy. Draw the heading from the continuous uniform distribution ()Continuous uniform distribution U(a,b): A continuous variable spread evenly between a and b, with density 1/(b−a). Explained in: Uniform and Log-normal Distributions, §2. The uniform distribution from 0° to 360°, every value equally likely. Then any arc of 30° gets the same share of the draws, wherever on the circle it lies (notebook, 100,000 draws: 8.4 percent for 10° to 40°, 8.3 percent for 200° to 230°):

30/360=0.083

Equal shares for equal arcs, wherever they lie, is what uniform means for rotations. A uniform random rotation ()Uniform random rotation: A random rotation drawn so that every orientation is equally likely: turning the whole set of samples by any fixed rotation does not make some orientations more common; for headings, a draw evenly from 0° to 360°. Explained in §9.1 What uniform means for headings. makes every orientation equally likely: turning the whole set of samples by any fixed rotation must not make some orientations more common (LaValle §5.1.4, §5.2.2). The circle of headings has no favoured place, so the plain uniform draw is right. Directions and rotations in 3D, as for a drone or the gripper of an arm, are where the traps are.

9.2 Random directions

A uniform random direction ()Uniform random direction: A random unit vector drawn so that every direction is equally likely; a normal sample scaled to length 1 gives one, while a point in a square or cube scaled to length 1 favours the diagonals. Explained in §9.2 Random directions. is a unit vector (a vector of length 1) drawn so that every direction is equally likely: every patch of the circle, or of the sphere, of the same size gets the same share (LaValle §5.2.2).

Two traps and two fixes, with the shares from the notebook (Figure 10):

  • A point in a square, scaled to length 1. Draw x and y evenly from −1 to 1 and divide by the length. The square reaches 1.41 times as far along a diagonal as along an axis, so a thin wedge along a diagonal holds about twice the area, and diagonal directions come up more often: within 5° of a diagonal 16.2 percent of the time, within 5° of an axis 8.6 percent, against a fair share of 11.1 percent (LaValle §5.2.2 warns of this bias towards a cube’s corners).
  • A normal sample, scaled to length 1. Draw each coordinate from a normal distribution with the same spread. Its density depends only on the distance from the centre, so every direction is equally likely: 11.1 percent near the diagonals and 11.1 percent near the axes (LaValle §5.2.2).
  • On the sphere, a uniform latitude angle. Drawing the latitude evenly from −90° to 90° packs as many points into the short circles near the poles as into the long equator. The two caps above height 0.9 and below −0.9 hold 10 percent of the sphere’s surface but get 28.7 percent of the points.
  • On the sphere, a uniform height: the bread slicer. Drawing the height evenly from −1 to 1 and the longitude evenly from 0° to 360° is uniform, because slices of a sphere of equal thickness have equal crust area, like a loaf through a bread slicer (LaValle §5.2.2). The caps get 10.1 percent.

The caps by the two recipes, the height 0.9 being at latitude 64.2°:

arcsin0.9=64.2∘ latitude: 2×(90−64.2)/180=0.287 height: 2×(1−0.9)/2=0.1

The latitude recipe gives each degree of latitude the same share; the height recipe gives each slice of height the same share, which is right because equal slices have equal area.

9.3 Random rotations in 3D

A rotation in 3D, such as the orientation of a gripper, can be stored as four numbers h=(h1​,h2​,h3​,h4​) whose squares add up to 1, so h is a point on a sphere in four dimensions (LaValle §5.2.2):

h12​+h22​+h32​+h42​=1

  • u1​, u2​, u3​ three numbers drawn evenly from 0 to 1;

0.242+0.458=0.700=1−0.3 0.104+0.196=0.300=0.3 0.700+0.300=1
