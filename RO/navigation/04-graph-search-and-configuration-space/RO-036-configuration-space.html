1. Overview

Key point

The routes of the earlier Notes move a point from cell to cell, but the robot is a body with a size and a heading, and placing it takes three numbers, (x, y, θ). The numbers that place every point of a robot are its configuration; all configurations together form the configuration space, and planning a motion means finding a path through that space.

The robot can now plan a task. The task plannerExplained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §5. Forward search: finding the plan of the cup task returns four actions, and each move is a route on the 0.5 m grid, such as the shortest route from the start cell to the cell just inside the door, 3.414 m long (routes of the planExplained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §5.3 From the plan back to routes).

What still breaks is the robot itself (Figure 1). The route moves a point from cell centre to cell centre. The real robot is a body: seen from above, a disk of radius 0.25 m centred on the middle of its axle, big enough to hold the 0.2 m axle and the wheels. At the start it stands at (2 m, 1 m) and faces 30°. Two problems follow:

  • the two numbers (x, y) say where the centre is, but not which way the robot faces, and a robot facing the table drives off differently from one facing the door;
  • the heading wraps round: 350° and 10° are only 20° apart, so a planner that treats the heading as an ordinary number thinks the two are 340° apart.

Figure 1: The room with the table, the cabinet and the two poles. Orange: a route of the graph-search Notes, a point moved from cell centre to cell centre towards the door. Red: the robot, a disk of radius 0.25 m centred at (2, 1), with an arrow for its heading of 30 degrees measured from the x-axis

In this Note we answer three questions:

  1. Which numbers place the whole robot, and how many are needed (Section 2 and Section 3)?
  2. What shape do all those numbers form, and how do we write them down without a jump at 360° (Section 4 and Section 5)?
  3. What is the planning problem in that space (Section 6)?

A second robot joins the floor robot in this Note: an arm with two joints on the robot’s top plate, the kind of arm that picks up the cup. Each of its two joints turns all the way round, so it shows a shape that the floor robot’s single angle cannot (the 2R arm of MR §2.3.1, R for a revolute joint). Two facts from earlier work are used throughout. A robot arm is a chain of rigid links joined by joints, and a revolute joint lets one link turn about the other. And once the position and heading of a rigid body are known, the position of each of its points follows by a rotation by the heading and a shift by the position.

2. Configuration and configuration space

Key point

The first question is which numbers place the whole robot. For the floor robot, the centre (x, y) and the heading θ fix every point of its body, so (2, 1, 0.524) is its configuration; all configurations together form the configuration space, a box whose top face and bottom face are the same headings.

2.1 The configuration: numbers that place every point

The route gives two numbers, the (x, y) of a point. The robot’s body is rigid: every point of it keeps its distance from every other point. So once we know where the centre is and which way the robot faces, every other point of the body is fixed: rotate the point by the heading, then shift it by the centre’s position.

Take the front point of the body, 0.25 m ahead of the centre:

  • x, y are the centre’s position, 2 m and 1 m;
  • θ is the heading, 30°, which is 0.524 rad;
  • a is how far the point lies ahead of the centre, in the robot’s own frame, 0.25 m;
  • xf​, yf​ are the front point’s position in the room, in m.

The point lies a along the heading, and a length along the heading splits into a run along x and a rise along y, the same split as the robot’s speed in cosine and sine partsExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.1 Why the speed splits into cosine and sine parts:

xf​=x+acosθ =2+0.25×0.8660 =2+0.2165=2.2165 m yf​=y+asinθ =1+0.25×0.5=1.125 m

Any other point of the body works the same way, so three numbers fix all of them. So the C-space of the floor robot is a box with its top and bottom glued together. The box needs three numbers per point. Section 3 asks whether three is the right count, and how to count for any robot.

3. Degrees of freedom

Key point

How many numbers are needed, the rest of the first question, is a robot’s degrees of freedom, the smallest number of numbers that places it: 3 for the floor robot and 2 for the arm. We count them as freedoms minus independent constraints, point by point for one body (2, 1 and 0 freedoms for three of its points), and with Grübler’s formula for links joined by joints.

3.1 Counting point by point

Section 2 placed the floor robot with three numbers. A body has endless points, each with two numbers of its own in the plane, so why exactly three? We count point by point (MR §2.1), on three points of the robot’s body:

  • A, the centre, at (2, 1);
  • B, 0.25 m ahead of A, at (2.2165, 1.125), the front point of Section 2.1;
  • C, 0.25 m to the left of A, at (1.875, 1.217), and 0.354 m from B.

Figure 4 places them one at a time. Watch the table on the right fill in, one row per point:

  • frame “A, the centre”: A can be anywhere in the plane: 2 numbers, no rule, so 2 freedoms;
  • frame “B stays 0.25 m from A”: B has 2 numbers, but one rule, its fixed distance to A, keeps it on the orange circle round A. Only its place on the circle is free, 1 freedom, and sliding B round the circle turns the body;
  • frame “C stays 0.25 m from A and 0.354 m from B”: two rules keep C on two circles, which meet in only 2 points, and one of them is the body flipped upside down, which a floor robot cannot do. So C is fixed: 0 freedoms.

Figure 4: Close-up of the robot’s body, a grey disk of radius 0.25 m at (2, 1), with a table of ‘numbers − rules = freedoms’. Frame ‘A, the centre’: A (blue) at the centre, row A: 2 − 0 = 2. Frames ‘B stays 0.25 m from A’: B (orange) slides round the orange dashed circle of radius 0.25 m about A, and the red line from A to B turns with it, its direction being the heading θ; row B: 2 − 1 = 1. Frame ‘C stays 0.25 m from A and 0.354 m from B’: the purple dashed circle about B meets the circle about A at C and at a grey cross marked ‘upside down’; row C: 2 − 2 = 0. Last frame: total 2 + 1 + 0 = 3, the three numbers (x, y, θ); the same count in space, 3 + 2 + 1 = 6

Any further point is fixed by its distances to A, B and C, so it adds nothing. The total:

2+1+0=3

A’s two numbers are x and y, and B’s place on its circle is the heading θ. The smallest number of real numbers needed to place a robot is its degrees of freedom ()Degrees of freedom (of a robot): The smallest number of real numbers that places a robot, which is also the number of dimensions of its C-space: the freedoms of its points minus the independent constraints on them, such as 3 for a floor robot (x,y,θ) and 2 for a 2R arm; it tells a planner how many numbers it must search over. Explained in §3.1 Counting point by point., DOF for short; it is also the number of dimensions of its C-space, 3 for the box of Figure 2 (MR Definition 2.1).

The general rule (MR eq. 2.1):

  • F is the sum of the freedoms of the points, before any rule;
  • c is the number of independent constraints, rules that fix how the points may lie relative to each other;
  • DOF is the degrees of freedom.
DOF=F−c

For the three points of the body in the plane, and then the same body in space, where each point has 3 numbers:

F=3×2=6 DOF=6−3=3 F=3×3=9 DOF=9−3=6

Point by point, as in the last frame of Figure 4, the body in space gives the same 6: A has 3 freedoms, B 2 and C 1.

3+2+1=6

A body that moves in space, such as a flying drone, has 6 degrees of freedom: 3 numbers for its position and 3 angles (MR §2.1).

“Independent” matters. A fourth point D of the body in the plane has 3 distance rules, to A, B and C, but once two of them hold the third holds too, so only 2 are independent, and D’s 2 numbers minus its 2 independent rules leave it no freedom (MR §2.1). A rule that follows from the others removes nothing.

The wheels’ rule of RO-001, that the robot cannot slide sideways, is not a constraint of this kind. The rule limits which way the robot can move at each momentExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §6.2 Can the robot still get everywhere?, not where it can be, so the floor robot keeps all 3 degrees of freedom (MR §2.4). Counting point by point works for one body; a robot of several links needs a quicker count.

3.2 Degrees of freedom of a robot made of links

The arm of the Overview is our example of a robot made of several links: link 1 turns on the robot’s top plate at joint 1, and link 2 turns on link 1 at joint 2 (Figure 5, left). Both are revolute joints.

  • Each link moving freely in the plane would have 3 freedoms, like the robot’s body in Section 3.1.
  • A revolute joint lets one link only turn about the other: it leaves 1 of the 3 freedoms and so removes 2 (MR §2.2.1).

Figure 5: Left: the 2R arm on the robot. The robot’s top plate is the ground (link 0); link 1 (blue) turns about joint 1 at angle theta1, link 2 (orange) turns about joint 2 at angle theta2, ending at the hand; joints are open circles. N = 3 links with the ground, J = 2 revolute joints: 3 (3 − 1 − 2) + 2 = 2. Right: a closed loop, three links (blue, orange, purple) and the ground joined end to end by 4 revolute joints. N = 4, J = 4: 3 (4 − 1 − 4) + 4 = 1

For the arm, the count goes:

  • m is the number of freedoms of one body, 3 in the plane;
  • N is the number of links, counting the ground (here the top plate) as a link: 3; the ground does not move, so only the other 2 links have freedoms to lose;
  • J is the number of joints, 2;
  • fi​ is the number of freedoms joint i leaves, 1 for a revolute joint;
  • ∑ means add upExplained in: Measures of Central Tendency, §3. Mean, here over the joints i from 1 to J.

Our numbers first, each followed by the same step in symbols:

DOF=3×2−(3−1)−(3−1) DOF=m(N−1)−i=1∑J​(m−fi​) =3×2−3×2+1+1 =m(N−1)−mJ+i=1∑J​fi​ =3(3−1−2)+2=2 =m(N−1−J)+i=1∑J​fi​

The last line is Grübler’s formula ()Grübler’s formula: The count of a mechanism’s degrees of freedom from its links and joints, m(N−1−J)+∑fi​ (m = 3 in the plane, N links with the ground, J joints, fi​ freedoms of joint i), such as 2 for a 2R arm; it holds when the joints’ constraints are independent. Explained in §3.2 Degrees of freedom of a robot made of links. (MR eq. 2.4). The arm has 2 degrees of freedom, its two joint angles (θ1​,θ2​).

Two checks:

  • The floor robot is one body and no joint, N = 2 (the floor and the body) and J = 0: the formula gives 3 (first line below), as in Section 3.1.
  • The closed loop of Figure 5, right, has 3 moving links joined end to end, with both ends pinned to the ground, so 4 joints, and the formula gives 1 (second line below). Pinning the end of the third link to the ground removes 2 more freedoms (MR §2.2.2).
3(2−1−0)+0=3 3(4−1−4)+4=1

The formula holds only when the joints’ constraints are independent; when some of them follow from the others, the true count is higher than the formula says (MR §2.2.2). The robot carrying the arm has the 3 degrees of freedom of its body plus the 2 of the arm, 5 in all.

The counts say how many numbers place each robot: 3 for the floor robot, 2 for the arm. They do not say what shape those numbers form, and both of the arm’s angles wrap round, which Section 4 takes up.

4. The shape of a configuration space

Key point

The second question is what shape all those numbers form: a heading lives on a circle, not on a line, and the arm’s two angles live on a torus, the surface of a doughnut. Two spaces have the same shape when one can be bent or stretched into the other without cutting or gluing.

4.1 Same shape: bending allowed, cutting and gluing not

Section 3 counted the numbers, but the count is not the whole story. The heading is one number, like the position of a cart on a rail. Yet on a rail, 3.5 m and 0.1 m are 3.4 m apart, while the headings 350° and 10° are 20° apart. The difference is the shape: a heading’s values close up into a circle, while a rail’s values run along a line with nothing joining the ends.

Figure 6 makes “same shape” precise. Watch the circle of headings in the frames “stretch and bend the circle”: it becomes an ellipse and a squashed loop, but it stays one closed loop, so it is still the same shape. In the frames “cut at θ = 0 and open it out”, the circle has to be cut before it can be laid straight, and the cut makes two ends that the circle did not have.

Figure 6: The circle of headings, with θ = π marked green and θ = 0 = 2π marked orange. Frames ‘stretch and bend the circle’: it becomes an ellipse and then a squashed loop, still closed: no cut, no glue, the same shape. Frames ‘cut at θ = 0 and open it out’: the circle opens at θ = 0 into an arc and then a straight segment, with red ends marked θ = 0 and θ = 2π. Last frame: a segment from 0 to 2π, not the same shape as the circle: two ends the circle did not have

Two spaces are topologically equivalent ()Topologically equivalent: Said of two spaces when one can be deformed into the other by bending and stretching, without cutting or gluing, such as a circle and an ellipse, or a coffee mug and a doughnut; equivalent spaces have the same shape for planning. Explained in §4.1 Same shape: bending allowed, cutting and gluing not. when one can be deformed into the other by bending and stretching, without cutting or gluing (MR §2.3.1). A coffee mug and the surface of a doughnut are equivalent this way: the handle’s hole is the doughnut’s hole. Three shapes of one dimension appear in robots (MR §2.3.1):

  • the circle, written S1: a heading, or a revolute joint that turns all the way round;
  • the line, written R, all real numbers: a sliding joint with no end stops;
  • the closed interval, written [a,b], the numbers from a to b with both ends included: a sliding joint with end stops, such as one that slides from 0 to 0.5 m.

None of the three is equivalent to another: the circle has no end, the line has no end but does not close up, and the interval has two ends. Our robots have more than one number, so their spaces are built from these shapes.

4.2 Building bigger spaces: the Cartesian product

The floor robot has three numbers: x and y each run along a line, and θ runs round a circle. What shape do the three make together? Every choice of (x, y) can be combined with every heading, so the space holds every pair of a point of the plane with a point of the circle. Taking every pair of one point from each of two spaces makes a new space, their Cartesian product ()Cartesian product (of spaces): The space X×Y of every pair made of one point of X and one point of Y, with as many dimensions as both together, such as R2×S1 for a floor robot’s position and heading; it builds a robot’s C-space from the spaces of its parts. Explained in §4.2 Building bigger spaces: the Cartesian product., written with ×; its number of dimensions is the sum of theirs (MR §2.3.1; LaValle §4.1.2). In symbols:

  • R2×S1 is every pair of a point (x, y) with a heading θ, such as (2, 1, 0.524).

4.3 Manifolds: every small patch looks flat

Section 4.2 built curved and wrapped spaces, but a robot moves in small steps. What does a C-space look like close to one configuration? Figure 8 zooms in on one point of three shapes. A small piece of the circle looks like a piece of a straight line, and a small patch of the torus looks like a piece of a flat plane. At the joint of a T, three pieces of line meet, which no single line looks like.

A space in which the neighbourhood of every point looks like a piece of flat space of the same number of dimensions, a line for one dimension, a plane for two, is a manifold ()Manifold: A space in which a small neighbourhood of every point looks like a piece of flat space of the same dimension (a line, a plane), such as a circle or a torus but not a T-junction; near any configuration a robot can then move a little in each of its directions. Explained in §4.3 Manifolds: every small patch looks flat. (LaValle §4.1.2). Near any point of a manifold, “the set of directions that one can move appears the same” as in flat space (LaValle §4.1.2), so a robot at any configuration can move a little in each of its directions, 3 for the floor robot. The C-spaces of this Note are all manifolds: the circle, and the cylinder, the torus and the floor robot’s R2×S1, which are built as Cartesian products of manifolds (LaValle §4.1.2).

A manifold is flat only up close. Its overall shape, the circle closing up or the torus wrapping round in two directions, still matters as soon as we write a configuration down as numbers, which is the subject of Section 5.

5. Writing a configuration as numbers

Key point

The rest of the second question is how to write a configuration down without a jump at 360°. The fewest numbers, θ for a heading and (θ1, θ2) for the arm, jump at 2π, because the circle and the torus must be cut open to lay them flat; more numbers with a rule, such as (cos θ, sin θ) with cos² θ + sin² θ = 1, never jump.

5.1 Cutting the torus into a square

The arm’s configurations form a torus, but a program stores two numbers, θ1​ and θ2​, each from 0 to 2π. Those pairs fill a square. Figure 9 shows how the square and the torus are related. Watch the frame “roll it until the orange edges meet”: the square rolls into a tube, because θ2​ = 0 and θ2​ = 2π are the same angle of joint 2. In the frames “stretch and bend the tube”, the tube bends round until its blue ends, θ1​ = 0 and θ1​ = 2π, meet. Run backwards, the film cuts the torus twice to lay it flat as the square (MR §2.3.1).

5.2 More numbers, no jump: the implicit representation

The jump of Section 5.1 in numbers, for the heading, which turns 2° from 359° to 1°:

  • θ is the explicit coordinate, in rad, from 0 to 2π.
θ(359∘)=6.26573 θ(1∘)=0.01745 0.01745−6.26573=−6.24828 rad

A turn of 0.0349 rad shows up as a change of −6.24828 rad. The fix is to write the heading as the point where its direction meets a circle of radius 1. Two numbers with one rule:

  • cosθ and sinθ are the two numbers, the point’s distance across and up from the circle’s centre;
  • the rule is that the point lies on the circle of radius 1.
cos2θ+sin2θ=1

By the rule of Section 3.1, 2 numbers minus 1 independent rule leave 1 degree of freedom, as a heading should. At the same two headings (notebook):

(cos359∘,sin359∘)=(0.9998,−0.0175) (cos1∘,sin1∘)=(0.9998,0.0175)

The point moves 0.035, the size of the 2° turn, and nothing jumps. The same pair settles the second problem of the Overview, the headings 350° and 10°. As explicit numbers they lie far apart:

θ(350∘)−θ(10∘) =6.10865−0.17453=5.93412 rad

As implicit pairs they lie close together:

(cos350∘,sin350∘)=(0.98481,−0.17365) (cos10∘,sin10∘)=(0.98481,0.17365) 0.17365−(−0.17365)=0.34730

The two points differ only in their second number, sinθ, by 0.3473, about the 0.349 rad of a 20° turn, so a planner that compares the pairs sees two nearby headings. Watch the two plots on the right: in the frame “heading 360° = 0°” the explicit θ drops from 2π to 0, while cos θ and sin θ move on smoothly; the last frame shows one jump in θ and none in the other two.

Using more numbers than the degrees of freedom, tied by rules, is an implicit representation ()Implicit representation: Writing a space with more numbers than its dimension, tied by rules, such as a heading as (cosθ,sinθ) with cos2θ+sin2θ=1; it costs extra numbers but has no jumps and no bad points such as a pole. Explained in §5.2 More numbers, no jump: the implicit representation. (MR §2.3.2). Its cost is extra numbers; its gain is that it has no jumps and no bad points anywhere. Modern Robotics uses implicit representations throughout for that reason (MR §2.3.2).

An explicit parametrization can be worse than a jump. Latitude and longitude on the surface of a ball misbehave near the North Pole (MR §2.3.2). Walking 1 m east changes the longitude by the angle of a 1 m arc on the circle round the pole, which is arc divided by radius, the arc ruleExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.3 Why angles are measured in radians of RO-001:

  • ρ is the distance from the pole, in m;
  • Δλ is the change in longitude for 1 m walked, in rad.
Δλ=1/ρ ρ=10:Δλ=0.1 rad=5.7∘ ρ=1:Δλ=1 rad=57.3∘

Figure 12 draws the two walks. Watch the two thick arcs: each stands for 1 m walked (the figure is not to scale), but the orange one, 1 m from the pole, turns the longitude ten times as far as the blue one. The closer to the pole, the faster the longitude changes, without any limit, and at the pole itself every longitude names the same point.

Figure 12: Near the North Pole, seen from above, not to scale. Blue: on the circle 10 m from the pole, 1 m walked east changes the longitude by 5.7 degrees. Orange: on the circle 1 m from the pole, 1 m walked changes it by 57.3 degrees. At the pole itself every longitude names the same point

The sphere looks the same everywhere, at the pole as on the equator, so the trouble is in the numbers, not in the space (MR §2.3.2). The implicit representation, three numbers for the point’s position in space with one rule, that its distance from the centre is 1, has no such bad point (MR §2.3.2). Robots meet the sphere as the C-space of a pendulum that can swing in every direction (MR §2.3.1), and planners meet the heading’s jump whenever they measure how far apart two configurations are, which needs the distance between two headingsExplained in: Collision Checking and Nearest Neighbours in C-space, §7.2 The distance between two headings measured the short way round.

Every configuration can now be written down as numbers, with or without jumps. What is left is the question the C-space was built for: how to plan a motion through it.

6. The basic motion planning problem

Key point

The third question is the planning problem itself: find a path in the C-space from the start configuration to the goal configuration along which the robot touches no obstacle. The question is the basic motion planning problem, also called the piano mover’s problem.

We can now restate the opening problem. The robot’s body is a disk with a heading, and every placement of it is one point of the box of Figure 2. The robot starts at the configuration (2, 1, 0.524) and should end in the doorway, with its centre at the doorway’s centre, (3.6, 4.0). The grid routes stopped at the centre of the cell under the door, (3.75, 3.75), because a grid route can only stop at a cell centre; a configuration can be any point, so from here on the goal is the doorway’s centre.

Figure 13 plays the simplest motion that does it, in the room and in the C-space at once:

  1. turn on the spot from 30° to 61.9°, to face the doorway’s centre;
  2. drive 3.4 m straight to (3.6, 4.0).

The turn and the drive come from how far the doorway’s centre lies along x and along y:

  • Δx is the run of the drive, in m;
  • Δy is its rise, in m;
  • atan2 ()atan2: A function atan2(rise, run) that returns the direction of a line over the full circle from its rise and run given separately; needed because the plain inverse tangent of the ratio cannot tell opposite directions apart. Explained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §4.2 Turn, drive, turn turns the rise and the run into the direction of the drive.
Δx=3.6−2.0=1.6 m Δy=4.0−1.0=3.0 m length=1.62+3.02​=3.4 m heading=atan2(3.0, 1.6)=61.9∘

The robot ends facing along its drive, so the goal configuration is (3.6, 4.0, 1.081), with 61.9° written as 1.081 rad. Watch the right panel of Figure 13. In the frames “turn on the spot”, only θ changes, so the point moves straight up. In the frames “drive straight”, θ stays the same, so the point moves level. The last frame shows the whole motion as one connected path from the grey start point to the green goal point. Along it, the robot’s centre keeps at least 0.382 m from every obstacle and wall, passing closest to the table’s corner, so the 0.25 m body clears the table by 0.132 m (notebook; obstacles in C-spaceExplained in: Obstacles in C-space, §2.4 The room in C-space, and the drive to the door shows the same drive in its C-space):

0.382−0.25=0.132 m

Figure 13: The motion to the doorway. Left: the room seen from above, the robot (red disk with heading arrow) and its orange trail. Right: the configuration space box with the same motion as an orange path. First frame: the robot at q start = (2, 1, 0.524), one grey point. Last frame: the robot at q goal = (3.6, 4.0, 1.081), the green point, its centre at the doorway’s centre; the whole motion is one path in the box

A motion of the robot is a path in its C-space: a continuous curve of configurations, one for each moment, from the start to the goal (LaValle §4.3.1). The robot touches no obstacle exactly when every configuration on the path is one in which the body overlaps nothing; the set of such configurations is the free space ()Free space: All configurations at which the robot touches no obstacle, Cfree​, the C-space without the obstacle region; a planner looks for a path that stays inside it. Explained in: Obstacles in C-space, §2.2 The obstacle region: every placement that touches an obstacle, which the next Note builds. In symbols:

  • C is the configuration space, the box of Figure 2 for the floor robot;
  • Cfree​ is the part of C in which the robot touches no obstacle;
  • qstart​ is the start configuration, (2, 1, 0.524);
  • qgoal​ is the goal configuration, (3.6, 4.0, 1.081);
  • s is how far along the path we are, from 0 at the start to 1 at the end;
  • τ(s) is the configuration at s; the whole curve τ is the path in configuration space;
  • ∈ means “is one of”: τ(s)∈Cfree​ says the configuration at s is free.

The planner must find a path with

τ(0)=qstart​ τ(1)=qgoal​ τ(s)∈Cfree​ for every s

or report correctly that no such path exists (LaValle §4.3.1, Formulation 4.1; MR §10.1). The task of finding such a path is the basic motion planning problem ()Basic motion planning problem (piano mover’s problem): Given a robot, its obstacles, a start configuration and a goal configuration, find a continuous path in C-space between them through configurations where the robot touches no obstacle, or report that none exists; it states every collision-free motion task as one search through C-space. Explained in §6. The basic motion planning problem.. The problem is also called the piano mover’s problem (MR §10.1; LaValle §4.3.1): moving a piano through a tight doorway is pure geometry, and the piano often has to turn on the way, so its heading is part of the path just as the robot’s θ is. Solving it exactly is very hard in general: it is PSPACE-hard ()PSPACE-hard: Said of a problem at least as hard as every problem solvable with memory growing like a power of the input size (PSPACE, which contains NP); the general motion planning problem and Sokoban are PSPACE-hard, which is why practical planners give up exactness. Explained in: Graphs and Uninformed Search, §8. Why exact planning is hard: NP-hard and PSPACE-hard, at least as hard as every problem that a computer can solve with memory growing like a power of the input size, because the number of C-space dimensions grows with the robot (LaValle §4.3.1).

The opening question has its answer: the robot’s motion to the door is a path through a three-dimensional C-space, from (2, 1, 0.524) to (3.6, 4.0, 1.081), and the route of the earlier Notes was its shadow on the floor. What is still unknown is which part of the box is free.
