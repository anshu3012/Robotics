1. Overview

Key point

A list of actions found from facts alone can ask the arm for something the table does not allow, such as putting the cup down where a bottle stands. Task and motion planning searches for the actions and for their numbers (places, joint angles, motions) together, and a language model can choose skills from a request in words if each skill’s score is multiplied by the chance the robot can carry it out now.

The robot can already do three things. Its task plannerExplained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §5. Forward search: finding the plan puts actions in order from true-or-false facts, such as “pick up the cup, then put it down at the door”. Its arm can plan the transit and transfer motionsExplained in: Manipulation Planning, §2.3 The plan: transit, transfer, transit that carry a cup from spot A to spot B on a table. And Nav2 ()Nav2: The navigation stack of ROS 2: separate programs (a lifecycle manager, AMCL, costmaps, a behaviour tree navigator, planner, controller and behaviour servers, a velocity smoother and a collision monitor) that together take a goal pose and drive a robot there. Explained in: Nav2, Start to Finish, §1. Overview, the navigation stack of this chapter, drives its base to any pose in the room. The three are planned separately, and the task planner never looks at the table.

So we return to the table top of manipulation planningManipulation planning: Planning the motions of a robot together with the objects it moves, as a sequence of transit and transfer motions joined at transition configurations, each planned in its own free space. Explained in: Manipulation Planning, §1. Overview, where the actions meet the geometry: the arm with links of 0.3 m and 0.2 m, its shoulder 0.1 m above the table, the cup (6 cm wide, 8 cm tall) at A, 0.18 m from the shoulder. The bottle (4 cm wide, 14 cm tall) has been left standing on spot B, 0.42 m out. The request: put the cup on spot B.

The facts say the cup is at A and the hand is empty. From these facts the task planner returns two actions, and Figure 1 checks them against the table:

  1. pick up the cup at A: the arm finds a motion to the cup’s lid, 154.6 degrees of joint motion;
  2. put the cup down at B: the cup at B would overlap the bottle by 4 cm, the bottle’s whole width, so no placement exists.

Figure 1: Animation: the task planner’s two-action plan checked against the table by our Python planner (tampkit.py, not PDDLStream), side view. The arm (blue and brown links) starts straight up; the cup (orange) stands at A, the bottle (green) on spot B (dashed outline). Action 1, pick up the cup at A: the planner searches for the arm’s motion to the cup’s lid and finds one, 154.6 degrees of joint motion (the dotted line is the hand’s track). Action 2, put the cup down at B: the cup drawn at B (red) would overlap the bottle by 4 cm, so the plan fails

The plan fails at its second action, after the first motion has already been planned, because nothing in the facts states that the bottle stands on B. There is a second problem: a person does not give the robot facts, but a request in words, “put the cup on spot B”.

The opening question of this Note: how does the robot find a list of actions that the arm can carry out on this table, and how does it choose those actions from a request in words? We answer it in steps:

  • why the list of actions fails, and what numbers each action hides (Section 2);
  • searching for the actions and their numbers together on the cup and the bottle (Section 3);
  • the code base that does this on real robots, PDDLStream (Section 4);
  • a language model choosing skills from the request, the way SayCan does (Section 5).

Every run in this Note is real code in the notebook RO-081-task-and-motion-planning.ipynb: the planner in images/tampkit.py (extended from the arm model of manipulation planning) and GPT-2 small for the language scores. Every number comes from its output.

2. Why the list of actions fails: the numbers inside each action

Key point

The two-action plan of the Overview fails because “put the cup down at B” hides numbers: where the cup will stand, the joint angles that put the hand there, and the motion. Each action is a skill whose numbers must pass tests (on the table, free of other objects, within the arm’s reach, a free motion) before the action can run.

2.1 What the facts leave out

The Overview’s plan broke at “put the cup down at B” with a 4 cm overlap. The facts the task planner works from are STRIPS-style facts ()Fact (task planning): A statement about the task that is either true or false, such as robot-at table or holding-cup; facts are what a planner’s states, actions and goals are made of. Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §2.1 Facts and states, true-or-false statements such as cup-at A and hand-empty. The precondition ()Precondition: The facts that must be true for an action to be done, such as robot-at table, cup-at table and hand-empty for picking up the cup; the planner tries an action only in states that hold all of them. Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §3.1 Precondition, add list and delete list of a put-down is holding cup, and that fact holds after the pick-up, so the task planner sees nothing wrong.

What decides whether the put-down can run is geometry: the cup, 6 cm wide, needs 6 cm of table at B, and the bottle fills 4 cm of it. The facts carry no widths and no positions. So the task planner assumes that every list of actions it finds can be carried out by the arm. The first planners, on the Shakey robot, made the same assumption (Garrett §3.3.1); on a table with objects it rarely holds, because whether one action can run depends on where earlier actions left things (Garrett §1.1; Robohub TAMP).

A motion planner alone cannot solve this either. A motion planner moves the arm from one configuration to another but never changes where the objects stand; the mode ()Mode (manipulation): Which kind of motion applies, transit or transfer, together with where the object stands or how it is held; each mode has its own free space. Explained in: Manipulation Planning, §2.1 Two kinds of motion, two free spaces, which objects stand where and what the hand holds, changes only at a grasp or a release (Garrett §2.2). Moving the bottle off B is a change of mode, and choosing to make it is a decision about actions. So the robot needs both searches at once: one over actions, one over the numbers inside them.

2.2 A skill and its numbers

Each action the robot can carry out on command, such as “pick up the bottle”, with the preconditions it needs and a motion that carries it out, is a skill ()Skill: An action a robot can carry out on command, such as “pick up the bottle”, with the preconditions it needs and a motion that carries it out; planners and language models build tasks out of skills. Explained in §2.2 A skill and its numbers. (Ahn et al. §3). A pick-up skill hides four numbers. Figure 2 marks them for “pick up the bottle” on our table:

  • x is where the object stands, the distance of its centre from the shoulder along the table: 0.42 m for the bottle;
  • the grasp is where the hand holds it: the suction gripper holds a flat top, so the one grasp is the centre of the bottle’s cap, at (0.42, 0.04) m;
  • q is the arm’s configuration, its two joint angles, that puts the hand on the grasp: (31.2, −66.4) degrees;
  • the motion is a free path of the arm from where it is to q: here 88.7 degrees of joint motion from home, found by the probabilistic roadmap ()Probabilistic roadmap (PRM): A graph of random free configurations (milestones) joined by straight edges that pass a collision check, built once and then searched with A*; it describes the free space without building the obstacle region, so it scales to robots with many joints. Explained in: Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM), §2.2 Phase 1 and phase 2: milestones and edges.

Figure 2: The skill “pick up the bottle” and its numbers from our Python planner, side view: the bottle (green) stands on spot B, x = 0.42 m from the shoulder; the grasp is the centre of its cap, (0.42, 0.04) m; the joint angles q = (31.2, −66.4) degrees put the hand there (the arm in blue and brown); the dotted line is the hand’s track along the motion from home (the faint upright arm), 88.7 degrees of joint motion

Written as an action with parameters ()Parameter (PDDL action): A blank in an action, written with a question mark such as ?from and ?to in move ?from ?to, that the planner fills with objects; one action with parameters replaces many STRIPS actions (6 moves for 3 places, 90 for 10). Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §4.1 One action with parameters, the pick-up gets these numbers as parameters, and its precondition gains tests on them. PDDLStream’s example domain for a table-top arm writes it this way, shortened to the lines used here (PDDLStream continuous_tamp/domain.pddl):

Reading it line by line:

  • ?b is the object, ?p its place, ?g the grasp, ?q the configuration and ?r the robot;
  • (Kin ?b ?q ?p ?g) is the new test: with the arm at ?q, the hand is on grasp ?g of object ?b standing at ?p;
  • (AtPose ?b ?p) and (HandEmpty ?r) are the facts of STRIPSExplained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §3.1 Precondition, add list and delete list, as before.

The put-down skill has the same numbers and one more test: the object at its new place must not overlap any object standing there (PDDLStream’s CFree). The Overview’s plan failed this test at B. The tests of our planner, one per kind of number:

  1. on the table: the object’s base lies on the table top, from 0.05 m (where the robot’s top ends) to 0.60 m;
  2. no collision: the object does not overlap another standing object, a yes-or-no collision detection ()Collision detection: Deciding, yes or no, whether two bodies overlap, such as the robot’s disk and the table; planners call it thousands of times instead of building the obstacle region. Explained in: Collision Checking and Nearest Neighbours in C-space, §2.1 The yes-or-no question and what it costs;
  3. within reach: some joint angles put the hand on its grasp without the arm hitting anything;
  4. motion: a free path of the arm exists to those joint angles.

Finding a list of actions together with numbers for every action that pass every test is task and motion planning ()Task and motion planning (TAMP): Planning a list of actions together with the numbers inside every action (where objects go, how they are grasped, the arm’s joint angles, the motions) so that each number passes its tests; it finds plans such as “move the bottle off spot B first” that a task planner or a motion planner alone cannot. Explained in §2.2 A skill and its numbers., TAMP: task planning whose actions carry continuous parameters and constraints on them (Garrett §1, §3.1). The next section runs it on the cup and the bottle.

3. Searching for the actions and their numbers together

Key point

Our planner first finds a list of actions from the facts, then searches for numbers that pass every test; a failed test at a named place becomes a new fact, and a failed test of a sampled number sends the search back to sample again. On the table, the second list of actions moves the bottle to 0.485 m first, after five samples, and the cup reaches B.

3.1 Plan skeletons, and a failed test that becomes a fact

The Overview’s two actions came from the facts alone, with their numbers left open. A list of actions whose numbers are still to be found is a plan skeleton ()Plan skeleton: A list of actions whose numbers (places, grasps, joint angles, motions) are still to be found, such as “pick up the bottle, put it down at a new place, pick up the cup, put it down at B”; a planner tests it by searching for numbers that pass every test. Explained in §3.1 Plan skeletons, and a failed test that becomes a fact. (Garrett §1.1; Robohub TAMP). Our planner searches in two loops (Garrett §3.3.1):

  1. the task planner, forward search ()Forward search (progression): Planning by starting at the start state, applying every applicable action to make the next states, and searching the resulting graph of states until one meets the goal; any graph search, such as breadth-first search or A*, can run it. Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §5.1 The state graph, built while searching on the facts, returns the shortest skeleton it has not yet tried;
  2. a second search tries to find numbers for every action of that skeleton that pass every test;
  3. if it succeeds, the skeleton with its numbers is the plan; if not, the search returns to step 1.

Ordering the search this way, the whole skeleton first and its numbers after, is called sequence before satisfy ()Sequence before satisfy: A task and motion planning strategy that first finds a whole plan skeleton from the facts and then searches for its numbers, trying the next skeleton if none pass; it spends no samples on actions no plan needs, but a skeleton can fail late. Explained in §3.1 Plan skeletons, and a failed test that becomes a fact. (Garrett §3.3.1).

For the task planner, a place is a name: A and B for the cup, “start” for where the bottle stands, and “new” for a place on the table whose position the second search will choose. The first skeleton is the Overview’s: pick up the cup at A, put it down at B. Its test at B fails, and the planner turns the failure into a new fact the task planner must respect: the cup at B collides with the bottle where the bottle stands. Recording a failed test as a counterexample, so that every later skeleton with the same failure is skipped, is what the survey calls a negative method (Garrett §3.4.2).

With that fact, putting the cup at B requires the bottle to move first, and the shortest skeleton becomes four actions. A person setting a table follows the same order: when a bottle stands where the cup belongs, the bottle is moved first.

  1. pick up the bottle;
  2. put the bottle down at a new place;
  3. pick up the cup at A;
  4. put the cup down at B.

The second skeleton has one number that is still open: where the bottle goes.

3.2 Sampling the bottle’s place, and backing up

The table offers infinitely many places for the bottle, so the planner cannot list them. The planner draws them at random, the way the probabilistic roadmapProbabilistic roadmap (PRM): A graph of random free configurations (milestones) joined by straight edges that pass a collision check, built once and then searched with A*; it describes the free space without building the obstacle region, so it scales to robots with many joints. Explained in: Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM), §2.2 Phase 1 and phase 2: milestones and edges draws configurations: a function returns a random x at which the bottle’s whole base is on the table, from 0.07 m to 0.58 m (Robohub TAMP; PDDLStream s-region). Each sample then goes through the four tests of Section 2.2, action by action.

The collision test. Each object covers half its width on each side of its centre, so two objects reach towards each other by half their widths added. If their centres are at least that far apart, a gap is left; if closer, they overlap. With:

  • x1​ and x2​ are the two centres, in m;
  • ∣x1​−x2​∣ is the gap between the centres, their difference taken as positive whichever is larger, in m;
  • w1​ and w2​ are the two widths: 0.06 m for the cup, 0.04 m for the bottle.

The two are free of each other when:

∣x1​−x2​∣≥2w1​+w2​​

For the cup and the bottle, half the widths added:

20.06+0.04​=0.05 m

The first sample puts the bottle at 0.3949 m, and action 4 then tests the cup at B, 0.42 m. The gap between the centres:

0.42−0.3949=0.0251 m 0.0251<0.05

So the two overlap, from the cup’s left end, 0.39 m, to the bottle’s right end, 0.4149 m:

0.4149−0.39=0.0249 m

In the opening, both centres were at 0.42 m, a gap of 0, and the bottle lay wholly inside the cup’s 6 cm: the overlap was the bottle’s whole width, 4 cm.

Backing up. The first sample passed every test of its own action and of action 3, so the planner had already planned two motions, 11.1 and 87.0 degrees, before action 4 failed. Only the bottle’s place can be changed, so the search goes back to that choice, drops the two motions and samples again. Going back to the latest choice and trying another value when a later test fails is backtracking ()Backtracking: Going back to the latest choice and trying another value when a later test fails, such as sampling a new place for the bottle when the cup can no longer reach B; it lets a search recover from early choices at the cost of the work done after them. Explained in §3.2 Sampling the bottle’s place, and backing up. (Robohub TAMP; Garrett §3.3.1). The 2.5 cm overlap involves a sampled place, not two named ones, so the planner samples again instead of turning it into a new fact.

The next three samples fail early:

  • 0.2076 m: its centre is only 0.0276 m from the cup’s at A, less than 0.05 m, so the bottle would overlap the cup by 0.0224 m (collision test);
  • 0.0909 m and 0.0784 m: the hand cannot reach the cap (within-reach test).

The within-reach test. Folded fully, the arm’s hand is no nearer to the shoulder than the difference of its links; stretched fully, no farther than their sum (the triangle of the two links of manipulation planningManipulation planning: Planning the motions of a robot together with the objects it moves, as a sequence of transit and transfer motions joined at transition configurations, each planned in its own free space. Explained in: Manipulation Planning, §2.2 Where the modes meet: transition configurations). With:

  • L1​ and L2​ are the link lengths, 0.3 m and 0.2 m;
  • (x,y) is the cap’s position from the shoulder, along the table and up, in m: (0.0909, 0.04) for the third sample, because the cap stands 0.04 m above the shoulder’s height;
  • d is the cap’s distance from the shoulder, in m.

The nearest and farthest the hand reaches:

0.3−0.2=0.1 m 0.3+0.2=0.5 m

The third sample’s cap:

d=0.09092+0.042​ =0.008263+0.0016​ =0.0993 m

which is less than 0.1 m, so no joint angles put the hand there. At 0.0784 m the distance is 0.0880 m. The same test in symbols:

d=x2+y2​ L1​−L2​≤d≤L1​+L2​

The fifth sample, 0.4848 m, passes everything. The bottle there spans 0.4648 m to 0.5048 m and leaves 1.48 cm between itself and the cup at B (no collision), its cap is within reach, and a motion of 42.1 degrees takes it there. The cup’s pick-up and put-down then pass too, and the search ends.

So the bottle’s place is found by drawing values and testing them, and the one value that failed only at a later action, 0.395 m, cost two motions that were discarded. The next section runs the plan it found.

Our planner chose the whole skeleton first and its numbers after, and threw away 3 of its 7 motions on the way. The other order is possible too, and the survey groups planners by it (Garrett §3.3). The two orders:

  • sequence before satisfy ()Sequence before satisfy: A task and motion planning strategy that first finds a whole plan skeleton from the facts and then searches for its numbers, trying the next skeleton if none pass; it spends no samples on actions no plan needs, but a skeleton can fail late. Explained in §3.1 Plan skeletons, and a failed test that becomes a fact. finds a whole skeleton, then its numbers; it wastes no samples on actions no plan needs, but a skeleton can fail late, as action 4 did for the sample 0.395 m;
  • satisfy before sequence ()Satisfy before sequence: A task and motion planning strategy that first samples and tests values (places, joint angles, motions) and then searches for a plan that uses only tested values, sampling more when no plan is found; PDDLStream’s incremental algorithm works this way. Explained in §3.4 Two orders for the two searches. first samples values and tests them one constraint at a time (places on the table, joint angles for each place, motions between joint angles), then lets the task planner search for a plan that uses only values already tested; if none exists, it samples more and searches again (Garrett §3.3.2).

Most planners in the survey mix the two, adding actions and solving their numbers as they go (Garrett §3.3, Table 1). In each order the loop back to the start is backtracking in another form, so no order avoids it (Robohub TAMP). PDDLStream, the code base of the next section, offers both orders.

4. The code real robots use: PDDLStream

Key point

Our planner was written for one table; PDDLStream writes the same samplers and tests as streams, Python functions next to a PDDL domain, and alternates between sampling with them and searching for actions with a PDDL planner. Planning takes seconds to minutes, and the plan is not changed while it runs.

Section 3 wrote its own tests and samplers. An open-source planner by the survey’s first author writes the same pieces in files a general planner can read. PDDLStream ()PDDLStream: An open-source planning framework that extends PDDL with streams, so samplers and tests written in Python sit next to a PDDL domain; its algorithms alternate between sampling values and searching for actions with a PDDL planner, which lets robots plan tasks with geometry. Explained in §4. The code real robots use: PDDLStream. is a planning framework that extends PDDL ()PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language): The standard language for writing a planning task, defined in 1998 for the International Planning Competition: actions with parameters, preconditions and effects, in a domain file and a problem file; a task written in it can be handed to any planner that reads PDDL. Explained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §4.1 One action with parameters with streams (PDDLStream README). A stream ()Stream (PDDLStream): A user-written Python function that returns new values satisfying a condition, such as a place on the table or a motion, or that only tests given values, such as “these two objects do not overlap”; the facts it certifies become preconditions of PDDL actions. Explained in §4. The code real robots use: PDDLStream. is a user-written Python function that takes values and returns new values that satisfy a condition, such as a place on the table, or that only checks whether given values satisfy one; each value it returns comes with the facts it certifies, which the actions then use as preconditions (PDDLStream README; Robohub TAMP).

The example examples/continuous_tamp in the repository (caelan/pddlstream, branch main, read 2026-10-12) is close to our table: blocks stand on a line of ground, drawn from the side, and a suction gripper picks them from above; its collision test is the overlap of two blocks’ extents along the ground, as ours is (primitives.py, collision_test). Its file stream.pddl declares the streams our planner wrote as Python functions:

Stream in stream.pddlWhat it returnsIn our planner (tampkit.py)
s-regiona place ?p for block ?b inside region ?r; certifies Contain, Posesample_place: a random x on the table
s-graspa grasp ?g for block ?b; certifies Graspthe one grasp, the centre of the top
s-ika configuration ?q that puts the hand on grasp ?g of ?b at ?p; certifies Kingrasp_conf: the within-reach test

The place action of domain.pddl asks CFree for the new place against every block standing anywhere, the test the Overview’s plan failed. PDDLStream has several algorithms on the same files (README, “Algorithms”):

  • Incremental samples values with the streams, hands the facts they certify to a PDDL planner, Fast Downward, and samples more when no plan is found: satisfy before sequence, much as a probabilistic roadmap samples configurations and then searches the graph they make;
  • Focused, Binding and Adaptive plan first with placeholder values for numbers not yet sampled, then call only the streams that plan needs (Garrett §3.4.1); the README says Adaptive “typically performs best for domains with many possible sampling pathways, such as robot manipulation domains”.

The open-source robot simulator pyrobosim uses PDDLStream for its task and motion planning (README, “Applications”; Robohub TAMP walks through it). Two limits come with the method:

  • planning a task such as twisting the cap off a bottle took from under a minute to two or three minutes, much of it spent sampling (sampling can need a very long time when few values satisfy a constraint, Garrett §3.3);
  • the plan is computed before the robot moves and is not replanned if something changes while it runs.

5. A request in words: a language model chooses the skills

Key point

For the request in words, SayCan multiplies, for each skill, the language model’s probability that the skill’s words come next (Say) by the chance the skill succeeds now (Can), and runs the best product, one skill at a time. On our table, with GPT-2 small, Can rules out the two skills with the highest Say scores at step 2, and “done” then wins by 1.104 to 1, so the robot ends the plan with the cup still in its hand.

5.1 Say: the language model’s score for each skill

The second problem of the Overview was the request “put the cup on spot B”, given in words. A large language model ()Large language model (LLM): A transformer language model with billions of parameters, trained on a vast amount of text. Explained in: From Encoder–Decoder to ChatGPT: A History of Large Language Models, §8. Stage 5: large language models (2018 onward), a transformer trained on a vast amount of text, has read many descriptions of how to put things in places. Asked directly, however, it may answer with steps for which the robot has no skill: asked to help with a spill, one model suggested “You could try using a vacuum cleaner” (Ahn et al. §1). So SayCan does not let the model write freely. SayCan asks the model only to score the robot’s own skills ()Skill: An action a robot can carry out on command, such as “pick up the bottle”, with the preconditions it needs and a motion that carries it out; planners and language models build tasks out of skills. Explained in §2.2 A skill and its numbers., the actions it can carry out on command, each written in a few words such as “pick up the cup” (Ahn et al. §3).

The score is the probability that the model gives to the skill’s words as the next step. A GPT ()GPT: A language model that writes text by predicting the next token again and again (Generative Pre-trained Transformer); it is built from transformer decoder blocks only. Explained in: GPT: the Decoder-Only Transformer, §1. Overview model gives a probability for every possible next token, so the probability of several tokens is the product of each token’s probability given the ones before it (Ahn et al. §2). Adding their logarithms gives the same ranking with easier numbers, the log-likelihood ()Log-likelihood: The logarithm of the likelihood, which turns the product of probabilities into a sum of log probabilities; it peaks at the same parameters as the likelihood and is easier to compute and maximise. Explained in: Logistic Regression: Maximum Likelihood and the Log Loss, §4.2 Taking logs of the skill’s words.

We score our seven skills with GPT-2 small, a GPT ()GPT: A language model that writes text by predicting the next token again and again (Generative Pre-trained Transformer); it is built from transformer decoder blocks only. Explained in: GPT: the Decoder-Only Transformer, §1. Overview of 124 million parameters, run in the notebook. The text before the skills follows SayCan’s prompt (Ahn et al. App. D.3, Listing 1): a line saying what the robot is, worked examples with other objects, then the request (the full prompt is in the notebook). For example:

At step 1, GPT-2 small gives “pick up the cup” the log-likelihood −12.68, a probability:

e−12.68=3.113×10−6

The seven skills’ probabilities add up to 3.130 × 10⁻⁶ (notebook), so the share of the seven that goes to “pick up the cup” is:

3.1303.113​=0.9946

With:

  • ℓ is a skill’s words, such as “pick up the cup”;
  • i is the request and the steps chosen so far;
  • p(ℓ∣i) is the language model’s probability for ℓ after i, shared out over the robot’s skills: the Say score.

The share is each probability divided by the sum of all seven:

p(ℓ∣i)=∑k​pk​pℓ​​

where pℓ​ is the probability of skill ℓ‘s words and the sum runs over the seven skills k. Sharing out does not change which skill is best; it only makes the scores add up to 1.

The Say score is computed from words alone, not from the table. At step 1 it puts 0.0007 on “put down the cup on spot B”, a skill that cannot run with an empty hand. A second score must state what the robot can do now.

5.2 Can: the chance the skill succeeds now

SayCan gives every skill a second number: the probability that the skill, started in the present state, succeeds. This probability is the skill’s affordance ()Affordance (SayCan): The probability that a skill, started in the present state, succeeds, such as 0 for “put down the cup on spot B” while a bottle stands there; SayCan multiplies the language model’s score by it so impossible skills are never chosen. Explained in §5.2 Can: the chance the skill succeeds now.: what the scene allows the robot to do right now (Ahn et al. §3). In SayCan each skill’s affordance comes from a different source (Ahn et al. App. D.2):

  • pick-up skills: an estimate of success learned by trial and error, by reinforcement learning ()Reinforcement learning: Learning by acting and receiving rewards or punishments. Explained in: Types of Machine Learning, §5.1 Agent, environment, policy and reward, rescaled to 0 to 1;
  • go-to skills: from the distance to the place; the skill itself drives the base with a classical navigation stack;
  • place skills: always 1;
  • “done”: a small fixed value, 0.1, so the robot stops when no skill is possible.

Our affordance uses the four tests of Section 2.2, which our planner already computes: 1 when the skill’s place, joint angles and motion pass every test in the present state, 0 when one fails, and SayCan’s 0.1 for “done”. “Put down the cup on the table” means a free place anywhere on the table outside spot B, drawn by the sampler of Section 3.2. With:

  • s is the present state: where the objects stand and what the hand holds;
  • p(c∣s,ℓ) is the probability that skill ℓ completes from s: the Can score.

Each skill now has two numbers, Say from the words and Can from the table; Section 5.3 combines them into one.

5.3 Say times Can, one skill at a time

SayCan needs one number per skill: the chance that running the skill now moves the request forward. A skill moves the request forward only if it succeeds and is the right next step. If it fails, it moves nothing (Ahn et al. §3). For “done” at step 2:

0.1×0.01735+0.9×0 =0.001735

In symbols, the chance that the skill completes times the chance it is the right step, plus the chance it fails times zero:

p(c∣s,ℓ)p(ℓ∣i)+(1−p(c∣s,ℓ))×0 =p(c∣s,ℓ)p(ℓ∣i)

So the score of a skill is Say times Can, and SayCan runs the skill with the largest product, adds its words to the text, and scores again, until “done” wins (Ahn et al. Algorithm 1). Choosing skills by Say times Can is SayCan ()SayCan: A method that picks a robot’s next skill by multiplying a language model’s probability that the skill’s words come next (Say) by the skill’s chance of succeeding now (Can), one skill at a time, so a request in words is turned only into skills the robot can carry out. Explained in §5.3 Say times Can, one skill at a time., a method from Robotics at Google and Everyday Robots named after its two scores (Ahn et al. §1).

Figure 6 runs it on our table from the notebook’s numbers. Watch the green column, Say times Can, shared out over the skills: at step 1 the skill with the highest Say score is possible and is chosen; at step 2 the two skills with the highest Say scores get Can 0.

At step 2 the hand holds the cup, and the products of the two possible skills are:

done: 0.01735×0.1=0.001735 table: 0.001572×1=0.001572 0.001735/0.001572=1.104

GPT-2 small gives “done” 11.04 times the Say score of “put down the cup on the table” (notebook); Can multiplies the score of “done” by 0.1, which leaves “done” 1.104 times ahead. So SayCan’s plan is “pick up the cup, done”, and the robot ends the plan with the cup in its hand, short of B.

The Can score removed “pick up the cup” (the hand is full) and “put down the cup on spot B” (the bottle is there), which together held 0.9810 of the Say score at step 2. The Can score does not, however, say why a skill is impossible. The language model sees only the request and the skills chosen so far (Ahn et al. Algorithm 1), so it never learns that the bottle stands on B, and the skill that would help, “pick up the bottle”, scores 0.0001 at step 2. Our planner of Section 3 learned the reason as a fact and planned around it.

5.4 SayCan on a real robot, and what each method needs

A model as small as GPT-2 is not what SayCan uses. On a mobile robot with one arm, in a mock kitchen built like an office kitchen, SayCan with PaLM, a language model of 540 billion parameters, 4,339 times GPT-2 small, was tested on 101 requests (Ahn et al. §5, Table 2):

  • it chose the right skills for 84 percent of them and carried 74 percent out to the end;
  • with the language model alone, choosing the skill with the best Say score, 67 percent were planned right;
  • on the requests whose answer depends on the scene, 64 percent were planned right with Can and 18 percent without it;
  • with the smaller FLAN model, 137 billion parameters, 70 percent were planned right and 61 percent carried out, so PaLM halved the errors (Ahn et al. Table 3).

Most failures on the longest requests came from the language model ending the plan too early, as “done” did on our table (Ahn et al. §5).

So the two methods answer the two halves of the opening question: the planner finds actions the arm can carry out, and the language model turns words into skills. Each method has what the other lacks.
