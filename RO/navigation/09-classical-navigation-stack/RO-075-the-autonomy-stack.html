1. Overview

Key point

A robot’s software is split into layers that each run at their own rate: the mission and the route rarely, the local plan about 10 times a second, the wheel commands 20 times a second, all at once. One loop that waits for its slowest part sees the person in the room 0.6 s late and touches them; layered, the same robot reaches the door at 8.0 s.

The room robot already has every part it needs to reach the door:

  • A* ()A search:* Dijkstra’s algorithm with the open list sorted by f=g+h, the cost so far plus a heuristic guess of the cost left; with a consistent heuristic it returns the shortest route while expanding far fewer states (9 cells against 54 on the room grid). Explained in: A* and Heuristics, §4.5 The algorithm plans the route on the room’s 0.5 m grid, 3.414 m;
  • a particle filter ()Particle filter: A Bayes filter that stores the belief as a set of weighted samples (particles) and runs each round as move, weigh, resample; it can hold beliefs of any shape, such as a belief with three modes, one at each of three doors. Explained in: Particle Filter, §4. The particle filter, step by step run on the map estimates where it is;
  • the local planner ()Local planner: The planner that turns the next few metres or seconds of the global plan into a timed trajectory within the robot’s limits and around obstacles its sensors see, solving again many times a second, such as TEB at 10 Hz over 1.5 m; it handles what the global plan cannot. Explained in: Trajectory Optimisation: The Local Planner, §2.1 Levels of planning bends a timed plan round obstacles the map lacks, every 0.1 s;
  • the trackers of the last chapter, such as Kanayama’s trackerKanayama’s tracker: A trajectory tracker for a wheeled robot that sends the reference’s speed and turn rate plus feedback on the error in the robot’s frame, v=vr​cosθe​+Kx​xe​ and ω=ωr​+vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​); a Lyapunov function proves its error cannot grow without bound while the reference drives forward. Explained in: Tracking a Timed Trajectory: Kanayama and Feedback Linearisation, §5. Kanayama’s tracker, turn a plan into wheel speeds and correct the robot as it drives.

Each part was built on its own. Nothing yet says which part runs when, how often, or which sensors feed them. The running scene shows why that matters.   1. Why does one loop fail, and how often must the robot look?
  2. How should the parts be arranged so that the slow ones do not hold up the fast ones?
  3. Which sensors see the person in time, and where should they go?

We answer them in this order:

  • one loop that senses, plans and acts, and the rate it would need (Section 2);
  • the opposite extreme, sensors wired straight to the wheels (Section 3);
  • several such reflexes stacked in levels (Section 4);
  • the layered stack that combines a plan with fast reactions, its rates, and the same layers in Nav2, Autoware and Apollo (Section 5);
  • choosing and placing the sensors that feed it (Section 6).

2. Sense, plan, act: one loop, as slow as its slowest step

Key point

Sense–plan–act runs sensing, modelling, planning and acting in order, in one loop, so the robot looks again only when the whole loop is done. To stop for the person it must look at least every 0.187 s, but a whole-map step runs about once a second.

2.1 The loop

The first opening question asks why one loop fails. The simplest way to join the parts is to run them one after another, in a repeating loop:

  1. sense: read every sensor, the laser scan and the wheels;
  2. plan: update the map and the robot’s pose, then plan a route over the whole map;
  3. act: drive that plan until the next pass.

Figure 2 steps through the opening’s left-hand robot, whose loop looks once a second, from 1.9 to 3.22 s. Watch which step is lit when the person steps in at 2.4 s.

Figure 2: Animation: the opening’s one loop, step by step. Bottom: a time line with the loop’s looks (green, 2.0 and 3.0 s), the person stepping in (pink, 2.4 s) and the touch (red, 3.22 s). At 2.0 s the loop looks and plans with nobody on the route, then acts on that plan; at 2.4 s the person steps in 0.465 m ahead while the loop is acting, not looking; at 3.0 s it sees them 0.113 m ahead and plans to brake; at 3.22 s it touches them

Running sensing, planning and acting in this fixed order, with the whole plan made from a model of the world before the robot moves, is sense–plan–act ()Sense–plan–act (deliberative control): A way to run a robot in one fixed loop: read the sensors, update a model of the world and plan, then act on the plan, and repeat; the robot looks again only when the whole loop is done, so the slowest step, such as planning over the whole map, sets how often it can react. Explained in §2.1 The loop., also called deliberative control. Sense–plan–act was the first way robots were built: Shakey, the 1960s robot whose planner was STRIPSExplained in: Symbolic Task Planning: STRIPS and PDDL, §3.1 Precondition, add list and delete list, ran this way (Chernova, CS 7633 slides).

An everyday picture: a driver who glances at the road, then closes their eyes to plan the whole trip again before looking up. Between two glances, whatever happens on the road goes unseen. The loop’s period, the time from one look to the next, is set by its slowest step.

2.2 How often the loop must look

The person steps in 0.465 m ahead of the robot’s rim. In the worst case they step in just after a look, so the robot drives on without seeing them for one whole period and only then starts to brake. With:

  • v, the robot’s speed when the person steps in, 0.595 m/s;
  • a, the hardest the robot can brake, 0.5 m/s²;
  • g, the gap from the robot’s rim to the person, 0.465 m;
  • T, the loop’s period, the time between two looks, in s;
  • d, the distance the robot covers from the person stepping in to standing still, in m.

The distance has two parts: the robot drives on at the speed v for up to one period before it sees the person, then it brakes over the stopping distanceExplained in: Planning with Motion Limits, §2.2 The state, its motion equations and the stopping distance derived for this robot, the speed squared over twice the braking:

d=vT+2av2​

The braking part with our numbers:

2×0.50.5952​=0.354 m

The robot stops in time only if d is less than the gap g. Our numbers first, then the same steps in symbols:

0.595T+0.354<0.465 vT+v2/(2a)<g 0.595T<0.465−0.354 vT<g−v2/(2a) T<(0.465−0.354)/0.595 T<vg−v2/(2a)​ T<0.111/0.595=0.187 s

The loop must therefore look at least every 0.187 s, about 5.3 times a second. A check at an easy value: with T=0, a robot that sees the person the instant they step in needs only the 0.354 m of braking, less than 0.465 m, so it stops. Figure 3 plots d for every period.

The run of Figure 1 is less severe than the worst case: its 1 s loop looks at 3.0 s, 0.6 s after the person steps in, and it still touches them at 3.22 s. The same loop looking every 0.1 s brakes at 2.4 s and stands still at 3.59 s, 0.112 m from the person (notebook). A loop that looks every 0.1 s would therefore be fast enough; the question is whether the whole loop can run that often.

2.3 Why the whole loop cannot run every 0.1 s

The obvious remedy is to run the whole loop every 0.1 s. The plan step makes that impossible. Updating a map of the whole area and planning a route across it is the slowest work a navigation robot does, which is why the global plan is refreshed rarelyExplained in: Trajectory Optimisation: The Local Planner, §2.2 The global plan and the local planner in numbers, every 2 s in a ROS benchmark. Nav2, the ROS 2 navigation stack, updates its whole-map costmap, a grid of the area that marks where obstacles are, once a second and asks for a new global plan once a second (nav2_params.yaml; Nav2 default tree).

A loop that contains this step therefore looks about once a second, more than five times too slowly for the 0.187 s that Section 2.2 requires. Sense–plan–act works only when the world can be modelled accurately and hardly changes while the robot carries out its plan (Chernova, CS 7633 slides). The person broke the second condition in less than one pass. The robot needs a part that reacts within one sensor reading, without waiting for a plan.

3. Reactive control: sensors wired to the wheels

Key point

Reactive control maps sensor readings straight to wheel speeds, with no map and no plan, so the robot reacts within one reading. Braitenberg’s vehicles show what two sensors and two wires can do: wired one way the robot steers to a light, but it cannot sense the table in its path.

3.1 One sensor, one wheel

The opening’s first question needs a robot that reacts within one reading, not one plan. The opposite of a long chain of steps is no chain at all: wire a sensor straight to a motor. A designer who chains many steps must foresee every situation the robot can meet and write a response for each; a simple wire foresees nothing, yet the robot still behaves sensibly.

The simplest case is one light sensor wired to one wheel: the more light, the faster the wheel. In the shade the vehicle moves slowly, in bright light it moves fast, and so it spends most of its time in the dark. An onlooker would say it fears the light, but nothing inside it knows about fear; the behaviour comes from the wire alone. With the wire reversed, so that more light means slower, the same vehicle lingers in the light instead.

Control that maps the present sensor readings straight to motor commands, with no model of the world and no plan, is reactive control ()Reactive control: Control that maps the present sensor readings straight to motor commands, with no model of the world and no plan; the robot reacts within one reading, but it cannot plan a route, build a map or choose between behaviours. Explained in §3.1 One sensor, one wheel.. One such mapping from sensor readings to motor actions, made for one task, is a behaviour ()Behaviour (robotics): One mapping from sensor readings to motor actions, made for one task, such as “turn towards the light” or “halt if something is close ahead”; reactive and behaviour-based robots are built by combining several. Explained in §3.1 One sensor, one wheel. (Chernova, CS 7633 slides). A reflex is the everyday example: a hand pulls back from a hot stove before any thought about stoves. Because nothing waits for a plan, a reactive robot reacts within one sensor reading. One sensor, though, can only change the speed, not the direction.

3.2 Two sensors, two wheels: steering to a light

One sensor can only change the speed. With two light sensors at the front and the two wheels of our robot, the wiring can also steer. Figure 4 shows the four ways to wire them:

  • the wires go to the wheel on the same side, or cross over;
  • each wire speeds its wheel up with more light (+) or slows it down (−).

Figure 4: Braitenberg’s vehicles 2 and 3, seen from above: two light sensors at the front, two wheels at the back, and the wires between them. 2a fear: same-side wires, more light makes a wheel faster; it turns away from the light. 2b aggression: crossed wires, faster; it turns to the light and charges at it. 3a love: same-side wires, more light makes a wheel slower; it turns to the light and stops near it. 3b explorer: crossed wires, slower; it comes near, then turns away

A vehicle of one or two light sensors wired straight to its wheels is a Braitenberg vehicle ()Braitenberg vehicle: A vehicle whose one or two light sensors are wired straight to its wheels, each wire speeding its wheel up or slowing it down with more light, on the same side or crossed; the wiring alone makes it flee a light, charge at it, stop near it or pass it by, with no plan. Why the wiring steers, on our robot. Our robot starts at (2.0, 1.0) facing 30°. We put a lamp at the door pose, (3.6, 3.5), ahead and to the left, so that a vehicle that seeks light has the room’s goal as its target. We give the robot two light sensors on its rim, 45° either side of its heading. Our model of a reading, a number from 0 to 1, is the lamp’s brightness at the sensor times how squarely the sensor faces the lamp. With:

  • ds​, the distance from the sensor to the lamp, in m;
  • D0​, the distance at which the brightness falls to half, 1 m (our choice);
  • α, the angle between the direction the sensor faces and the direction to the lamp, in degrees;
  • s, the sensor’s reading, a number from 0 to 1.
s=1+(ds​/D0​)21​×21+cosα​

The first part is 1 at the lamp and falls to one half at 1 m; the second is 1 when the sensor faces the lamp, one half when the lamp is square to its side and 0 when the lamp is behind it.

The left sensor faces 75°, 2.731 m from the lamp and 19.2° off it; the right sensor faces −15°, 2.902 m away and 77.09° off it (notebook):

sL​=1+2.73121​×21+cos19.2∘​ =0.1182×0.9722=0.1149 sR​=1+2.90221​×21+cos77.09∘​ =0.1061×0.6117=0.0649

The left sensor reads more light, as expected, because the lamp is to the left. We now wire vehicle 3a: same side, more light makes the wheel slower. With:

  • sL​ and sR​, the left and right readings, 0.1149 and 0.0649;
  • k, the wire’s gain, how strongly a reading slows its wheel, 2 (our choice);
  • vmax​, a wheel’s top speed, 0.6 m/s;
  • vL​ and vR​, the left and right wheel speeds, in m/s.
vL​=vmax​(1−ksL​) vR​=vmax​(1−ksR​)

With our numbers:

vL​=0.6×(1−2×0.1149) =0.6×0.7702=0.4621 m/s vR​=0.6×(1−2×0.0649) =0.6×0.8702=0.5221 m/s

The right wheel runs faster, so the robot turns left, towards the lamp. How fast it turns is the wheels’ difference over the 0.2 m wheel separation, the angular velocity ()Angular velocity ω: How fast a robot’s heading changes, in rad/s, positive to the left; for a differential drive, the difference of the wheel speeds divided by the wheel separation. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.1 Why we describe motion by linear and angular velocity formula:

ω=(0.5221−0.4621)/0.2 =0.300 rad/s

A check at an easy value: with the lamp straight ahead, both sensors read the same, both wheels turn at the same speed, and the robot drives straight at the lamp. As it approaches, both readings grow and both wheels slow, so it stops near the lamp.

The first gap can be closed with more sensors and more behaviours, one for the light and one for obstacles; the next section stacks them.

4. Subsumption: reflexes in levels

Key point

Subsumption runs several behaviours at once in levels, the lowest built first; a higher level replaces a lower level’s command when it has one, but a safety reflex such as “halt” is never overruled. In the room it never touches anything, yet it stops 0.283 m from the person and stays there, 1.748 m from the lamp.

4.1 Two levels for the room

The vehicle of Figure 6 reacts fast but senses only the lamp. The opening’s question becomes: how can several fast behaviours, each a mapping from readings to wheel commands, share one pair of wheels? Brooks’s answer was to build the robot in levels (Dean, CS148 notes; Wikipedia, “Subsumption architecture”):

  1. Build the lowest level first, a robot that only avoids things, and test it on its own.
  2. Add the next level on top, a robot that goes somewhere, and let it use the lower level’s work.
  3. Where both send wheel commands, let the higher level’s command replace the lower one’s, except for the reflexes that keep the robot safe.

For the room we build two levels (Figure 7). Level 0, avoid, has a ring of 36 range readings, one every 10°, taken every 0.1 s:

  • collide halts the robot, forward speed 0, when a reading within 30° of straight ahead is under 0.42 m;
  • push (Brooks’s feelforce) turns every reading under 0.5 m into a push away from it, harder when nearer;
  • runaway turns the robot along the push and drives away.

Level 1, go to the light, is vehicle 3a of Section 3.2 with one addition: it also turns away from the push, faster the harder the push, with a turn gain of 2. Its command replaces runaway’s.

The ring of 36 readings, collide’s 30° cone, the push’s 0.5 m reach and the turn gain of 2 are our own settings for the room; Section 4.2 varies the last two. Only collide’s 0.42 m follows from a rule, shown next.

Figure 7: The two levels. Level 1 (top): the light sensors feed “go to the light”, the Braitenberg 3a wheels plus a turn away from the push; its command replaces runaway’s at the suppression node S. Level 0 (middle and bottom): the range ring feeds “push” (each reading under 0.5 m pushes away) and “collide” (a reading ahead under 0.42 m: halt); push feeds runaway (turn and drive along the push), whose command reaches the wheels through S; collide’s halt goes straight to the wheels and sets the forward speed to 0

The 0.42 m halt distance comes from the formula of Section 2.2 at the robot’s top speed with the 0.1 s look. One look’s driving plus the braking:

0.6×0.1+2×0.50.62​ =0.06+0.36=0.42 m

The standard terms: a design in which behaviours run in parallel in levels, each level adding a competence on top of the ones below and taking over their commands when it has a command to send, is the subsumption architecture ()Subsumption architecture: A design, due to Brooks (1986), in which behaviours run in parallel in levels, the lowest such as “avoid” built and tested first; a higher level adds a competence such as “go to the light” and takes over the lower levels’ commands when it has one, while safety reflexes stay in force. Explained in §4.1 Two levels for the room., from Brooks (1986). A higher level takes over in two ways, suppression and inhibition ()Suppression and inhibition: The two ways a higher subsumption level takes over a lower one: suppression replaces a lower module’s input or command with the higher level’s own signal, and inhibition blocks a lower module’s output from going anywhere; they decide which behaviour drives the wheels. Explained in §4.1 Two levels for the room.: suppression replaces the input of a lower module with the higher level’s own signal, as level 1 does to runaway’s command at S; inhibition blocks a lower module’s output from going anywhere (Dean, CS148 notes). A course version writes the whole arbitration as one priority chain: if the top level’s condition holds, use its command; else if the next one’s holds, use that; down to level 0 (Jenkins, CSCI 1480 slides). The next question is what the two levels do when the person steps in.

The levels did what they were built for: collide fires at 3.2 s with the person 0.411 m ahead, the robot stands still from 3.91 s, and it never touches anything (notebook). But it stays there. At 20 s it is still 0.283 m from the person and 1.748 m from the lamp. The lamp’s pull turns it towards the person, the push turns it away by the same amount, and collide holds the forward speed at zero: the same balance of pull and push that traps a robot in a potential field’s local minimumExplained in: Potential Fields, §4. Why the robot stops: local minima.

The stop is not a matter of tuning. With the push’s reach set to 0.4, 0.5 or 0.6 m and its turn gain to 1, 2 or 3, nine runs in all, none came closer than 1.146 m to the lamp in 20 s, and none came closer than 0.139 m to anything (notebook). Wikipedia lists the architecture’s two weaknesses: choosing between behaviours through many suppression and inhibition links is hard to design, and without memory or a symbolic model it cannot build maps (Wikipedia, “Subsumption architecture”). Without a map, the robot has no way to know that the way round the person is to the left. The reflexes keep the robot safe; choosing the route requires the plan that Section 2 could not run fast enough.

5. The layered stack: slow planning above fast reaction

Key point

The layered stack runs the slow, long-range layers (the mission and the route) rarely and the fast, short-range layers (the local plan and the wheel commands) often, all at the same time, so a new reading reaches the wheels in one fast cycle while the route still comes from the map. In the room the person’s scan reaches the wheels within the 0.1 s cycle at 2.4 s, and the robot reaches the door at 8.0 s.

5.1 Deliberative layers above reactive layers

Sections 2 to 4 leave the opening’s second question half answered. Sense–plan–act has a plan but reacts once a second; reactive control reacts within a reading but has no plan. The answer is to run both at once, each at its own speed: slow planning layers on top, fast reacting layers below, the lower layers working from the upper layers’ latest output instead of waiting for them. Such a hybrid deliberative and reactive design is the best general architecture for robots, because the planning work runs separately from the reactions while the reactions keep their speed (Chernova, CS 7633 slides).

A robot design that combines a slow planning part, which reasons over a model of the world, with a fast reactive part that acts on the latest readings, the two running at the same time, is a hybrid deliberative/reactive architecture ()Hybrid deliberative/reactive architecture: A robot design that runs a slow planning part, which reasons over a model of the world, and a fast reactive part, which acts on the latest readings, at the same time; the robot keeps a plan and still reacts within one fast cycle. Explained in §5.1 Deliberative layers above reactive layers.. 5.3 Why each layer runs at its rate

Figure 9’s layers run at rates from once to 20 times a second. Each rate follows from how far the robot travels before the layer’s answer is out of date. At the robot’s top speed, 0.6 m/s:

PeriodThe robot travelsFine for
1 s0.6 ma route over the room: walls and tables do not move
0.1 s0.06 mthe local plan: Section 2.2 asked for 0.187 s or less
0.05 s0.03 mthe wheel commands: the robot’s own errors grow fast
  • The local planner must see a newcomer within the 0.187 s of Section 2.2, so 0.1 s is enough; a slower layer would repeat the 1 s loop’s failure.
  • The controller corrects the robot’s own drift from the plan, and a feedback loop works only when it ticks fast compared with how fast the robot responds; choosing the sample rateExplained in: Digital Control: Sampling a Continuous Controller, §6. Choosing the sample rate gives the rule. So it runs faster than the planner above it.
  • The mission and the global plan change only when the goal or the map changes, so running them more often gains nothing and takes computing time the fast layers need.

The slow layers can therefore be slow without harm: a lower layer always works from the latest output of the layer above, and the response to the person never waited for the global plan.

In all three the top layer plans far ahead and changes rarely, and the bottom layer looks a short way ahead and runs often. The stack can only react to what reaches it, so the last question is what its sensors see.

6. Choosing and placing sensors

Key point

A sensor must reach far enough to stop (range), look everywhere the robot can move (coverage), be backed by a sensor of another kind where it is blind (redundancy), and send no more data than the computer can process in time (compute budget). Our robot needs to see 0.67 m from its centre; a 240° laser scanner reaches 5.6 m but leaves 120° behind it blind.

6.1 Range: far enough to stop

The opening’s third question is which sensors see the person in time. The first number is how far the sensor must reach. The stack looks every 0.1 s (Section 5.3), and at the top speed of 0.6 m/s the robot then needs, by the formula of Section 2.2:

0.6×0.1+2×0.50.62​ =0.06+0.36=0.42 m

The 0.42 m is measured from the rim; the sensor sits at the centre, 0.25 m further back:

0.42+0.25=0.67 m

A sensor whose maximum range ()Maximum range zmax​: The longest distance a range sensor can report, such as 5 m; when no pulse returns, the sensor reports this value, so a reading of zmax​ usually means a failure, not a wall. Explained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §2.1 Range finders and scans, the longest distance it can report, is under 0.67 m sees obstacles too late to stop. Range has a cost. An ultrasonic sensor must wait for its echo, so a longer range means fewer readings a second (Correll §5.4): to hear an echo from 5 m, sound travels 10 m out and back, at about 300 m/s:

10/300=0.0333 s 1/0.0333=30 readings per second

A laser scanner such as the Hokuyo URG-04LX-UG01 measures 0.02 to 5.6 m with one scan every 0.1 s (Hokuyo specification), far more than the 0.67 m needed. Nav2 marks obstacles in its costmaps only up to 2.5 m and clears free space up to 3.0 m (obstacle_max_range, raytrace_max_range in nav2_params.yaml), because farther readings are not needed for the next few metres of driving. Sufficient range is of no use in a direction the sensor does not cover.

6.2 Coverage: everywhere the robot can move

Range says how far; coverage says in which directions. Figure 11 puts two real sensors on the robot at 2.4 s. Look at where each one is blind.

Figure 11: Two real sensors on the robot at 2.4 s, when the person steps in; the dashed circle is the 0.67 m the robot needs to see. Left: a laser scanner like the Hokuyo URG-04LX-UG01 at the robot’s centre, facing forward: 240° in 0.352° steps (682 readings) to 5.6 m; red dots are where the readings stop, on the person, the table’s edge and the top wall. The grey wedge is the 120° behind it that it never sees, where Nav2’s default back-up recovery drives 0.30 m (arrow). Right: a depth camera like the RealSense D435: what it sees (orange) within its 87° view from 0.3 to 3 m, with the person’s shadow behind them

The directions a sensor can measure, as an angle around the robot, is its field of view ()Field of view: The directions a sensor can measure, as an angle around it, such as 240° for a laser scanner or 87° for a depth camera; with the range it decides what the sensor covers, and outside it the sensor measures nothing. Explained in §6.2 Coverage: everywhere the robot can move.; the field of view and the range together decide what the sensor covers. In Figure 11 both sensors see the person, 5.4° to the right of straight ahead. Each has a gap:

  • The scanner covers 240° and is blind in the 120° behind it (Hokuyo specification). Nav2’s default tree backs the robot up 0.30 m at 0.15 m/s as a recovery when it cannot make progress (Nav2 default tree), 2 s of driving into that blind wedge.
  • The camera covers only 87° and sees nothing nearer than about 0.3 m (RealSense D435 specification), so an obstacle beside the robot, or very close in front, is invisible to it.
  • Height matters too. A laser scanner measures in one plane, at its own height (sensor_msgs/LaserScan, “single scan from a planar laser range-finder”); a table top above that plane returns nothing. Nav2’s local costmap is therefore a stack of 16 layers of 0.05 m cubes (z_voxels: 16, z_resolution: 0.05), so that a 3D sensor can mark obstacles above the scanner’s plane.

Placement follows: a scanner on top of the robot, at its centre, sees all round if nothing on the robot blocks it; one at the front edge sees at most the half circle ahead. Coverage counts directions; it says nothing about surfaces a sensor cannot measure.

6.3 Redundancy: a second kind of sensor for the first one’s blind spots

Even a well-placed scanner has blind spots of a different kind: surfaces it cannot measure. The beams that hit a glass paneExplained in: Range Sensors: the Beam Model, §4.3 Failures: a spike at the maximum range of a laser scanner return no echo and report the maximum range, as if nothing were there. A robot that relies only on the scanner drives into glass. Ultrasonic sensors do detect glass, so robots add them to the laser (Barreto-Cubero et al. 2021). Figure 12 shows why the two differ in front of a pane.

Figure 12: A laser scanner and an ultrasonic sensor in front of a glass pane with a wall behind it. Left: the laser beam passes through the glass, so the scanner reads only what lies behind it, or its maximum range: the glass is not seen. Right: the ultrasonic sensor’s cone of sound echoes off the glass, so it reads the pane and the robot can stop in time

Correll gives the general reason: a sensor of one kind cannot solve a whole problem such as localization on its own; each measures something the others miss (Correll §5.1).

Adding a second sensor of a different kind, so that one sensor’s blind spots or failures are covered by the other, is sensor redundancy ()Sensor redundancy: Adding a sensor of a different kind that measures the same thing, such as ultrasonic sensors beside a laser scanner, so that one sensor’s blind spots or failures (glass for the laser) are covered by the other; combined, they are also more accurate. Explained in §6.3 Redundancy: a second kind of sensor for the first one’s blind spots.. Redundancy has a second benefit, accuracy: in the Kalman filter’s corridorExplained in: Kalman Filter, §1. Overview the commands alone missed by 0.27 m and the beacon alone by 0.51 m, and the two together by 0.17 m. Every extra sensor, though, adds data to process.

6.4 Compute budget: data the computer can process in time

More sensors and finer sensors send more data, and every reading must be processed before the next arrives. Per second:

682×10=6820 (laser readings) 1280×720×30=27.6 million (camera) 27648000/6820=4054

The depth camera at its full 1280 × 720 resolution and 30 frames a second (RealSense D435 specification) sends 4054 times as many points as the laser scanner. Figure 13 draws the two as areas.

Figure 13: Readings per second drawn as areas: the depth camera’s 27.6 million points a second (orange square) against the laser scanner’s 6820 readings a second (the small blue square, arrowed), 4054 times fewer. Each reading must be processed before the next one arrives

The time and memory the robot’s computer can spend on each sensor’s data, so that every reading is processed before the next one arrives, is its compute budget ()Compute budget: The computing time and memory a robot can spend on each sensor’s data so that every reading is processed before the next one arrives; a sensor that sends more than the budget allows gives the planner readings that are already out of date, so stacks use less of the data. Explained in §6.4 Compute budget: data the computer can process in time.. A reading processed late is a reading about where the person was.

Real stacks keep within the budget by using less of the data:

  • Nav2’s particle filter (AMCL) uses 60 of each scan’s readings (max_beams: 60);
  • Nav2’s local costmap is updated 5 times a second, not at the sensor’s own rate (update_frequency: 5.0);
  • each laser reading clears the cells along its ray, up to 3.0 m (raytrace_max_range) of 0.05 m cells, so one scan visits at most the cells below, and a camera frame of 921,600 points would visit far more:
3.0/0.05=60 cells per reading 682×60=40920 cells per scan

The robot now has its sensors chosen to see the person in time: range 0.67 m, all round, with a second kind of sensor for glass, within what the computer can process every 0.1 s.
