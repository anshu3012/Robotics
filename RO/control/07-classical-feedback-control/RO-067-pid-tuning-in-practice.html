1. Overview

Key point

A PID controller’s three numbers are found by experiments on the robot, either by raising the gain until the loop oscillates (Ziegler–Nichols) or by fitting a small model to one bump test. Practice then changes the textbook PID in three places, so that command steps and sensor noise do not jerk the motors.

The robot of this chapter can follow speed and heading commands. Its PID controller ()PID controller: A controller whose command is the sum of a proportional, an integral and a derivative part of the error, K_p e + K_i (integral of e) + K_d de/dt; the commonest feedback controller, it reacts to the error now, its past and its trend; with K_d = 0 it is a PI controller, with K_i = 0 a PD controller. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Add the error up adds three actions on the error: one proportional to it, one to its accumulated area, one to its rate of change. RO-066 added feedforward ()Feedforward: A control force computed from the plan and the robot’s model, such as mass times the planned acceleration plus drag (mar​+bvr​), and sent before any error appears; it lets the robot follow a changing plan without first falling behind, while feedback removes what the model gets wrong. 3. Ziegler–Nichols: tuning from one experiment

Key point

The Ziegler–Nichols method raises the proportional gain until the loop oscillates steadily, reads that critical gain and critical period ()Critical gain and critical period: The proportional gain kc​ at which a loop oscillates without growing or dying, and the period Tc​ of that oscillation (200 N per m/s and 0.40 s for the robot’s speed loop); they mark the point where the loop’s lags add up to half a period, and the Ziegler–Nichols table uses them. Also called ultimate gain and period. Explained in §3.3 Raise the gain until the loop oscillates., and turns them into PID gains by a table. On the robot’s speed loop it gives fast but fragile gains.

3.1 Why the ideal speed model never oscillates

The first problem of the Overview asks how large the speed gains can be before the robot rings. On the ideal model the answer is: any size. Under proportional control alone, the speed plant of RO-059 is a first-order system. With:

  • m the robot’s mass, 8 kg;
  • b the rolling drag, 2 N·s/m;
  • v the speed, in m/s, and r the commanded speed, 0.5 m/s;
  • kp​ the proportional gain, in N per m/s;

the drive force, the gain times the speed error, minus the drag accelerates the mass:

mdtdv​=kp​(r−v)−bv mdtdv​=kp​r−(kp​+b)v

The second line has the shape of a first-order system ()First-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with only a first derivative, such as the robot’s speed under P control; it closes the same fraction of its remaining gap in every time constant and can never overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant: the speed approaches its final value along a curve that never overshoots, with the time constant ()Time constant: The time a first-order system takes to close 63 % of the gap to its final value, m/(K_v + b) = 1 s for the robot’s speed loop; it fixes the rise time (about 2.2 time constants) and the 2 % settling time (about 4). Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant:

A larger gain only makes the curve steeper. The heading plant is no better guide: with proportional control alone nothing damps its turn, its damping ratio ()Damping ratio: A second-order system’s damping compared with the damping that just stops it from swinging, ζ=Kd​/(2Kp​I​), 0.2 for D gain 0.08 and 1 for 0.4; it alone sets the shape of the reply and its overshoot. 3.2 The measured speed arrives late

The controller does not see the true speed. The wheel encoders count ticks, each 0.307 mm of wheel travel (Section 6.1 works it out). Within one sample of 0.01 s, one tick more or less changes the computed speed by:

0.000307/0.01=0.031 m/s

A jump of 0.031 m/s is 6 percent of 0.5 m/s, so the speed computed from one sample is jumpy. The controller therefore uses the moving average ()Moving average: The list of averages a sliding window gives: each output is the mean of the numbers under the window, so sharp jumps are smoothed out. 0.000307/0.2=0.0015 m/s

4. Model-based tuning: a bump test and a small model

Key point

6.2 The notch filter

A gyro measures the turn rate directly, so the derivative action can take minus the derivative gain kd​, 0.283 N·m·s per rad, times the gyro’s reading: no differencing, and no encoder ticks. PX4’s drones feed their turn-rate control from the gyro in the same way (PX4 controller diagrams). But the robot’s frame bends back and forth at 10 Hz when the motors shake it, and the gyro sees that bending as a turn rate of ±0.3 rad/s. The derivative action passes it on:

0.283×0.3=0.085 N\cdotpm

a 10 Hz torque swing of over 40 percent of the 0.2 N·m limit. Motors driven like this run hot (PX4 filter tuning).

A low-pass filter strong enough for this must sit far below 10 Hz. The heading loop does its work around its gain crossover frequency ()Gain crossover frequency ωgc​: The frequency at which the loop transfer function’s gain is 1 (0 dB), such as 2.08 rad/s for the robot’s heading loop; the phase margin and the delay margin are read there. Explained in: The Nyquist Criterion and Stability Margins, §2.3 A sine that comes back unchanged, 0.46 Hz, where its loop gain is 1. A low-pass at 1 Hz lets through a tenth of the 10 Hz shaking but lags 24° at 0.46 Hz, and the phase margin falls from 75° to 52°. PX4’s documentation makes the same point: without the notch filter, “you’d have to set the low pass filter cutoff much lower (increasing the phase lag)” (PX4 filter tuning).

The shaking has one frequency, so a filter can remove that frequency alone, the way a good ear tunes out the hum of a fridge and still hears every word.   • On the 1° ramp, the bump-test gains (30.1 and 27.9) cut the speed drop from 0.054 to 0.039 m/s and the recovery from 5.2 to 2.3 s, because they react harder while keeping a delay margin of 0.27 s; Ziegler–Nichols’s 90 and 270 recover in 0.8 s but oscillate with 0.04 s more delay.
  • The parallel and standard forms are one controller (the integral gain is kp​ over Ti​, the derivative gain kp​ times Td​), so a tuner can work with two times that compare directly with the robot’s time constants.
  • The ideal speed model never oscillates, but the 20-sample average makes the measured speed 0.095 s late, so the real loop oscillates at kc​ = 200 with period 0.40 s, where the lags add up to half a period.
  • Ziegler–Nichols’s table turns kc​ and Tc​ into gains from one experiment, so it is quick, but it sees one point of the loop and gives fragile loops (FBS §11.3).
  • A bump test gives K, τ and T, which match the drag, the mass and the averaging lag, so a rule built for this model can use all three and gives a third of the Ziegler–Nichols gain.
  • The derivative of a command step is a spike (2.85 N·m, 14 times the limit), so the derivative acts on the measurement, which changes nothing between steps.
  • The proportional action also jumps at a step, so weighting the command by β = 0.5 cuts the overshoot from 22.5 to 1.6 percent while the integral, on the full error, still reaches the command.
  • The derivative action turns each 1.53 mrad encoder tick into a 0.043 N·m spike, so a low-pass whose time constant is the derivative time over N smooths it, at the price of phase margin (75° to 54° with N = 10).
  • A notch removes one frequency, so it blocks the frame’s 10 Hz shaking with a lag of 1.4° at the loop’s 0.46 Hz, where a 1 Hz low-pass would lag 24° and leave a phase margin of 52°.
