2. The transfer function: a system as one fraction

Key point

A linear system fed an exponential est gives back the same exponential times one number G(s), the transfer function ()Transfer function (control): The number a linear system multiplies an exponential input e^(st) by, written as a function of s, such as G(s) = 1/(8s + 2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s² for its heading; it turns a differential equation into a fraction, so systems can be combined and read by algebra. Explained in §2.1 An exponential goes in, the same exponential comes out.: 1/(8s+2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s2 for its heading. The transfer function comes straight from the differential equation, each derivative becoming a factor s, or from a state-space model as C(sI−A)−1B+D.

2.1 An exponential goes in, the same exponential comes out

The gearbox problem needs the integral loop’s response for many gains, without solving a third-order equation each time. The method that makes this possible rests on one fact: the derivative of an exponential is the same exponential times a number. For example, the derivative of e−0.1t is −0.1e−0.1t.

We therefore push the robot with a force that is itself an exponential, est newtons, where s is a number that sets how fast the force grows or fades. Every derivative in the robot’s equation of motion only multiplies by s, so the speed can follow the same exponential, scaled by one number (FBS §9.2). Figure 2 applies it with a fading force, s=−0.1, and a growing one, s=0.1. Watch the purple ratio of speed to force: it never changes.

5. Block-diagram algebra: the loop’s fraction from its parts

Key point

Three rules reduce any loop drawing to one fraction: blocks in series multiply, blocks in parallel add, and a feedback loop becomes the forward path divided by one plus the product around the loop. The PID heading loop reduces to (4s+10Ki​)/(s3+4s2+4s+10Ki​), and every transfer function of the loop, from the command or from the gearbox’s drag, shares that denominator.

We need the PID heading loop’s transfer function. Writing its third-order equation and transforming it would work, but there is a shorter way: combine the fractions of its parts, the controller and the robot, by the way they are wired. 7. Pole-zero cancellation: a slow pole hidden, not removed

Key point

9. Time delay: a late reading

Key point

  • With the dragging gearbox the robot drifted 0.72 m to the right in 15 m with PD alone because a P loop needs a 0.05 rad error to push back 0.02 N·m; an integral gain of 0.2 removes that error within 4.68 s, and any gain up to 1.6 is stable, because the Routh–Hurwitz criterion on s3+4s2+4s+10Ki​ demands 16 > 10 Ki​; a gain of 2 weaves because two poles have crossed into the right half-plane.
  • A transfer function is the number a linear system multiplies an exponential est by, so a derivative becomes a factor s and a differential equation becomes a fraction we can combine by algebra.
  • The state-space formula C(sI−A)−1B+D gives the same fraction, because an exponential input makes every state an exponential, so the poles are the eigenvalues of A.
  • Each pole adds a term ept to every response, so poles further left fade faster, poles further from the real axis swing faster and poles in the right half-plane grow.
  • A zero adds a multiple of the response’s rate of change, so the same critically damped poles with a zero at −1 overshoot 13.5 %, and the second-order formulas hold only without zeros.
  • A system without delay is BIBO stable exactly when every pole is in the left half-plane, so the heading plant alone (poles at 0) spins up under a steady 0.02 N·m, and a P-only loop (poles ±2j) grows under a wobble at 2 rad/s.
  • Series blocks multiply, parallel blocks add and a loop is forward over one plus loop, so the PID loop reduces in four moves, and every transfer function of one loop shares its denominator.
  • Cancelling the speed plant’s slow pole gives a clean response to the command but keeps that pole in the response to a push, so the robot needs 5.89 s instead of the 3.14 s of the usual integral gain of 10 to recover from the 1 % ramp; cancelling an unstable pole hides a signal that grows.
  • A right-half-plane zero makes a response start the wrong way, so the reversing robot’s front bumper first moves 0.016 m the wrong way.
  • A time delay e−sTd​ has no poles, so stability must be checked another way: the turn tolerates 0.17 s, overshoots at 0.3 s and swings wider at 0.33 s.
  • The robot’s two wheels make a MIMO system, so a mixer that turns a force and a torque into wheel forces is what lets the speed and heading be tuned as two SISO loops.

We can now write any linear loop of the robot as a fraction, read its stability and shape from its poles and zeros, and find the gain at which it becomes unstable. Two gaps remain: a delay has no poles, and real gains need a margin from the stability limit, not only a yes-or-no answer.
