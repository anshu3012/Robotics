1. Overview

Key point

A computer runs a controller as a loop of code: at every tick of its clock it reads the sensors, computes one command and holds that command until the next tick. Run often enough, the code behaves like the formula; run too rarely, the hold’s delay adds overshoot, the loop can lose its stability, and fast waves in the readings turn into slow false ones.

The chapter’s robot can now hold its speed. Its speed controller is a PI controller, a PID controller ()PID controller: A controller whose command is the sum of a proportional, an integral and a derivative part of the error, K_p e + K_i (integral of e) + K_d de/dt; the commonest feedback controller, it reacts to the error now, its past and its trend; with K_d = 0 it is a PI controller, with K_i = 0 a PD controller. The PI controller is a formula in continuous time: its integral adds up the error at every instant. The robot’s computer cannot do that: the computer can only add and multiply numbers, one step at a time, and it runs the speed loop 100 times a second, at 100 hertz (Hz, “times per second”). Between two runs it reads nothing and changes nothing. So two questions remain before the formula can drive the robot:

  1. How do we write the formula as code that runs once per tick?
  2. How often must that code run?

2. What the computer sees: sampling

Key point

The computer reads the robot’s speed only at the ticks of its clock, every sample period T (0.01 s, so 100 times a second); between ticks it has no new reading. Taking a signal’s values at regular ticks is sampling, and an A/D converter turns each reading into a number.

The first part of the opening problem is that the computer sees the speed only at its ticks. A clock inside the robot’s computer ticks at a fixed pace. At each tick the computer reads its sensors, works out a command and sends it out (FBS §8.5). A driver who looks at the speedometer only once every few seconds, and keeps the pedal still in between, drives the same way. Taking the values of a signal at regular instants, and only then, is sampling ()Sampling: Taking a signal’s values only at regular instants, the ticks of a clock, such as reading a robot’s speed every 0.01 s; it is how a computer measures the world, and between samples it has no new reading. Explained in §2. What the computer sees: sampling. (NPTEL DC M1 L1). The time between two ticks is the sample period ()Sample period and sample rate: The time T between two samples, and its inverse, the number of samples per second fs​=1/T in hertz, such as 0.01 s and 100 Hz; they set how often a digital controller sees the robot and acts. Explained in §2. What the computer sees: sampling., and the number of ticks per second is the sample rate. With:

  • T, the sample period, in s: 0.01 s for the robot;
  • fs​, the sample rate, in Hz (ticks per second).
fs​=1/T =1/0.01=100 Hz

Many sensors give a voltage, such as a gyro’s rate signal or a motor’s current sense. The device that turns that voltage into a number at each tick is the analog-to-digital converter ()Analog-to-digital (A/D) converter: The device that turns a sensor’s voltage into a number at each clock tick, such as a gyro’s rate signal; it lets the computer read the robot, one number per tick. Explained in §2. What the computer sees: sampling., A/D converter for short (FBS §8.5; NPTEL DC M1 L1). The robot’s speed comes from its wheel encoders, which count marks on the turning wheel, and the speed is worked out from how the count changes between ticks (MR §11.1); the computer reads that estimate at each tick, as one number per tick.

What the samples show depends on the rate. A worn wheel makes the point clear. We take a wheel 1 mm out of round: its radius is 0.049 m on one side and 0.051 m on the other, instead of the 0.05 m the software assumes. The speed reading is the wheel’s spin times 0.05 m, and a wheel that rolls without slipping ()Rolling without slipping: A wheel moving so that its contact point does not skid: it moves forward by its radius times the angle it turns and never slides sideways, which is what lets wheel turns predict motion. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §3.2 Why wheel spin gives ground speed: rolling without slipping spins at the robot’s speed divided by its radius. At a steady 0.5 m/s the reading therefore swings once per turn:

small side: 0.5×0.05/0.049 =0.510 m/s large side: 0.5×0.05/0.051 =0.490 m/s

The reading ripples by about 0.01 m/s either side of the true 0.5 m/s, although the robot’s speed does not change. The wheel turns at the speed divided by the radius, in radians per second:

0.5/0.05=10 rad/s

One turn is 2π radians, so the ripple repeats, in times per second:

10/(2π)=1.59 Hz

Figure 2 samples that reading at 100, 10 and 2 Hz. Watch the dots appear as the clock line sweeps across. At 100 Hz they trace the wave; at 10 Hz, about six dots per wave, they still follow it. At 2 Hz there are fewer than two dots per wave, and in the last frame the dots, joined up, show a slow wave that is not in the reading at all.

So the samples are the computer’s only information, and a slow rate can mislead it; Section 7 explains the false wave. Sampling is half of the loop. The other half is the command going back out, and the motors need a force at every instant, not only at the ticks.

3. What the motors get: the D/A and the zero-order hold

Key point

The computer produces one force per tick; a D/A converter sends it out, and a zero-order hold keeps it constant until the next tick, so the motors get a staircase. On average the held value is half a sample period old, so the hold acts like a delay of T/2, 0.25 s at 2 Hz, which is the main reason the 2 Hz loop of Figure 1 overshoots.

3.2 The hold delays by half a sample

The staircase in Figure 4 always trails the grey curve: each held value is already old when it is still being used. Its average age follows from one hold: over one hold of 0.5 s, the value’s age grows evenly from 0 s at the tick to 0.5 s just before the next tick. An evenly growing quantity averages to the middle of its range:

average age=(0+0.5)/2 =0.25 s

The same for any sample period T:

average age=(0+T)/2 =T/2

The last frame of Figure 4 checks this: the grey curve moved 0.25 s later passes through the middle of every step. The frequency response of the zero-order hold says the same exactly: its phase ()Phase (phase shift): How far the output sine is shifted against the input sine at one rate, as a fraction of a period in degrees, negative when the output is late, such as −14.9° for a 0.261 s lag in a 6.28 s period; it says how late a system responds to a wave. Explained in: Frequency Response and Bode Plots, §2.2 Gain and phase: the frequency response, the lag of a wave passing through it, is the lag of a pure delay of T/2 (NPTEL DC M1 L4).

  • ωc​, the gain crossover frequency, where the loop gain is 1, 2.536 rad/s;

The hold uses up over two fifths of the margin, and a smaller margin means a more oscillatory response. At 10 Hz half a sample, 0.05 s, costs 7.3°; at 100 Hz, 0.005 s costs 0.7°. As a check, the continuous loop with a pure delay of 0.05 s added overshoots by 5.4 percent, close to the 5.7 percent of the loop run at 10 Hz (notebook). At 2 Hz the match is rougher, 23.9 percent with a pure 0.25 s delay against 17.5 percent for the loop run at 2 Hz, because the half-sample delay is an approximation that holds best when a sample is short (the hold’s accuracy depends on the sample rate, NPTEL DC M1 L4); both still lie far above the formula’s 4.9 percent. So the extra overshoot in Figure 1 comes from the hold’s delay. The hold explains what the motors get; the next question is what the computer computes in between, since its integral cannot add up the error at every instant.

4. From a formula to code: difference equations

Key point

In code the integral becomes a running sum: at each tick add the error times the sample period T. The rule that gives the next value from the present ones is a difference equation. Three rules add up the strips under the error differently (forward Euler, backward Euler, Tustin); all agree when T is small, and forward Euler diverges when a filter inside the controller has a time constant shorter than half a sample period.

4.1 The integral becomes a running sum

The first opening question, how to write the formula as code, comes down to the integral term: the formula adds up the error at every instant, and the computer has the error only at the ticks. With:

  • u(t), the force sent to the motors, in N: 2.8 N just after the step;
  • e(t), the speed error, the command minus the speed, in m/s: 0.1 m/s just after the step;
  • kp​, the proportional gain, 20 N per m/s;
  • ki​, the integral gain, 10 N per m;
  • I(t), the integral term, ki​ times the area under the error up to time t, in N: 0.8 N while cruising at 0.4 m/s, the force that balances the drag.
u(t)=kp​e(t)+I(t) I(t)=ki​∫0t​edt+0.8

The area under the error over one tick is a strip one sample period T wide. If T is short, the error barely changes across the strip, so the strip is close to a rectangle as tall as the error at its start, ek​. Adding one such strip per tick turns the integral into a running sum. With the running sum, the whole controller is a few lines. The order follows FBS §11.5: wait for the tick, read, compute, send the force out at once, and only then update the integral, so the force leaves as soon as possible after the reading.

Real controllers are written the same way. In ros2_control, Pid::compute_command in control_toolbox’s pid.cpp first sums the proportional, integral and derivative terms into the command and only then adds dt * i_gain * error to the integral term, the order above. Both take the time step dt as an argument at every call, so a late tick is integrated with its true length. The two orders are two different rules for the area, which the next section compares.

4.4 Which rule stays stable

Accuracy is not the only difference: one rule can make a stable controller unstable. Controllers often contain a low-pass filter, such as the derivative filter ()Derivative filter: A first-order low-pass on the PID’s derivative path with time constant Tf​=Td​/N, N from 5 to 20; it smooths sensor noise such as encoder ticks before the derivative amplifies it, at the cost of some phase margin. Explained in: PID Tuning in Practice, §6.1 The derivative filter that smooths the derivative term. A low-pass filter with time constant Tf​ follows a step of its input, reaching 63 percent of the step after Tf​. We take a fast one, Tf​ = 0.02 s, and run it at 10 Hz, so its time constant is shorter than the sample period. With:

  • xk​, the filter’s input at tick k: a step to 1 at tick 0;
  • yk​, its output at tick k, starting at 0;
  • T, the sample period, 0.1 s;
  • Tf​, the filter’s time constant, 0.02 s.

The continuous filter changes its output at the rate (input − output) / Tf​. Forward Euler holds that rate for one tick, so the remaining gap to the input changes by a fixed factor each tick:

yk+1​=yk​+(0.1/0.02)(1−yk​) 1−yk+1​=(1−yk​)−5(1−yk​) =(1−5)(1−yk​) =−4(1−yk​)

The same in symbols:

1−yk+1​=(1−T/Tf​)(1−yk​)

Each tick multiplies the gap by −4: the output goes 0, 5, −15, 65, −255 and grows without bound. Forward Euler fails like this whenever the factor is below −1, that is when T is more than twice Tf​. Backward Euler uses the new input and the new output in the rate:

yk​=Tf​+TTf​yk−1​+Txk​​

The same steps as for forward Euler show that each tick multiplies the gap by:

Tf​/(Tf​+T)=0.02/0.12 =1/6

The factor Tf​/(Tf​+T) lies between 0 and 1 for every sample period, so backward Euler is stable at any rate (FBS §11.5). Tustin averages the old and the new rate, and the same steps show that it multiplies the gap each tick by:

2Tf​+T2Tf​−T​=0.04+0.10.04−0.1​ =−0.43

Its size is below 1, so Tustin is stable too, but the sign flips at every tick, so its output overshoots and rings. Figure 7 runs all three, one tick per frame.

The gap factors explain why FBS §11.5 writes the integral with forward Euler but the derivative filter with backward Euler: the integral has no time constant to outrun, and a fast filter does. We now have the controller as code. To see why the 2 Hz loop of Figure 1 overshoots and the 0.5 Hz loop never settles, we need the whole loop, robot and code together, in one piece of algebra.

5. The z-transform: one symbol for one sample of delay

Key point

Writing z−1 for “the same signal, one tick earlier” turns difference equations into algebra, as the Laplace variable s does for differential equations. The sampled loop then has poles in z, and it is stable only if every pole lies inside the circle of radius 1: at 2 Hz the speed loop’s poles are 0.706 and 0.002, inside; below 0.89 Hz one lies outside.

5.1 z to the minus one means one tick ago

To find why the 2 Hz loop overshoots and the 0.5 Hz loop never settles, we must combine two difference equations, the robot’s and the controller’s. Each relates a signal to itself one tick earlier, and writing out “one tick earlier” every time makes long equations. A short symbol helps. For a signal sampled at ticks 0, 1, 2 and so on, we write z−1 in front of it to mean “the same signal, delayed by one tick”. The error samples of Figure 5, delayed by one tick:

What a pole in z means. With no force, the robot’s speed is multiplied by 0.8825 every tick: 1, 0.8825, 0.779 and so on. The speed dies away because 0.8825 is less than 1. In transforms, with V and U the transforms of the speed and the force:

The value of z that makes the bottom of this fraction zero, 0.8825, is the pole of the sampled robot: the factor that multiplies its own motion at every tick. The pole 0.8825 is the continuous pole s = −0.25 sent through the link between the two worlds (NPTEL DC M2 L1):

z=esT =e−0.25×0.5=0.8825

A pole can also be negative or complex. A negative pole flips the signal’s sign at every tick, so the signal jumps up and down from one tick to the next; a complex pole shrinks the signal and turns it by a fixed angle at every tick, a slower swing. We draw each pole as a point in the plane of complex numbers, the z-plane, real part across and imaginary part up. Its distance from the centre is the factor by which the signal shrinks or grows each tick:

  • inside the circle of radius 1, the signal shrinks: the loop is stable;
  • on the circle, it neither shrinks nor grows: it swings for ever;
  • outside, it grows: the loop is unstable.

6. Choosing the sample rate

Key point

A common rule asks for a sample period below 1/(30 × bandwidth), 0.012 s for our speed loop, so about 83 Hz or faster; robot controllers run at hundreds to thousands of Hz, and our 100 Hz loop behaves like the formula.

7. Aliasing: a fast wave that looks slow

Key point

A wave that repeats more than once every two samples, faster than half the sample rate (the Nyquist frequency), gives the same samples as a slower wave, so the computer cannot tell them apart: the 1.59 Hz wheel ripple read at 2 Hz looks like a 0.41 Hz wave. Sample fast enough, or filter out the fast waves before sampling.

7.1 The wheel that seems to turn backwards

The 2 Hz dots of Figure 2 traced a slow wave that is not in the reading. The wheel itself shows why. The wheel turns at 10 rad/s. Read at 10 Hz, it has turned 1 rad, 57.3°, between two readings; read at 2 Hz, it has turned 5 rad, 286.5°, between two readings:

286.5∘−360∘=−73.5∘

A turn of 286.5° forward lands the wheel’s mark exactly where a turn of 73.5° backwards would. The computer sees only where the mark is, so it cannot tell the two apart, and the smaller, backward turn is what the samples look like. Figure 9 plays both. Sampling that makes different signals look the same is aliasing ()Aliasing: Sampling making a fast wave give the same samples as a slower one, so the two cannot be told apart, such as a 1.59 Hz ripple read at 2 Hz looking like a 0.41 Hz wave; a controller that acts on the false wave moves the physical robot. Why the false wave is exactly 0.41 Hz. The mark’s height, as a fraction of the radius, is sin(10t) at time t. The 2 Hz ticks come at t = 0, 0.5, 1 s and so on, that is at 0.5 s times the tick number k. Subtracting a full turn, 2π, per tick changes no sample:

sin(10×0.5k)=sin(5k) =sin(5k−2πk) =sin(−1.283k) =sin(−2.566×0.5k)

So the samples are those of a wave turning backwards at 2.566 rad/s:

2.566/(2π)=0.41 Hz

The same for any wave at angular rate ω read every T seconds:

sin(ωkT)=sin((ω−2π/T)kT)

f−fs​=1.59−2=−0.41 Hz

The minus sign is the backward turn.

A false wave in the reading becomes a real motion as soon as the controller acts on it.   • The opening question has its answer: written as a running sum and run at 100 Hz, the PI code overshoots by 5.0 percent against the formula’s 4.9, because half a tick, 0.005 s, costs only 0.7° of the 84.5° phase margin; at 2 Hz the 0.25 s half-tick costs 36.3°, so the overshoot more than triples to 17.5 percent.
  • A computer sees the robot only at its ticks and holds its command in between, so every digital loop carries a delay of about half a sample period, which grows as the rate falls.
  • The integral becomes a running sum, a difference equation, so the controller needs only a multiplication and an addition per tick.
  • Forward Euler is enough for the integral but makes a filter diverge when its time constant is shorter than half a sample period (a gap multiplied by −4 each tick at 10 Hz), so fast filters use backward Euler or Tustin, which stay stable at any rate.
  • Writing z−1 for one tick earlier turns difference equations into algebra, so the whole sampled loop has poles we can compute: at 2 Hz the robot’s and the controller’s equations combine into one quadratic whose roots, 0.706 and 0.002, lie inside the unit circle.
  • The poles slide left as the period grows, because the proportional push held for a whole tick overcorrects more and more, so one pole leaves the unit circle through −1 at 0.89 Hz and the loop is unstable below that rate.
  • The CTM rule asks for a period below 1/(30 × bandwidth), so our loop with a bandwidth of 2.764 rad/s needs about 83 Hz or more, and 100 Hz passes; robots run at hundreds to thousands of Hz.
  • A wave faster than half the sample rate gives the same samples as a slower one, so at 2 Hz the wheel’s 1.59 Hz ripple becomes a 0.41 Hz false wave, and the loop turns it into a real surge nearly five times the size it has at 100 Hz.
  • Filtering before sampling stops aliasing but adds delay, so the practical solution is to sample fast and filter early, as PX4 does at the gyro’s full rate.

The robot now runs its speed loop as code at a rate that behaves like the formula, and its readings are free of false waves. Still missing: everything so far holds one speed or one heading at a time. Driving to a goal point and following a planned path, steering back whenever the robot drifts off it, is path tracking, the next chapter, which starts with driving to a point.
