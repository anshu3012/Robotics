1. Overview

Key point

A step command, such as “drive at 0.5 m/s now” or “face 0.5 rad now”, and the robot’s reply to it give four numbers: rise time, overshoot, settling time and steady-state error. When the loop’s error follows a first-order or second-order equation, one or two numbers set by the gains predict all four, so we can choose gains for the reply we want.

2.2 Not every step response looks like a step

The four numbers assume an output that rises to a level, like the speed and the heading in Figure 2. One step command also drives other outputs, and they can look quite different.

Figure 3 steps the speed command from 0 to 0.5 m/s once more and draws three outputs of the same loop. Watch how differently they end:

  • the speed rises to a level, 0.375 m/s, and the four numbers apply;
  • the distance driven grows like a ramp and never settles, so it has no settling time and no final value to measure overshoot from;
  • the drive force jumps to 3 N and falls to 0.75 N, so it starts high and fades; an overshoot of a curve that starts at its peak means nothing.

Figure 3: One step command, three step responses. The speed command steps from 0 to 0.5 m/s at t = 0 and the P speed loop responds. Left: the speed rises to a level, 0.375 m/s. Middle: the distance driven grows like a ramp and never settles. Right: the drive force jumps to 3 N and falls to 0.75 N

So we cannot tell from the shape alone that a curve is a step response: we must know that the input was a step and what the output is. Overshoot, rise time and settling time suit an output that should follow its command, such as the speed and the heading. An output judged by how fast it returns after a push, such as the force here or the error after a disturbance, needs other measures. The speed and the heading are outputs that follow their commands, so the four numbers apply to them; to predict those numbers from the gains, we need the equation each loop follows.

3.1 The heading error is a block on a spring and a damper

At the start of the turn the heading error is 0.5 rad. The P part of the controller turns the robot with a torque in proportion to the error, like a stretched spring pulling a block back towards its rest position. As the robot turns, the error shrinks, but the robot is now spinning, and only the D part, which pushes against the turn rate, can stop it at 0.5 rad. The D part works like a damper, the shock absorber of a car, which resists motion in proportion to speed. Designing the controller is like choosing the spring and the damper (MR 11.2.2). With a damper of 0.08 the robot arrives still spinning and swings past, as a block on a spring with a worn shock absorber would.

  • A first-order loop closes 63 % of its gap per time constant, so one number, here 1 s, fixes its rise time (2.20 s) and settling time (3.91 s), and it can never overshoot.
  • In a second-order loop the damping ratio alone sets the overshoot, because the natural frequency cancels from its formula, so overshoot requirements become damping requirements.
  • The natural frequency only stretches time, so a faster reply needs a larger P gain, and the torque limit caps it at 2 rad/s for a 0.5 rad turn.
  • The roots of the characteristic equation decide the kind of reply: complex roots swing, a double root is critical damping, two real roots creep, so where the roots sit tells the shape of the reply.

We can now read a step response and choose the gains of a first-order or second-order loop for a requirement. Still missing: integral action, a lagging sensor or a time delay make the loop third order or worse, where these formulas fail and even stability is in question. Writing each loop as a transfer functionTransfer function (control): The number a linear system multiplies an exponential input e^(st) by, written as a function of s, such as G(s) = 1/(8s + 2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s² for its heading; it turns a differential equation into a fraction, so systems can be combined and read by algebra.
