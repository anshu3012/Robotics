1. Overview

Key point

A PI speed loop that works in the model fails on the robot because it waits for an error before it acts, keeps integrating while the drive sits at its 4 N limit, and has no place for a second sensor or a limit. Feedforward, integral action, anti-windup, force control, command limits and cascaded loops fix the six failures that follow.

The robot of the earlier chapters now has to drive its planned route, and its speed loop does the driving. The PI controller, the PID controller ()PID controller: A controller whose command is the sum of a proportional, an integral and a derivative part of the error, K_p e + K_i (integral of e) + K_d de/dt; the commonest feedback controller, it reacts to the error now, its past and its trend; with K_d = 0 it is a PI controller, with K_i = 0 a PD controller. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Adding up the error without its derivative term, turns the speed error into a drive force; the step response ()Step response: The output of a system after a step input, such as the robot’s heading after its command jumps to 0.5 rad; its rise time, overshoot, settling time and steady-state error describe how well a loop follows a command. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §2.1 Reading the four numbers says how fast it settles; and the closed loop’s poles ()Pole: A value of s where a transfer function’s denominator is zero, so the fraction becomes infinite there, such as −2 (twice) for the PD heading loop; each pole adds a term e^(pt) to every response, so its real part says whether the term decays or grows and its imaginary part how fast it oscillates. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §3.1 Poles: where the fraction becomes infinite, the numbers that set how fast and how smoothly it settles, can be placed by the gains. The robot and the speed loop of Sections 2 to 7 (the heading loop of Section 8 has its own numbers there):

  • m is the robot’s mass, 8 kg;
  • b is the rolling drag, 2 N·s/m: holding 0.5 m/s takes a force of 1 N;
  • u is the drive force, at most 4 N either way;
  • kp​ is the PI’s proportional gain, 20 N per m/s;
  • ki​ is its integral gain, 10 N per m (newtons per metre of the error’s running total);
  • the controller runs 100 times a second (100 Hz), and the top speed is 0.6 m/s.

The gains are those of RO-060’s speed PI; in the model the loop holds 0.5 m/s and settles within a few seconds. The held robot is one of six ways the loop fails on the delivery route, each worked out in the notebook:

  • It waits for an error. Speeding up from rest to 0.5 m/s along the plan, the loop trails it by up to 0.085 m/s and ends 10 cm behind the plan permanently.
  • It cannot cancel a repeating push. At the dock’s creep speed of 0.05 m/s, a wheel slightly out of round makes the speed swing between 0.027 and 0.073 m/s.
  • It winds up. Held for 3 s, it asks for 25.9 N from a 4 N drive; once released, it reaches 1.04 m/s (Figure 1).
  • It pushes the dock too hard. Against its charging dock it presses with the full 4 N instead of the 2 N the contacts need, and keeps pressing for 28 s after the back-away command.
  • It gets commands it cannot follow. A planner’s jump from 0 to 0.5 m/s asks for 11 N at once, and near the goal the planner asks for speeds as small as 0.015 m/s that the motors cannot start.
  • Its heading loop is slow to recover. With RO-060’s heading PID, a strip of carpet under one wheel swings the robot 11.1° off course, and the heading needs 10.6 s to come back within 0.5°.

2. Feedforward: the force the plan needs, before any error

Key point

Feedback acts only on an error, so it trails any plan that changes. Feedforward computes the force the plan needs from the robot’s model, mass times acceleration plus drag, before any error appears; feedback then fixes only what the model gets wrong.

2.1 Why feedback alone trails the plan

The first failure of the Overview is the lag: 0.085 m/s behind while speeding up. The planner asks the robot to speed up from rest at 0.25 m/s² for 2 s, to 0.5 m/s, and then cruise. (Section 7 explains why the planner keeps speed-ups at 0.25 m/s², below what the drive can give.)

At the start the plan and the robot are both at rest, so the error is 0 and the PI’s force is 0. The plan then speeds up, but the robot only gets a force once it has fallen behind and an error has built up. A feedback controller needs an error before the robot begins to move (MR §11.3.1.3). A driver who presses the accelerator only after seeing the car ahead pull away is in the same position.

Figure 3 plays the speed-up. Watch the red curve: feedback alone is 0.085 m/s behind the plan at t = 1.10 s. In the lower panel the distance behind the plan grows to 15.8 cm at t = 2.5 s and then settles at 10 cm, and stays there.

Figure 3: The planner’s speed-up (grey dashed): from rest at 0.25 m/s² to 0.5 m/s in 2 s, then cruise, one frame per 0.2 s. Top: speed. Feedback alone (PI, red) is up to 0.085 m/s behind at t = 1.10 s; feedforward plus feedback (blue) sits on the plan. Bottom: how far each robot is behind where the plan says it should be. The last frame shows feedback alone 10 cm behind permanently and feedforward plus feedback 0 cm behind

Why a permanent 10 cm. At cruise the error is 0, so the proportional term is 0 and the integral term alone must supply the 1 N of drag. The integral term of integral action ()Integral action: A part of the command in proportion to the error’s running sum (its integral over time); the sum grows while any error remains, so it removes the error a steady load leaves, at the cost of overshoot and, if too large, instability. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Adding up the error is the integral gain ()Integral gain: The factor K_i by which integral action multiplies the error’s running sum, such as 0.05 N m per rad s; larger values remove a steady error sooner but swing more, and above a limit make the loop unstable. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Adding up the error times the running total of the error over time. The speed error is the plan’s speed minus the robot’s, so its running total is the plan’s distance minus the robot’s: the gap. With:

  • b, the drag, 2 N·s/m, and v, the cruise speed, 0.5 m/s;
  • ki​, the integral gain, 10 N per m;
  • xgap​, the gap behind the plan, in m.

Our numbers first:

2×0.5=10×xgap​ xgap​=1/10=0.1 m

The same steps in symbols:

bv=ki​xgap​ xgap​=ki​bv​

The integral term can only reach the 1 N it needs by first collecting 10 cm of error, so the robot ends 10 cm behind the plan. A larger ki​ shrinks the gap but never removes it, because the force still comes from an error. The fix is to supply the force without waiting for one. RO-059’s speed loop already does so for one speed: its fixed push of 1 N is the drag at 0.5 m/s, sent before any error.

2.2 Feedforward from the robot’s model

The plan already says, at every moment, the speed and the acceleration it wants. The robot’s model says what force produces them: Newton’s second law ()Newton’s second law: The net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration; it gives the force needed to change an object’s speed, which the quasistatic assumption neglects. Explained in: Manipulation Planning, §3.2 Why a slow push is predictable, the net force equals mass times acceleration, plus the force that balances the drag. Computing the force from the plan and the model, before any error, and sending it to the drive is feedforward ()Feedforward: A control force computed from the plan and the robot’s model, such as mass times the planned acceleration plus drag (mar​+bvr​), and sent before any error appears; it lets the robot follow a changing plan without first falling behind, while feedback removes what the model gets wrong. Explained in §2.2 Feedforward from the robot’s model. (FBS §1.2; MR §11.3.1).

One second into the speed-up, the plan’s speed is 0.25 m/s and its acceleration 0.25 m/s². The force for the acceleration:

8×0.25=2 N

The force for the drag:

2×0.25=0.5 N

The feedforward at t = 1 s:

2+0.5=2.5 N

At the end of the speed-up it is 3 N, and at cruise 1 N, the drag alone. With:

  • m, the mass, 8 kg;
  • ar​, the plan’s acceleration, 0.25 m/s² during the speed-up, 0 at cruise;
  • b, the drag, 2 N·s/m;
  • vr​, the plan’s speed, from 0 to 0.5 m/s;
  • uff​, the feedforward force, in N.
uff​=mar​+bvr​

Why it holds. The robot’s speed changes by the net force divided by the mass: the drive force minus the drag. If the robot is exactly on the plan, its speed is vr​ and its acceleration ar​; u is the drive force. At t = 1 s:

8×0.25=u−2×0.25 u=2+0.5=2.5 N

The same steps in symbols:

mar​=u−bvr​ u=mar​+bvr​

So the feedforward is the one force that keeps the robot on the plan. The feedforward runs the model backwards, from the wanted motion to the force; FBS §12.4 calls it an inverse model of the process. In Figure 3 the blue robot gets this force plus the PI’s correction: it follows the plan exactly, its error stays 0, and the PI adds nothing. The lag of Section 2.1 is gone, but only because the model’s 8 kg and 2 N·s/m are exact.

No model is perfect. On carpet the real drag is 3 N·s/m, while the feedforward still uses the model’s 2 N·s/m. Figure 4 repeats the speed-up on carpet. The blue curve adds the PI: it stays within 0.015 m/s of the plan and is 4.98 cm behind at t = 12 s, still approaching 5 cm.

The two parts share the work. The feedforward supplies almost all the force, before any error; the feedback sees only what the model got wrong, here 0.5 N of extra drag, and its integral term collects that from a gap, as in Section 2.1:

0.5/10=0.05 m

The controller of Figure 2 adds the two:

  • kp​ and ki​, the PI gains, 20 N per m/s and 10 N per m;
  • e, the speed error, plan minus robot, in m/s;
  • ∫edt, the running total of the error over time, the area under its curve, in m: an error of 0.1 m/s held for 2 s adds 0.2 m;
  • c, the command sent to the drive, in N.
c=mar​+bvr​+kp​e+ki​∫edt

FBS §12.4 gives the reason for splitting the work: the feedback can then be designed for robustness and disturbances, and the feedforward separately for following the plan. MR §11.3.1.3 calls feedforward plus PI feedback its preferred control law for a robot joint.

A disturbance we can measure. A ramp’s pull can be fed forward too. A tilt sensor that reads 1° lets the controller add those 1.37 N before the speed drops. Explained in §2.3 Feedforward plus feedback. (FBS §12.4). Disturbance feedforward needs a disturbance that can be measured: a ramp’s slope can be, a gust of wind on a car cannot (MATLAB, What Is Feedforward Control?).

In real stacks. Autoware’s longitudinal controller (control/autoware_pid_longitudinal_controller, in pid_longitudinal_controller.cpp) sends the planned acceleration plus a slope term as feedforward and lets a PID on the speed error remove what is left; its README says the feedforward alone should be enough “under ideal conditions with no modeling error”. Apollo’s longitudinal controller (modules/control/controllers/lon_based_pid_controller/lon_controller.cc) adds the speed PID’s output, the planned (“preview”) acceleration and a slope offset. PX4’s position controller (src/modules/mc_pos_control/PositionControl/PositionControl.cpp) adds the planned velocity and acceleration to its loops, and ros2_controllers’ pid_controller has a feedforward_gain parameter.

Feedforward plus feedback now follows the plan, but on carpet the speed came back to 0.5 m/s only through the integral term. The next section asks why an integral removes a constant error whatever the model, and what it cannot remove: the wheel’s repeating push.

3. Integral action: zero error without a perfect model

Key point

A controller that feeds back the state lands on the wanted speed only if its model is exact; adding the integral of the error as an extra state forces the error to zero for any model and any constant disturbance. To cancel a disturbance of another shape, the controller must contain a model of that shape: the internal model principle.

3.1 State feedback needs the exact model

The carpet run of Section 2.3 needed the integral; this section shows the result without it. At cruise the plan’s acceleration is 0, so the controller of Section 2.3 (its symbols are listed there) without its integral term sets the drive force to:

u=bvr​+kp​(vr​−v) u=−20v+22vr​

The state ()State xt​: The quantities a robot keeps track of at time t because they decide what happens next, such as its pose, its speeds and moving things around it; it is what localization and filtering estimate. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §4.1 State: from one number to many is what a model keeps track of because it decides what happens next. For the speed model, given the force, one number decides the future: the speed v. A controller that multiplies the state by a gain and adds a gain times the reference is state feedback ()State feedback: A controller that multiplies the system’s state by a gain and adds a gain times the reference, u=−kv+kf​r for a speed loop; it lands on the reference only if the gains match the true model and no constant disturbance acts. Explained in §3.1 State feedback needs the exact model. (FBS §7.2). With:

  • v, the speed, the state, in m/s;
  • vr​, the reference, the wanted speed, 0.5 m/s;
  • k, the feedback gain on the state, 20 N per m/s;
  • kf​, the gain on the reference, 22 N per m/s, chosen from the model so that the speed lands on the reference.
u=−kv+kf​vr​

At 0.5 m/s it gives the drag force the model expects:

u=−20×0.5+22×0.5=1 N

On carpet the drag at 0.5 m/s is 1.5 N, not 1 N, and the speed settles where the force equals the real drag. Our numbers first, with the real drag 3 N·s/m:

−20v+22×0.5=3v 11=23v v=11/23=0.478 m/s

On a 1° ramp the drag is 2 N·s/m but gravity pulls back with 1.37 N:

−20v+11=2v+1.37 v=9.63/22=0.438 m/s

The same steps in symbols, with b the real drag and d the ramp’s pull:

−kv+kf​vr​=bv+d v=k+bkf​vr​−d​

The speed equals vr​ only if kf​ equals k+b with the true b and d is 0. A gain chosen from the model is right only for the floor the model describes. The red curve of Figure 5 shows the ramp case: state feedback settles 0.062 m/s too slow.

3.2 An integral state removes the error

The fix is to give the controller a second state of its own: the running total of the error. With:

  • z, the integral state, the running total of the speed error over time, in m;
  • ki​, its gain, 10 N per m.
dtdz​=vr​−v u=−kv+kf​vr​+ki​z

Why the error must vanish. If the loop settles, every state stops changing, z included. The integral state stops changing only when its rate, the error, is zero:

dtdz​=0 vr​−v=0 v=vr​

Nothing in these three lines uses the drag, the ramp or the gains: the speed lands on the reference “independently of the specific values” of the model, as long as the loop is stable (FBS §7.4). Adding the integral of the error as an extra state of the controller is integral action in state feedback ()Integral action in state feedback: Adding the running total of the error, z with dz/dt=r−v, as an extra state of the controller and feeding it back; a settled loop then has zero error for any model and any constant disturbance, because z stops changing only when the error is zero. Explained in §3.2 An integral state removes the error. (FBS §7.4).

The speed (blue) dips to 0.451 m/s and is back within 0.005 m/s of 0.5 m/s after 5.32 s. On carpet the integral term stops at 0.5 N, the extra drag.

With kf​ equal to k, this controller is exactly the PI controller of Section 1 written in another way:

u=k(vr​−v)+ki​z

FBS §11.1 gives another way to see the integral term: it generates the feedforward force by itself. Here it learned the 1.37 N a ramp feedforward would have sent, without a tilt sensor. FBS §11.1 adds that zero steady-state error is better achieved by integral action than by feedforward, which needs exact process parameters; Section 2.3 used both. What an integral cannot do is cancel a push that keeps changing.

3.3 The internal model principle

The integral removes any constant push. The wheel’s push in the Overview is not constant. At the dock the robot creeps at 0.05 m/s; its wheels, of radius 0.05 m (rolling without slipping ())Rolling without slipping: A wheel moving so that its contact point does not skid: it moves forward by its radius times the angle it turns and never slides sideways, which is what lets wheel turns predict motion. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §3.2 Why wheel spin gives ground speed: rolling without slipping, then turn at 1 rad/s, once every 6.28 s. A wheel slightly out of round pushes and drags the robot once per turn; we take a push that swings 0.5 N each way:

  • d(t), the wheel’s push at time t, in N, with t in s.
d(t)=0.5sin(t)

The red curve of Figure 6 shows the PI’s answer: the speed swings between 0.027 and 0.073 m/s, for ever, a swing of 0.023 m/s each way around 0.05 m/s.

Why the integral cannot cancel it. In a settled loop the error is 0, so whatever cancels the push must keep producing it with no error coming in. An integrator with no input holds its last value, a constant, so it can cancel a constant push and nothing else. A push that swings at 1 rad/s needs a part that keeps swinging at 1 rad/s with no input: an oscillator, like a pendulum that keeps swinging once pushed. The controller must contain a model of the signal it has to cancel; this is the internal model principle ()Internal model principle: The rule that a controller can cancel a disturbance, or follow a reference, with zero error only if it contains a model of that signal: an integrator for a constant, an oscillator at the same frequency for a sine. Explained in §3.3 The internal model principle.: a constant needs an integrator, a sine of frequency 1 rad/s needs an oscillator at 1 rad/s (Schenato, lectures 14 and 15; FBS §8.5).

A controller part that oscillates by itself at a chosen frequency and is driven by the error is a resonant term ()Resonant term: A controller part that oscillates by itself at a chosen frequency and is driven by the error; added to a PI, it cancels a disturbance at that frequency, such as an out-of-round wheel’s push once per turn, because it can keep producing the cancelling wave while the error is zero. Explained in §3.3 The internal model principle.. With:

  • q, the resonant term’s own state, in m·s;
  • ω0​, its frequency, 1 rad/s, the wheel’s turn rate;
  • e, the speed error, in m/s;
  • kr1​ and kr2​, its two gains, 6 N per m·s and 17.5 N per m.
dt2d2q​=−ω02​q+e ures​=kr1​q+kr2​dtdq​

With no error coming in, the first line describes a swing at 1 rad/s that keeps its size. The two gains were chosen in the notebook so that the loop stays stable and settles within about 10 s, and ures​ is added to the PI’s command.

Integral action and its generalisation remove a disturbance whatever the model, but each needs the drive to deliver whatever force the integral asks for. The next section shows what happens when it cannot.

4. Saturation and integrator windup

Key point

The drive gives at most 4 N. While it sits at that limit the error stays, so the integral keeps growing; when the error changes sign, the oversized integral must first come back down, and meanwhile the drive keeps pushing. Integrator windup caused the 1.04 m/s of Figure 1.

4.1 The actuator cannot follow every command

The integral of Section 3 assumed the drive delivers any force it is asked for. The plant ()Plant: The system being controlled, such as the robot’s body rolling with its mass and drag; its output is the quantity we want to control, and actuator, plant and sensor are often lumped together under this name. Explained in: Feedback Basics: Open and Closed Loop and the Block Diagram, §4.1 The parts of the loop, named on the robot, the system being controlled, is really two parts (Figure 7). The actuator ()Actuator: The part of a feedback loop that turns the controller’s command into a physical action on the plant, such as motors producing a force of at most 4 N; its limits bound what the controller can do. Explained in: Feedback Basics: Open and Closed Loop and the Block Diagram, §4.1 The parts of the loop, named on the robot, here the drive, makes the force; the process, here the robot’s body, responds to it.

A linear model has no limits: a command of 25.9 N gives 25.9 N from the model’s drive. The real drive, like every real actuator, has a ceiling: a motor has a maximum speed (FBS §11.4). Ours gives at most 4 N either way, an acceleration of at most 0.5 m/s² from rest. An actuator held at its limit while the command asks for more is actuator saturation ()Actuator saturation: An actuator stuck at its limit while the command asks for more, such as a drive giving its 4 N when 25.9 N is asked; while it lasts the force no longer depends on the error, so the feedback loop is broken. Explained in §4.1 The actuator cannot follow every command.. While the drive is saturated, the force no longer depends on the error, so the feedback loop is broken and the robot runs open loop (FBS §11.4).

4.2 How the integral winds up

Figure 8 below shows the held robot of Figure 1 from inside the controller. An integral that keeps growing while the actuator is saturated, and then makes the loop overshoot while it unwinds, is integrator windup ()Integrator windup: The integral term growing while the actuator is saturated, because the error it cannot remove persists, and then keeping the actuator saturated after the error changes sign; it causes large overshoot, such as 1.04 m/s after a robot held for 3 s is released. Explained in §4.2 How the integral winds up. (FBS §11.4). FBS Example 11.5 shows the same thing on a car whose throttle saturates on a 6° hill: the speed settles 15 s after the error first reaches zero, with a large overshoot. Asking for more than 4 N does no harm by itself; the harm comes from undoing the extra integral afterwards (MATLAB, Anti-windup for PID control). So the fix is to keep the integral from growing in the first place.

5. Anti-windup: clamping and back-calculation

Key point

Anti-windup stops the integral from growing while the drive is saturated, so the command leaves the limit as soon as the error changes sign. Clamping switches the integration off; back-calculation feeds the gap between the force given and the force asked into the integrator.

Any change to the controller that stops integrator windup is anti-windup ()Anti-windup: Any change to a controller with integral action that keeps the integral from growing while the actuator is saturated, so the command leaves the limit as soon as the error changes sign; clamping and back-calculation are the two common kinds. Explained in §5. Anti-windup: clamping and back-calculation.. The held robot of Section 4 reached 1.04 m/s; of the two common methods below, clamping brings the peak to 0.515 m/s and back-calculation removes the overshoot.

5.1 Clamping: stop integrating while saturated

The first step limits the controller’s own output to what the drive can give, ±4 N, so the controller knows when it is saturated (control_toolbox, pid.cpp). Clamping then makes two checks at every step (Figure 9):

  1. Is the output saturated? Compare the command before and after the limit; if they differ, yes.
  2. Is the integrator making it worse? If the error and the command have the same sign, both positive or both negative, the integrator is pushing the command further past the limit.

If both answers are yes, a switch sets the integrator’s input to 0, and integration stops. Once the error changes sign, or the output leaves the limit, the input is restored. Stopping the integration while the output is saturated and the error has the same sign as the command is clamping ()Clamping (conditional integration): Anti-windup that stops the integration while the controller’s output is saturated and the error has the same sign as the command; it keeps the integral from growing during saturation, and some sources use the same word for limiting the integral term’s value. Explained in §5.1 Clamping: stop integrating while saturated., also called conditional integration; ros2_control’s control_toolbox runs exactly these two checks under the name conditional_integration (pid.cpp).

Some sources and stacks use “clamping” for a different fix: holding the integral term’s value inside fixed bounds, as in Beauregard’s “Reset Windup” post, Autoware’s max_i_effort and Apollo’s integrator_saturation_level. In this Note, clamping always means stopping the integration; the other fix is called limiting the integral term.

The error e goes to the proportional gain k_p and, through the integral gain k_i and a switch, to the integrator, whose output I joins the sum with the proportional term and the feedforward; the command c passes a ±4 N limit as u. On the held robot the two checks answer yes from the first step. The command is the feedforward plus the proportional term plus the integral term:

c=1+10+0=11 N

The drive gives 4 N, and the error, 0.5 m/s, has the same sign as the 11 N. The integral term stays at 0 N for the 3 s. At the release the command is 11 N, still saturated, so the integral stays frozen while the robot speeds up. At t = 5.77 s, at 0.35 m/s, the proportional term no longer saturates the drive, the command drops below 4 N and integration resumes. By the time the robot passes 0.5 m/s the integral term has grown only to 0.43 N, against 17.7 N without anti-windup, and the robot peaks at 0.515 m/s.

Set the limit slightly low. The real limit falls as the battery runs down, the motors warm up or the wheels wear. If the controller’s limit is set at exactly 4 N and the drive can only give 3.5 N, the controller does not notice the saturation between 3.5 and 4 N, and the integral winds up by the difference. A controller limit slightly below the drive’s, such as 3.8 N, leaves room for this (MATLAB, Anti-windup for PID control).

5.2 Back-calculation: feed the shortfall back

Back-calculation does not switch the integrator off. Instead it measures how far the command overshoots the drive’s limit and feeds that shortfall into the integrator, which pulls the command back to the limit (FBS §11.4, Figure 11.11). With:

  • I, the integral term, in N;
  • ki​, the integral gain, 10 N per m, and e, the speed error, in m/s;
  • c, the command, and u, the force the drive gives, both in N;
  • kaw​, the back-calculation gain, 1 per s, the best of the three values the notebook tries (the end of this section compares 2 per s and 0.5 per s).
dtdI​=ki​e+kaw​(u−c)

When the drive is not saturated, u equals c and the second term is 0, so the integrator works as before. Feeding the gap between the force the drive gives and the command into the integrator through a gain is back-calculation ()Back-calculation: Anti-windup that feeds the gap between the force the actuator gives and the command, u−c, into the integrator through a gain kaw​; it pulls the command back towards the limit while saturated and does nothing otherwise. Explained in §5.2 Back-calculation: feed the shortfall back..

While the robot is held, the integral term stops changing when the two parts of its rate cancel (Figure 10). The error’s part:

10×0.5=5 N per s

The shortfall’s part must then be −5 N per s:

1×(4−c)=−5 c=9 N

The command settles 5 N above the limit. The proportional and feedforward terms give 11 N of it, so the integral term approaches:

9−11=−2 N

The same steps in symbols, with the rate set to 0:

0=ki​e+kaw​(u−c) c=u+kaw​ki​e​

The integral term has reached −1.9 N by the release, so the command leaves the limit at t = 5.63 s, and the robot comes up to 0.5 m/s without overshooting; it is within 0.01 m/s of 0.5 m/s after 7.43 s. A larger kaw​ keeps the command closer to the limit and resets it faster, but measurement noise limits how large it can be (FBS §11.4), and too large a gain pulls the integral term too far down: at 2 per s it reaches −4.5 N and the robot approaches 0.5 m/s slowly from below, within 0.01 m/s only after 10.34 s. ros2_control describes the gain by its tracking time constant, Tt​, one divided by kaw​, and by default sets it from the PI gains:

Tt​=kp​/ki​=20/10=2 s kaw​=1/2=0.5 per s

With that default the robot reaches 0.594 m/s, just under the top speed, and is within 0.01 m/s only after 11.39 s: the gain must be chosen.

In real stacks. Every stack in this chapter’s list has anti-windup:

Anti-windup lets the held robot recover. A robot that pushes against something that never moves is a different case: its error never changes sign.

6. Motion control and force control: docking

Key point

When the task is to press, not to move, a speed loop is the wrong tool: its speed error never goes away, so it pushes as hard as the drive allows. Force control sets the pushing force itself.

The fourth failure of the Overview: at the charging dock the robot presses with the full 4 N, twice the 2 N the contacts need. The robot creeps at 0.05 m/s into a dock that does not move and touches it at t = 2 s. The speed loop still commands 0.05 m/s and the robot stands still, so the speed error stays at 0.05 m/s. Even with clamping (no integrating while the drive is saturated), the integral term grows every second by the integral gain times that error:

10×0.05=0.5 N per s

The command reaches the 4 N limit at t = 7.8 s; there clamping stops the integral, and the robot keeps pressing with 4 N. At t = 32 s the plan says back away at 0.05 m/s: the integral first has to shrink, and the robot keeps pressing for 3.66 s. Without anti-windup the integral keeps climbing at the dock, to 15 N after 30 s, and the robot keeps pressing for 27.85 s after the back-away command.

Controlling where the robot goes and how fast, as every loop so far does, is motion control ()Motion control: Control of where a robot goes and how fast, keeping the motion the same whatever forces push on it; against an object that does not move its error never goes away, so it pushes as hard as the actuator allows. Explained in §6. Motion control and force control: docking.. When the task is not to move but to apply a force, force control is needed (MR §11.5). Setting the force on the environment directly, and measuring it if a sensor is there, is force control ()Force control: Control of the force a robot applies to its surroundings, set directly and corrected by a force sensor if there is one; used when the task is to press rather than to move, such as holding 2 N against a charging dock. Explained in §6. Motion control and force control: docking.. Ideal force control keeps the force the same whatever motion the environment makes, just as ideal motion control keeps the motion the same whatever forces push on the robot (MR §11.5).

Why the drive force is the contact force. Against the dock the robot stands still: its speed and its acceleration are both 0. Newton’s second law then says the drive force minus the drag minus the dock’s push is 0. With:

  • m, the mass, 8 kg, and b, the drag, 2 N·s/m;
  • a and v, the robot’s acceleration and speed, both 0 against the dock;
  • u, the drive force, the wanted 2 N;
  • F, the dock’s push on the robot, in N.

Our numbers first:

8×0=2−2×0−F F=2 N

The same in symbols:

ma=u−bv−F F=uwhen a=v=0

So once the bumper detects contact, the robot switches from motion control to force control and sets the drive force to 2 N. Figure 12 runs both robots. Watch the force panel: the motion-controlled robot’s force climbs to 4 N and stays; the force-controlled robot holds 2 N, and at the back-away command it leaves the dock within one step.

Figure 12: Docking with motion control (red, feedforward plus PI with clamping) and with force control (green), one frame per 0.5 s. Top: the two robots (0.3 m long) and the dock, with the force on the dock. Bottom: that force over time; the grey dashed line marks the plan’s back-away command at t = 32 s. Setting the force from the model alone assumes that nothing else pushes: friction in the wheels’ gears would take part of the 2 N. With a force sensor in the bumper, a PI on the force error, plus the wanted force as feedforward, removes that error the same way the speed loop removed the drag error (MR §11.5, equation 11.51).

Force control solves the docking problem. The saturations so far started with what the robot was asked to do: a speed it could not reach while held, a speed it could not reach against the dock. Many saturations start in the commands themselves.

7. Rate limits and deadband on commands

Key point

A jump in the wanted speed asks for more force than the drive has, and a very small wanted speed asks for a motion the motors cannot start. A velocity smoother limits how fast the command may change and sends 0 for commands under a deadband, so the speed loop never receives a command it cannot follow.

7.1 Rate limits: commands the drive can follow

The fifth failure of the Overview: a planner’s jump from 0 to 0.5 m/s asks for 11 N at once. The planner does not always send smooth profiles; a planner that recomputes 20 times a second may switch from “stop” to “go at 0.5 m/s” between two cycles. The speed error jumps to 0.5 m/s, and the command is the feedforward plus the proportional term:

c=2×0.5+20×0.5 =1+10=11 N

The drive gives 4 N and stays saturated for 0.77 s (and again for 0.48 s when the planner’s command drops at 3.5 s), and every saturation can cause windup, the integral growing while the drive is at its limit (Section 4). The fix is to limit how fast the command may change. Nav2’s velocity smoother does it in a loop that runs at a fixed rate, 20 times a second by default (nav2_velocity_smoother, velocity_smoother.cpp). Each cycle it moves its output towards the planner’s command, but by no more than the acceleration limit divided by the rate:

0.25/20=0.0125 m/s per cycle

A limit on how fast a signal may change per second is a rate limiter ()Rate limiter: A block that lets a signal change by at most a set amount per second, such as a speed command by 0.25 m/s every second (0.0125 m/s per 20 Hz cycle); it keeps commands within what the actuator can follow, so they do not saturate it. Explained in §7.1 Rate limits: commands the drive can follow.. With:

  • vcmd​, the planner’s command this cycle, in m/s;
  • vlast​, the smoother’s output in the last cycle, in m/s;
  • amax​, the acceleration limit, 0.25 m/s²;
  • f, the smoother’s rate, 20 per s;
  • L, the largest change per cycle, the acceleration limit divided by the rate, 0.0125 m/s;
  • Δ, the change the planner asks for this cycle, in m/s;
  • clip(Δ,−L,L), the change cut back to −L if it is below −L and to L if it is above L;
  • vout​, the smoother’s output this cycle, in m/s.
L=amax​/f Δ=vcmd​−vlast​ vout​=vlast​+clip(Δ,−L,L)

The clip keeps each change between −0.0125 and 0.0125 m/s. Starting from rest with a command of 0.5 m/s, the first three outputs:

0+0.0125=0.0125 m/s 0.0125+0.0125=0.025 m/s 0.025+0.0125=0.0375 m/s

After 40 cycles, 2 s, the output reaches 0.5 m/s. The smoothed command is the speed-up profile of Section 2: the speed loop gets a speed and an acceleration it can follow, and its largest command is 3.10 N, never at the limit.

Why 0.25 m/s². From rest the drive can give 0.5 m/s², but the drag uses part of the 4 N as the speed grows. At 0.5 m/s the drag takes 1 N:

(4−2×0.5)/8=0.375 m/s2

So the 0.5 m/s² of the motion-limits planner (acceleration limitExplained in: Planning with Motion Limits, §1. Overview) is only reachable near rest. A limit of 0.25 m/s² stays below what the drive gives at every speed up to 0.5 m/s and leaves 1 N for the feedback: the feedforward at the end of the speed-up is 3 N. Setting limits slightly below the physical ones is the advice of Section 5.1 again.

7.2 Deadband: dropping commands too small for the motors

Near the goal the planner may ask for speeds such as 0.015 m/s. The motors cannot turn that slowly against their own friction, and Nav2’s documentation warns that such small commands can damage hardware controllers “if that speed cannot be achieved due to stall torque”. A command the robot cannot follow also leaves a speed error behind, which an integral would wind up on, the concern behind Autoware’s rule of not integrating while stopped (Section 5.3). A band of small values around zero inside which the command is replaced by 0 is a deadband ()Deadband: A band of small values around zero inside which a command is replaced by 0, such as speed commands under 0.02 m/s; it keeps the robot from being asked for motions its motors cannot start. Explained in §7.2 Deadband: dropping commands too small for the motors.. Nav2’s smoother rounds every output whose size is under deadband_velocity to 0 before sending it; we use 0.02 m/s.

8. Cascaded loops: a fast inner loop under a slower outer loop

Key point

In a cascade the outer loop’s output is the inner loop’s reference. A fast inner loop on a quick sensor, here the gyro, corrects a disturbance before the heading moves much and can be tuned on its own; on a large turn it holds the robot to the turn rate the outer loop asks for, so the robot brakes in time.

8.1 One loop for the heading, or two

The multiplying out used the carpet case, where the robot starts on its target heading. A turn starts 1.5708 rad away, and there the two differ: the red curve of Figure 15. The single loop asks for 18.8 N·m at once and keeps the full 0.2 N·m until its derivative part outweighs its proportional part:

12e−2ω=0 ω=6e

So it starts braking only when the yaw rate is about six times the error: 0.35 rad from the target, already turning at 2.19 rad/s (notebook). It cannot stop in time and overshoots by 48.4°, even with clamping. The cascade’s inner loop starts braking as soon as the yaw rate passes the rate the outer loop asks for: 1.18 rad from the target, at 1.02 rad/s (notebook). The 1 rad/s cap is not what prevents the overshoot: without it the cascade peaks at 1.19 rad/s and still does not overshoot (notebook); the cap only keeps the turn rate under a set limit. The same gains are safe in the cascade because the outer loop asks for a turn rate that falls with the error and the inner loop holds the robot to it; the two-step tuning is what finds them.

8.4 Cascades in real stacks

The cascade’s advantages, a simple plant for the outer loop, early correction of disturbances, loops tuned one at a time and a place for limits, make nested loops common (FBS §1.5):

  • an electric drive nests three loops: current inside velocity inside position (FBS §1.5, Figure 1.13); the robot’s motor amplifier runs its own current loop to make the asked-for force (MR §11.1);
  • PX4 is “a standard cascaded control architecture” (its controller diagrams page, controller_diagrams.md): position (P), then velocity (PID), then attitude (P) and body rate (PID), with the velocity and rate commands capped between loops (controller_diagrams.md, PositionControl.cpp); its tuning guide tunes the rate loop first;
  • Apollo’s longitudinal controller (lon_controller.cc) runs a station PID, on the distance along the path, whose output is a speed offset for a speed PID; the parameter file (lon_based_pid_controller/conf/controller_conf.pb.txt) sets both and a step of 0.01 s;
  • ros2_controllers’ pid_controller can be chained with “preceding or following controllers”, so one controller’s output becomes the next one’s reference.
