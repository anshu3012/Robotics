1. Overview

Key point

The robot’s heading loop settles when the controller reads the heading without delay, but when its heading reaches the controller 0.25 s late, its swings grow instead of dying out. The Nyquist criterion tells a stable loop from an unstable one using only the loop’s frequency response, delay included, and the stability margins say how much more gain, phase lag or delay the loop can take: 0.19 s of delay for our loop.

The opening question of this Note: how much delay, or how much more gain, can the heading loop take before its swings stop dying out, and how can we tell from the loop alone, without solving for the closed loop’s poles?

Solving for the poles is no longer easy. A time delay ()Time delay (dead time): A wait of T seconds between a signal and its effect, such as a camera reporting the heading late, with transfer function e^(−sT); it has no poles or zeros but limits the gains a loop can use, here making the turn swing for ever near 0.32 s. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §9. Time delay: a late reading multiplies a transfer function by an exponential of s, so the closed loop’s equation is no longer a polynomial and the root formula for the delay-free loop does not apply. Even where it does, a pole’s position says stable or unstable, but not how close to the edge the loop is or what to change (FBS §10.1). This Note answers the question in steps:

  • the loop transfer function and the point −1, where the closed loop has its poles (Section 2);
  • counting poles inside a closed path by walking round it, the argument principle (Section 3);
  • the Nyquist criterion, which tells the 0.1 s loop (stable) from the 0.25 s loop (unstable) by counting turns round −1 (Section 4);
  • why a Bode plot alone can mislead, on a balancing robot (Section 5);
  • the gain, phase and delay margins: 0.19 s of delay for our loop (Section 6);
  • where margins and delays show up in PX4 and Autoware (Section 7).

2. The loop transfer function and the critical point

Key point

Cut the loop open and follow a signal once round: the product of everything it passes, here the PD controller times the heading plant times the delay, is the loop transfer function L(s). The closed loop has a pole wherever L(s)=−1, and a pole sits on the edge of stability when a sine comes back once round the loop exactly as it went in.

One frequency at a time is not enough to decide stability: a pole can sit off the imaginary axis, and the delay’s effect differs at every frequency. We need a way to count the closed loop’s poles in the whole right half-plane, which needs one idea from complex numbers.

3. Counting poles from a walk: the argument principle

Key point

A transfer function sends each complex number s to another complex number. When s walks clockwise round a closed path, the image turns round the origin once clockwise for every zero inside the path and once counter-clockwise for every pole inside: the argument principle.

3.1 Mapping one point

To find whether the closed loop has poles in the right half-plane without solving 1+L(s)=0, we feed complex numbers into L and look at what comes out.

A complex number a+bj is the point a across and b up, with j2=−1; its length is its distance from 0 and its angle is measured from the positive real axis. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles:

(1+j)(1+j)=1+2j+j2=2j lengths: 2​×2​=2 angles: 45∘+45∘=90∘

Dividing divides the lengths and subtracts the angles.

Putting one point into L and getting one point out is called mapping the point; the output lives in its own plane, the w-plane. Take s=−1+j in the heading loop’s L(s)=(0.8s+4)/s2, with no delay:

s2=(−1+j)2=1−2j+j2 =−2j 0.8s+4=−0.8+0.8j+4 =3.2+0.8j L(s)=−2j3.2+0.8j​=−2j×j(3.2+0.8j)j​ =23.2j−0.8​ =−0.4+1.6j

The same answer comes from arrows (Figure 4). Written with its zero and its poles:

L(s)=s20.8s+4​=0.8s2s+5​

L has a zero at −5 and a double pole at 0. Draw an arrow from the zero to s and from the pole to s. The length of L(s) is 0.8 times the zero’s arrow divided by the pole’s arrow twice; its angle is the zero’s angle minus the pole’s angle twice (FBS §10.2):

length=0.8×4.123/1.4142 =1.649 angle=14.0∘−2×135.0∘ =−256.0∘

An angle of −256.0° points the same way as 104.0°, the angle of −0.4 + 1.6j, whose length is 1.649. One point maps to one point; a closed path of points maps to a closed curve.

One point gives one number, but the opening question needs to know whether any closed-loop pole lies anywhere in the right half-plane. A walk round a closed path tells what lies inside the path. The reason lies in the arrows of Section 3.1. The angle of 1+L(s) is the sum of the angles of the arrows from its zeros minus those from its poles. While s walks once clockwise round a zero, the arrow from that zero to s turns once clockwise, −360°. An arrow from a zero or a pole outside the path swings back and forth and ends where it started. An arrow from a pole inside also turns −360°, but a pole’s angle is subtracted, so it turns the image +360°, counter-clockwise (FBS §10.2).

4. The Nyquist criterion

Key point

Draw L(s) as s walks up the imaginary axis and round the right half-plane: if this Nyquist plot turns clockwise round −1 N times and L has P poles in the right half-plane, the closed loop has N+P poles there. Our loop gives N=0 with 0.1 s of delay (stable) and N=2 with 0.25 s (two growing poles).

4.1 The Nyquist contour: a walk round the right half-plane

The closed loop is unstable if 1+L(s) has a zero anywhere in the right half-plane, because a polePole: A value of s where a transfer function’s denominator is zero, so the fraction becomes infinite there, such as −2 (twice) for the PD heading loop; each pole adds a term e^(pt) to every response, so its real part says whether the term decays or grows and its imaginary part how fast it oscillates. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §4. BIBO stability: which poles keep the output bounded there makes the response grow. So the walk of Section 3.2 must go round the whole right half-plane. The walk goes up the imaginary axis from far below to far above and back down round a half circle so large that it holds every pole and zero on the right (FBS §10.2).

The walk must not pass through a pole of L, where L is infinite. Our L(s)=(0.8s+4)/s2 has its double pole at 0, on the imaginary axis, so the walk steps round it on a small half circle to its right. The double pole then lies outside, on the left of the walk.   • on the imaginary axis below 0, the frequencies are negative and the curve is the mirror image of the first part across the real axis (FBS §10.2);
  • on the large half circle, L shrinks to 0, since it falls like 0.8 divided by the length of s;
  • on the small half circle, L is close to 4 divided by s2, so its length is very large, and its angle, minus twice the angle of s, sweeps from +180° to −180°: one full clockwise turn, very far from the origin.

The last part goes off to infinity. To draw it, Figure 7 squeezes the plane: points within a distance 2 of the origin keep their places, farther points are pulled in, and infinity lands on a grey circle of radius 4. The squeeze slides points only along lines through the origin and leaves −1 and everything within 2 of the origin in place, so the curve never crosses −1 because of it and its turns round −1 do not change.

We count turns round −1, not round 0. The function whose zeros are the closed-loop poles is 1+L, and 1+L is the curve of L shifted 1 to the right. Its turns round 0 are the turns of L round −1 (FBS §10.2). Counting those turns is the last step.

4.3 Counting turns round the critical point

The count now decides Figure 1’s two delayed runs. Apply the argument principle to 1+L(s) on the Nyquist contour, with:

  • N, the number of clockwise turns of the Nyquist plot of L round the critical point −1;
  • P, the number of poles of L inside the contour, that is in the right half-plane;
  • Z, the number of zeros of 1+L in the right half-plane, which are the closed-loop poles there.

1+L has the same poles as L, because adding 1 does not change the denominator. So the argument principle gives, first for the 0.25 s loop, whose plot turns twice clockwise round −1 (N=2, Figure 7 below) and whose L has no poles in the right half-plane (P=0), then in symbols:

2=Z−0 Z=2+0=2 N=Z−P Z=N+P

The rule Z=N+P is the Nyquist stability criterion ()Nyquist stability criterion: The test Z=N+P: the closed loop has Z poles in the right half-plane, where N is the number of clockwise turns of the Nyquist plot round −1 and P the number of the loop transfer function’s poles there; stable when Z=0, delays and open-loop-unstable plants included. Explained in §4.3 Counting turns round the critical point.: the closed loop is stable when Z=0, that is exactly when the Nyquist plot turns round −1 counter-clockwise once for every pole of L in the right half-plane (FBS Theorem 10.3). Our L has no poles in the right half-plane, its double pole at 0 being outside the contour, so P=0 and the loop is stable exactly when N=0.

  • 0.1 s: it comes in from far left just below the real axis and passes below −1, half a turn counter-clockwise (−0.5); its mirror adds −0.5; the far circle adds +1. So N=0 and Z=0: stable.

5. When a Bode plot misleads: a balancing robot

Key point

The Bode-plot reading “unstable if the gain is above 1 where the phase is −180°” holds only when L has no poles in the right half-plane, like our heading loop. A balancing robot’s loop has one, and only the Nyquist count judges its stability correctly.

So far we could judge our loop from its Bode plot alone: where the gain is 1, is the phase above −180°? The reading treats each frequency as going round the loop on its own and asks whether it grows (FBS §10.1). The reading assumes the loop has nothing that grows by itself.

A two-wheeled robot that balances upright breaks that assumption: without control its tilt grows on its own, so its plant has a pole in the right half-plane, which our heading loop does not. We use the textbook’s version, with time measured in units that make the falling rate 1 (FBS Example 10.5). With:

  • Gb​(s), the tilt plant, with poles at +1 (falling) and −1;
  • Cb​(s), a PD controller;
  • Lb​(s), its loop transfer function;
Gb​(s)=s2−11​ Cb​(s)=s+2 Lb​(s)=s2−1s+2​

At the frequency 0, the Bode plot reads:

Lb​(0)=−12​=−2

A gain of 2 at a phase of −180°: the reading says unstable. The closed loop’s poles say otherwise:

1+Lb​(s)=0 s2−1+s+2=0 s2+s+1=0 s=−0.5±0.866j

Both are on the left: the robot balances. The Nyquist count agrees. In Figure 8, watch the plot start at −2 and go once round −1 counter-clockwise, so N=−1. Lb​ has one pole in the right half-plane, so P=1:

Z=N+P=−1+1=0

The plot starts at L(0) = −2 and goes once round −1 counter-clockwise: N, the clockwise turns, is −1. With P = 1 pole of L in the right half-plane, the closed loop has Z = N + P = 0 poles there: stable

The Bode reading is a short cut that works for loops like our heading loop, with no poles of L in the right half-plane and one crossing of the gain 1. When those conditions fail, the Nyquist criterion is the test (FBS §10.3). With stability settled, the opening question remains: how much delay or gain can our loop take?

6. Stability margins: how far from the critical point

Key point

The gain, phase and delay margins measure how far the Nyquist plot stays from −1 in extra gain, extra phase lag and extra delay, quantities we can change on the robot. Our delay-free loop has no gain limit, a phase margin of 22.6° and a delay margin of 0.19 s.

Figure 1’s turns settle with no delay and with 0.1 s, but the Nyquist count showed the 0.25 s loop past the edge. A stable loop is not enough: we want to know how far from instability it is (FBS §10.3), measured in quantities that change on a real robot, such as a heavier robot, which lowers the gain, or a slower computer, which adds delay. 6.1 Gain margin

The opening asked how much more gain the loop can take. Raising both controller gains by a factor k multiplies L by k and stretches the whole Nyquist plot away from 0 (FBS §10.3). The plot reaches −1 when the point where it crosses the negative real axis lands on −1. The crossing is where the phase of L, the loop transfer function of Section 2.1, is −180°; its frequency is the phase crossover frequency ()Phase crossover frequency ωpc​: The frequency at which the loop transfer function’s phase is −180°, where the Nyquist plot crosses the negative real axis; the gain margin is read there. Explained in §6.1 Gain margin., ωpc​. The factor that brings the crossing to −1 is the gain margin ()Gain margin: How many times the loop gain can grow before the closed loop becomes unstable, 1/∣L(jωpc​)∣ (often in dB); it says how far the Nyquist plot’s crossing of the negative real axis is from −1. Explained in §6.1 Gain margin. (FBS eq. 10.5). With:

  • ωpc​, the phase crossover frequency, in rad/s;
  • gm​, the gain margin, a pure number, or 20 times its base-10 logarithm in decibels;
gm​=∣L(jωpc​)∣1​

Without delay, the phase of our L is the angle of 4+0.8jω minus twice the angle of jω:

∠L(jω)=arctan(0.2ω)−180∘

The arctangent is above 0 for every positive frequency, so the phase never reaches −180°, the plot never crosses the negative real axis, and no gain increase makes the loop unstable. A check on the poles: with the gains times k, the closed loop’s equation is

s2+0.8ks+4k=0

and its two poles add up to, and multiply to:

sum of the poles=−0.8k product of the poles=4k

A positive product means two real poles of the same sign, or a complex pair; a negative sum then puts both in the left half-plane, for every k above 0.

With 0.1 s of delay, the delay’s angle grows with the frequency without end, so the phase, in radians, does reach −π:

∠L=arctan(0.2ω)−π−0.1ω

The notebook finds the crossing at 11.66 rad/s. The gain there, with the frequency squared 135.96:

∣L(j11.66)∣=135.9616+0.64×135.96​​ =135.96103.01​​=0.0747 gm​=1/0.0747=13.4 =22.5 dB

With 0.1 s of delay, the gains could grow 13.4 times before the loop oscillates; without delay, without limit. Gain is one direction away from −1; phase is the other.

6.2 Phase margin

The phase margin measures the other direction: how much extra phase lag the loop can take at the gain crossover frequency, where the loop transfer function L has length 1, before the point there turns onto −1. With:

  • ωgc​, the gain crossover frequency, where the gain of L is 1, in rad/s;
  • φm​, the phase margin ()Phase margin: How much extra phase lag the loop can take at its gain crossover frequency before the closed loop becomes unstable, 180°+∠L(jωgc​); typical designs keep 30° to 60°. Explained in §6.2 Phase margin., in degrees;
φm​=180∘+∠L(jωgc​)

(FBS eq. 10.6). For our loop without delay, the gain of L(jω) is the length of 4+0.8jω divided by ω2; setting it to 1, then squaring:

ω216+0.64ω2​​=1 16+0.64ω2=ω4 ω2=20.64+0.642+64​​ =20.64+8.026​=4.333 ωgc​=2.08 rad/s

In symbols:

Iω2Kp2​+Kd2​ω2​​=1 Kp2​+Kd2​ω2=I2ω4

The phase there:

∠L=arctan(0.2×2.08)−180∘ =22.6∘−180∘=−157.4∘ φm​=180∘−157.4∘=22.6∘

In symbols:

φm​=arctan(Kd​ωgc​/Kp​)

With 0.1 s of delay the gain is unchanged, so the crossover is still 2.08 rad/s, but the delay subtracts an angle:

2.08×0.1=0.208 rad=11.9∘ φm​=22.6∘−11.9∘=10.7∘

Typical designs keep the phase margin between 30° and 60° (FBS §10.3). Our 22.6° is below that range, in the same loop whose turn overshoots by 53 percent in RO-061. RO-060’s tuned gains, Kp​ = 0.2 and Kd​ = 0.283, cross over at 2.91 rad/s with a phase margin of 76.4° (notebook). A delay is a phase lag that grows with the frequency, which turns the phase margin into a time.

6.3 Delay margin

A delay takes phase without changing the gain, so the delay that turns the point at ωgc​ onto −1 is the one whose phase loss there equals the phase margin. The delay that does so is the delay margin ()Delay margin: The smallest extra time delay that makes the closed loop unstable, the phase margin in radians divided by the gain crossover frequency (0.19 s for the robot’s heading loop); it says how late sensor readings or commands may arrive. Explained in §6.3 Delay margin.: the smallest extra delay that makes the loop unstable (FBS §10.3). With:

  • Tm​, the delay margin, in s;
  • φm​, the phase margin in radians, 22.6° = 0.3945 rad;
  • ωgc​, the gain crossover frequency, 2.0815 rad/s;

the delay’s phase loss at the crossover must equal the margin, our numbers first:

2.0815×Tm​=0.3945 Tm​=0.3945/2.0815=0.19 s ωgc​Tm​=φm​ Tm​=φm​/ωgc​

7. Where margins and delay show up in real stacks

Key point

Real flight and driving stacks treat delay as a direct loss of margin: PX4 trades filtering against delay so that the rate loop’s gains can be raised, and Autoware predicts ahead by its 0.17 s actuator delay instead of losing that phase.

PX4, the drone autopilot. PX4’s multicopter tuning guide calls the time from a disturbance to the motors’ reaction the control latency, and says that “lowering latency allows you to increase the rate P gains” (PX4 filter tuning). Its table gives the delay of the gyro’s low-pass filter: about 8 ms at a 30 Hz cutoff, 3.8 ms at 60 Hz and 1.9 ms at 120 Hz. The delay margin says why: a delay Td​ costs phase ω times Td​ at the crossover, and raising the gains moves the crossover up, so the same delay costs more. On our heading loop, crossing over at 2.08 rad/s, an 8 ms filter would cost only 0.95° of the 22.6° (notebook); a loop crossing over ten times higher would lose ten times as much.

Autoware, the self-driving stack. Autoware’s PID longitudinal controller drives a car’s speed with a PID controller ()PID controller: A controller whose command is the sum of a proportional, an integral and a derivative part of the error, K_p e + K_i (integral of e) + K_d de/dt; the commonest feedback controller, it reacts to the error now, its past and its trend; with K_d = 0 it is a PI controller, with K_i = 0 a PD controller. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Adding up the error, PD control plus a term that adds up the past error. Its README says that gas and brake actuators “typically” have “a delay of about a hundred millisecond”, and its parameter file sets delay_compensation_time: 0.17 s (Autoware PID controller). Rather than accept the phase lost to this delay, the controller predicts the car’s speed 0.17 s ahead and feeds the prediction back. For our heading loop, a 0.17 s delay would use 90 percent of its 0.19 s delay margin; for RO-060’s tuned loop it would use 37 percent of 0.458 s and leave a phase margin of 48.0° (notebook).
