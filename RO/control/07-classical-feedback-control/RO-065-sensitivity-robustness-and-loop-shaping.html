1. Overview

Key point

How close the heading loop comes to the critical point −1 is one number, the peak of the sensitivity function, and the same function says how much of each disturbance the loop removes. Reshaping the controller with a lead and a lag compensator moves the loop away from −1, removes more of a steady drag and stops the derivative from feeding sensor noise to the motor.

2. How near the loop passes to the critical point: the sensitivity function

Key point

The distance from −1 to the Nyquist curve at each frequency is the size of 1 + L. Its reciprocal is the gain of the sensitivity function S, and the peak of that gain, Ms, is one over the closest distance: 2.55 for the heading loop.

2.1 Margins look in two directions only

The second problem of the opening, how close the loop is to −1, needs a number that the margins do not give. The Nyquist curve is the frequency response of the loop cut open, the controller times the robot, drawn as points in the plane of complex numbers, and −1 is the critical point ()Critical point: The point −1 of the complex plane: wherever the loop transfer function equals −1 the closed loop has a pole, so the Nyquist criterion counts turns round it and the margins measure the distance to it. Explained in: The Nyquist Criterion and Stability Margins, §2.2 Where the closed loop has its poles, where a sine sent round the loop comes back the same size and inverted, so it can keep itself going. A gain margin asks how far the curve can be stretched before it hits −1; a phase margin asks how far it can be turned (FBS §10.3). A real change, such as the payload of Figure 1, stretches and turns the curve at once, so the curve can reach −1 by a route that neither margin measures (FBS §10.3, Example 10.8).

The heading loop shows the gap. Its gain margin is infinite: no gain increase alone can make the loop unstable. Yet the curve passes 0.392 from −1 (Figure 2), and a model error that moves the curve by more than that, near the closest frequency, can reach −1. A single number that covers every direction is the shortest distance from −1 to the curve.

2.2 The distance from the critical point and its reciprocal

In plain words: at each frequency the loop’s response is a point on the Nyquist curve, and we measure how far that point is from −1. 3. Disturbances: the sensitivity function from the block diagram

Key point

A disturbance that moves the heading directly reaches the measured heading multiplied by S. Where the gain of S is below 1 the loop shrinks the disturbance; where it is above 1, near 2.1 rad/s for the heading loop, the loop makes it larger.

3.1 A disturbance at the output passes through S

Section 2 found S as a distance on the Nyquist plot; the first problem of the opening, the drag, asks what the loop does to a push from outside. Figure 3 draws the heading loop with the three outside signals a robot meets (FBS §12.1):

  • a load disturbance ()Disturbance: An unwanted outside effect that changes what the plant does, such as a ramp pulling a robot back with 0.785 N; it changes the true output, unlike sensor noise, which changes only the reading. Explained in: Feedback Basics: Open and Closed Loop and the Block Diagram, §4.1 The parts of the loop, named on the robot v, an unwanted outside effect that changes what the robot does, here a torque that enters beside the motor’s torque, such as the 0.02 N·m drag;
  • an output disturbance d, anything that moves the heading itself, such as a bump that knocks the robot round;
  • sensor noise ()Sensor noise: Small random errors in a sensor’s readings, so the same quantity measured twice gives slightly different numbers (a wall 2 m away reads 1.92 to 2.07 m); it is one reason a robot cannot take a reading at face value. Explained in: Why a Robot Is Never Sure: State, Controls and Measurements, §2.2 Five sources of uncertainty n, an error in the heading reading that does not move the robot at all.

A disturbance acts on the robot; noise only spoils the reading, which is why the two enter the block diagram at different places. Each outside signal takes its own path through the loop, and this section follows d.

3.2 Disturbance attenuation: which frequencies shrink

Section 3.1 showed that a disturbance reaches the heading times S. The bottom right panel of Figure 2 therefore says, frequency by frequency, what the loop does to the bumps:

  • below 1.47 rad/s, ∣S∣ is below 1: a slow wobble is shrunk, to 0.066 of its size at 0.5 rad/s;
  • above 1.47 rad/s, ∣S∣ is above 1: a wobble near 2.085 rad/s comes out 2.55 times larger than it went in;
  • far above, ∣S∣ falls back towards 1: the loop cannot react, and a fast wobble passes almost unchanged.

Shrinking disturbances is disturbance attenuation ()Disturbance attenuation: The shrinking of disturbances by feedback, which happens at the frequencies where the gain of the sensitivity function is below 1; near the crossover feedback amplifies disturbances instead. Feedback is not free: the same loop that shrinks slow pushes amplifies those near its crossover, and the higher Ms, the larger the amplification. Disturbances are half of what reaches the loop; the other half is the noise in the reading, which takes a different path.

4. Sensor noise: the complementary sensitivity function

Key point

Noise in the heading reading reaches the heading through T, the loop L divided by one plus L. Because S + T = 1 at every frequency, the loop cannot make both small at the same frequency: it rejects disturbances where L is large and noise where L is small.

4.1 Noise passes through T

The third problem of the opening is the noise. Noise n does not move the robot, but the controller cannot tell it from a real heading error, so it turns the robot to “correct” it (Figure 3). We follow n the way Section 3.1 followed d, with the disturbance and the command at zero, at the same 0.5 rad/s, where L is −16 − 1.6j:

The same steps in symbols:

The transfer function T just found, the loop divided by one plus the loop, is the complementary sensitivity function ()Complementary sensitivity function T: The transfer function T=L/(1+L) of a feedback loop: it carries sensor noise (and, with error feedback, the command) to the output; since S+T=1, a loop cannot make both small at the same frequency. At high frequency L is small, so T is small and fast noise barely moves the heading. For a controller that acts only on the error e, as the lead-lag of Section 7 does, the same algebra shows that T also carries the command r to the heading (FBS §12.2).

The sum is of complex numbers, not of gains: near the crossover S and T point in nearly opposite directions, so both gains can exceed 1 there. Figure 4 draws both gains for the heading loop. Read it from left to right:

  • at low frequencies ∣S∣ is small and ∣T∣ is 1 (0 dB, the decibel value of a gain of 1): disturbances shrink, noise and commands pass;
  • near 2 rad/s both peak, Ms​ = 2.55 and the largest gain of T, Mt​ = 2.73;
  • at high frequencies ∣T∣ falls and ∣S∣ is 1: noise is ignored, disturbances pass.

Because S + T = 1, a loop cannot shrink disturbances and ignore noise at the same frequency; the designer chooses where each happens (FBS §12.1). Disturbances are usually slow and noise fast, so the usual choice is S small at low frequencies and T small at high ones. S and T cover the heading; the drag enters at the torque and the noise also reaches the motor, so two more paths are needed.

5. The Gang of Four: the drag and the noise at the motor

Key point

Four transfer functions, S, T, PS and CS, describe how the outside signals reach the heading and the motor. PS sets the drag’s offset, 1/Kp = 2.5 rad per N·m, and CS shows the derivative feeding fast noise to the motor, 8.0 N·m per rad at 100 rad/s and rising.

The first and third problems of the opening, the drag and the noise at the motor, are two paths that S and T do not cover. The drag v enters with the motor torque (Figure 3), and the noise n also moves the motor, which wears the actuator and can drive it into its limit (FBS §12.2); PX4’s guide adds hot motors and shorter flights (PX4 filter tuning). 6. Robustness to model error

Key point

If the real plant differs from the model by a relative error δ, the loop stays stable as long as |δ| is smaller than 1/|T| at every frequency. The real robot’s payload and delay break that test for the PD loop between 1.6 and 2.4 rad/s, where its Nyquist curve passes close to −1.

6.2 The test: the model error against one over T

A disk that stays clear of −1 at every frequency leaves the number of encirclements ()Encirclement (winding number): One full turn of a closed curve round a point; the winding number is the net count, clockwise minus counter-clockwise, which the Nyquist criterion reads off the Nyquist plot round −1. ∣L∣=1+0.16​=1.077 radius=1.077×0.524=0.564 distance=∣1+L∣=0.400

A loop that passes this test at every frequency, for every model error of the expected size, has robust stability ()Robust stability: The property of a feedback loop of staying stable for every model error of an expected size; it holds when the relative model error ∣δ∣ stays below 1/∣T∣ at every frequency, so no possible real loop reaches −1. Explained in §6.2 The test: the model error against one over T. (FBS §13.2). Here the warning is right, since the real PD loop has a phase margin, the extra lag it could take at its crossover, of −5.5°, and its swing grows (Figure 1, notebook). The test says where robustness is needed: where T is large, near the crossover. All four problems now point at the same place, the shape of the loop, and the next section reshapes it.

7. Loop shaping: lead, lag and lead-lag

Key point

Loop shaping chooses the controller so that |L| is high at low frequencies, crosses 1 gently and falls at high frequencies. A lead compensator adds phase at the crossover without unlimited high-frequency gain; a lag compensator adds gain at low frequencies; together, as a lead-lag, they fix all four problems of the heading loop.

7.1 The shape we want

Sections 3 to 6 showed what each frequency band of the loop must do for the robot. Because S, T, PS and CS are all built from L (and C), the bands become a wanted shape for the gain of L (FBS §12.3):

  • low frequencies: ∣L∣ large, so S is small and disturbances shrink; for the drag the controller’s own gain at low frequency must be large, since the offset is the drag divided by that gain (Section 5);
  • the crossover: ∣L∣ passes through 1 gently, near −20 dB per decade ()Decade: A factor of 10 in frequency, such as 0.25 to 2.5 rad/s; on a log axis every decade has the same width, and Bode-plot slopes are given per decade (−20 dB per decade for one pole). Explained in: Frequency Response and Bode Plots, §4.1 Decibels and a log rate axis, a factor of 10 in frequency, because a steeper fall comes with a smaller phase margin, less room for extra lag (FBS §12.3, eq. 12.6);
  • high frequencies: ∣L∣ small and falling, so T and the noise gain CS are small; a controller whose gain falls at high frequencies has high-frequency roll-off ()High-frequency roll-off: A controller’s gain falling (or at least no longer rising) at high frequencies, so that sensor noise is not passed on to the actuator; a lead’s pole or a filter on the derivative provides it. Explained in §7.1 The shape we want. (FBS §12.3).

Choosing the controller to give the loop this shape is loop shaping ()Loop shaping: Designing a controller by choosing the shape of the loop’s gain curve: high gain at low frequencies to reject disturbances, a gentle crossing of 0 dB for phase margin, and low falling gain at high frequencies to ignore noise. Explained in §7.1 The shape we want. (FBS §12.3). Figure 7 lays the PD loop over the three bands. Look at the crossover: the PD loop falls at 37 dB per decade there (phase margin 22.6°); and at high frequencies it falls only 20 dB per decade, because the derivative keeps raising the controller’s gain.

7.3 The lead compensator: a derivative with a ceiling

With the pole above the zero, a<b, the compensator is a lead compensator ()Lead compensator: A controller k(s+a)/(s+b) with its zero below its pole (a<b): it adds a bump of phase, largest at ab​, to raise the phase margin at the crossover, while its gain stops rising above the pole; a PD controller with a filtered derivative. 8. The limit: the waterbed effect

Key point

For a loop with no unstable poles whose gain falls at least as fast as 1/ω², the area under ln|S| plotted on a linear frequency axis is zero. Pushing |S| down at some frequencies pushes it up at others; no controller removes every disturbance at every frequency.

Section 7.4 pushed ∣S∣ down at low frequencies with the lag and saw the peak rise from 1.299 to 1.352. The rise is not an accident of our design. A theorem of Bode states, for a loop with no poles in the right half-plane whose gain falls at least as fast as 1/ω² (FBS §14.2, Theorem 14.3):

  • ln is the natural logarithm, so ln∣S∣ is negative where disturbances shrink and positive where they grow;
  • ω runs over all frequencies from 0 to infinity, on a linear scale.
∫0∞​ln∣S(jω)∣dω=0

The area where the loop shrinks disturbances equals the area where it amplifies them. Pushing ∣S∣ down in one band lifts it in another, like pressing a waterbed: the waterbed effect ()Waterbed effect (Bode’s integral formula): The rule that, for a loop with no unstable poles whose gain falls at least as fast as 1/ω2, the area under ln∣S∣ on a linear frequency axis is zero; pushing disturbances down at some frequencies pushes them up at others. Explained in §8. The limit: the waterbed effect. (FBS §14.2). Figure 13 draws ln∣S∣ for the lead and the lead-lag on a linear axis.

On a linear axis the low-frequency band is narrow, so the lag’s tenfold drop in the drag’s offset costs only a small rise in the peak. A loop with unstable poles pays more: the total area is then π times the sum of those poles, a positive number (FBS §14.2). The PD loop on paper escapes the theorem only because its controller’s gain rises forever, which is the noise problem of Section 5; a practical derivative is filtered (FBS §12.2), as PX4’s is (Section 9), and then the loop’s gain falls fast enough for the theorem to hold. The waterbed bounds what loop shaping can do; the next section shows where real robots meet these trades.

9. Where loop shaping runs in real stacks

Key point

Apollo’s control module wraps its speed and position controllers in lead-lag compensators; PX4 caps its derivative with filters and cuts a resonance with a notch; Autoware turns the derivative off and filters the speed error, because of noise.

The trades of this Note appear in the code of open robot and vehicle stacks:

  • Apollo (Baidu’s self-driving stack) has a lead-lag block, leadlag_controller.cc, in its control module. The block builds a compensator from three parameters, alpha, beta and tau: a gain beta, a zero at one over tau and a pole at one over alpha times tau, and turns it into a difference equation with Tustin’s method ()Tustin’s method (bilinear transform): A discretisation rule that adds up each strip of an integral as a trapezoid joining its two samples, which amounts to replacing s by (2/T)(z−1)/(z+1); it is the most accurate of the three simple rules and sends every stable continuous pole inside the unit circle. Explained in: Digital Control: Sampling a Continuous Controller, §5.2 The three rules in z at the control period, 0.01 s in the longitudinal controller’s controller_conf.pb.txt. Its lon_controller.cc passes the output of the station (position) PID and of the speed PID through a lead-lag when enable_reverse_leadlag_compensation is on. The test file leadlag_speed_conf.pb.txt sets alpha: 0.35, beta: 1.0, tau: 0.1: a lead with its zero at 10 rad/s and its pole at 28.6 rad/s, giving 28.8° of phase at 16.9 rad/s (notebook). The shipped configuration leaves the block switched off, with alpha and beta 1 and tau 0, which passes signals unchanged (Apollo control).
  • PX4 (drone autopilot) filters the rate controller’s derivative separately (imu_gyro_parameters.yaml): IMU_DGYRO_CUTOFF, 20 Hz by default, is a low-pass filter on the derivative term, “the most susceptible to noise”, which lets the main gyro filter IMU_GYRO_CUTOFF (40 Hz) be set higher for less delay. The capped derivative is the lead’s idea of Section 7.3. Its notch filter IMU_GYRO_NF0_FRQ removes a narrow band “to avoid feedback amplification of structural resonances”; a notch filter ()Notch filter: A filter that blocks a narrow band around one frequency and passes the rest, (s2+ω02​)/(s2+(ω0​/Q)s+ω02​); it removes a known vibration, such as a frame’s 10 Hz bending seen by the gyro, with far less lag at the loop’s own frequencies than a low-pass. Explained in: PID Tuning in Practice, §6.2 The notch filter lowers Ms near one frequency without cutting the low-frequency gain. PX4’s filter-tuning guide states the trade of Section 4: a higher gyro cutoff lowers the delay and allows higher P gains, but sends more noise to the motors (PX4 filter tuning).
  • Autoware (self-driving stack) sets the derivative gain of its longitudinal PID to zero, kd: 0.0 with max_d_effort: 0.0, in autoware_pid_longitudinal_controller.param.yaml; its README warns that “unintended noise may cause the output of the derivative term to be very large”. The controller low-pass filters the speed error instead, lpf_vel_error_gain: 0.9: each new error counts 10 percent and the last filtered value 90 percent (lowpass_filter.hpp) (Autoware PID controller).
