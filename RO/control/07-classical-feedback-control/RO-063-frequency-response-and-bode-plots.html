1. Overview

Key point

A planner seldom asks for one turn; to weave between obstacles it swings the heading command back and forth. A linear loop answers a sine with a sine at the same rate, only taller or shorter and later, so two numbers per weave rate, the gain and the phase, say how well the robot follows; the Bode plot draws them for every rate at once.

The robot’s heading loop already turns the robot on command. Its PD controller ()PD control: Feedback with a proportional part and a derivative part, such as K_p e + K_d de/dt; it acts like a spring and a damper, so it can bring a frictionless joint or heading to its target without swinging. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §4.1 Look at how fast the error shrinks pushes with a turning torque in proportion to the heading error and brakes in proportion to the turn rate. RO-060 tuned it to Kp = 0.2 N·m/rad and Kd = 0.283 N·m·s/rad, so that a turn just does not pass its target, and adds integral action ()Integral action: A part of the command in proportion to the error’s running sum (its integral over time); the sum grows while any error remains, so it removes the error a steady load leaves, at the cost of overshoot and, if too large, instability. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §5.2 Add the error up, Ki = 0.05, only against a dragging gearbox. The loop’s transfer function ()Transfer function (control): The number a linear system multiplies an exponential input e^(st) by, written as a function of s, such as G(s) = 1/(8s + 2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s² for its heading; it turns a differential equation into a fraction, so systems can be combined and read by algebra. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §2.1 An exponential goes in, the same exponential comes out holds its behaviour in one formula.

Overview instead: Kp = 0.4 N·m/rad and Kd = 0.08 N·m·s/rad, with no integral action. Its step response ()Step response: The output of a system after a step input, such as the robot’s heading after its command jumps to 0.5 rad; its rise time, overshoot, settling time and steady-state error describe how well a loop follows a command. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §2.1 Reading the four numbers swings 53 percent past a 0.5 rad turn, to 0.763 rad. The same loop, the same 0.1 rad command, and three different answers. The opening question of this Note: for a heading command that swings as a wave, how far does the robot turn and how late, at each weave rate, and which rates should the planner avoid? We answer it in steps:

  • why the heading is again a sine at the same rate, and the two numbers that describe it, the gain and the phase (Section 2);
  • how the transfer function gives both numbers for any rate without a simulation (Section 3);
  • the Bode plot, which draws them for every rate (Section 4);
  • straight lines that sketch the plot by hand (Section 5);
  • resonance, the reason for the over-turn at 1.92 rad/s (Section 6);
  • bandwidth, the fastest weave the loop still follows (Section 7);
  • where real autopilots measure and use these numbers (Section 8).

2. A sine in gives a sine out

Key point

A loop built only from fixed multipliers, rates of change, running totals and sums turns a sine into a sine of the same rate. Only two things can differ: how far the output swings, the gain, and how late it is, the phase.

2.1 Why only the size and the timing change

In Figure 1 the heading never swings in some new shape: at each weave rate it is again a smooth wave at the command’s own rate. Why should a weave command give a weave at all, and why at the same rate?

The loop is built from four simple operations, and each keeps a sine a sine of the same rate (FBS §6.3):

  1. Multiplying by a fixed number, such as the gain Kp = 0.4: the wave gets taller or shorter.
  2. Taking the rate of change, such as the turn rate the derivative action brakes with: the rate of change of a sine is the same wave a quarter period earlier.
  3. Taking the running total, such as the heading, which is the running total of the turn rate: the running total of a sine swings as the same wave a quarter period later.
  4. Adding two signals, such as the two torques of the PD controller: two waves of the same rate add up to one wave of that rate.

Figure 2 shows all four on the speed loop’s PI controllerExplained in: PD and PID Control, §5.3 Too much integral: a drive force of 20 N per m/s times the speed error (its proportional gain), plus 10 N per m times the error’s running total (its integral gain). We pick it because one controller then shows a multiplier, a running total and a sum at once. We feed it a speed error e, in m/s, that swings 0.1 m/s at the rate 0.5 rad/s, with t the time in s:

e=0.1sin(0.5t)

Watch the last stage: the purple output is again a smooth wave of the same rate, taller and later.

Figure 2: The speed loop’s PI controller, force = 20 N per m/s × speed error + 10 N per m × running total of the error, fed the speed error e = 0.1 sin(0.5 t) m/s (grey, dashed, right axis). Stage 1: the error. Stage 2: the proportional part 20e = 2 sin(0.5 t) N (blue), crests at the same times as the error. Stage 3: the integral part, whose swing is −2 cos(0.5 t) N (orange), crests a quarter period (3.14 s) later. Stage 4: the sum (purple), 2.83 sin(0.5 t − 45°) N: a wave of the same rate, its crests 1.57 s after the error’s

The sum, step by step. The proportional part is 20 times the error:

20×0.1sin(0.5t)=2sin(0.5t)

The running total of the error rises and falls about a fixed level; the fixed level is not a wave and we leave it out. Its swing, in m, is the wave below, whose rate of change is the error again:

−0.2cos(0.5t)

Times the integral gain, 10 N per m:

10×(−0.2cos(0.5t))=−2cos(0.5t)

The two parts, both in N, add up to one wave:

2sin(0.5t)−2cos(0.5t) =2.83sin(0.5t−45∘)

Why it holds. Any sum of a sine and a cosine of the same rate is one sine of that rate (Control System Lectures, Bode plots). With:

  • a, the size of the sine part, here 2;
  • b, the size of the cosine part, here −2;
  • x, the angle inside both, here 0.5 t;
  • φ, the shift, the angle of the point (a, b) from the positive x-axis, here −45° for the point (2, −2).
asinx+bcosx =a2+b2​sin(x+φ)

With our numbers the size is:

22+(−2)2​=8​=2.83

A check at an easy value. At the time 0, the left side:

2×0−2×1=−2

The right side:

2.83×sin(−45∘) =2.83×(−0.707)=−2.0

The two sides agree. A shift of 45° is one eighth of a period, and one period of a 0.5 rad/s wave lasts 12.57 s, so the output’s crests come later by:

12.57 s/8=1.57 s

The same four operations make up the heading loop, so a weave command must give a weave heading at the command’s rate. One condition: the loop must stay linear. The torque must stay inside its limit of 0.2 N·m, because a clipped torque is no longer a fixed multiple of the error. In Figure 1 the largest torque is 0.094 N·m, at 1.92 rad/s (notebook). The sine in, sine out rule tells us the shape of the answer, but not its size or its delay, which the next section measures.

That swing shrinks because the loop is stable: its poles ()Pole: A value of s where a transfer function’s denominator is zero, so the fraction blows up there, such as −2 (twice) for the PD heading loop; each pole adds a term e^(pt) to every reply, so its real part says fade or grow and its imaginary part how fast it swings. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §3.1 Poles: where the fraction blows up sit at −0.4 ± 1.96j, and the real part −0.4 sets how fast the swing’s size shrinks. What is left, the settled part or steady state, depends only on the command. So the two numbers below describe the loop, not the way it started (FBS §6.3).

The two numbers. With:

  • ω, the weave rate, 1 rad/s here;
  • Tw​, one period of the weave, 6.283 s (2π divided by the rate);

M=A/Ar​ =0.1288/0.1=1.288

φ=−360∘×Δt/Tw​ =−360∘×0.2606/6.283 =−14.9∘

Measuring each rate with a simulation, as here, takes 30 s of simulated driving per rate. The transfer function gives the same two numbers with one line of algebra.

3. Gain and phase from the transfer function

Key point

Replacing the transfer function’s variable s by jω, where ω is the weave rate and j squares to −1, gives one complex number for each weave rate: its length is the gain and its angle is the phase.

3.1 Putting the weave rate into the transfer function

To answer the opening question at every weave rate, we cannot simulate every rate. The loop’s equation gives the gain and phase directly.

Two recaps.

  • Complex numbers. The number j squares to −1. A complex number such as 3 + 4j is an arrow on a plane, 3 across and 4 up; its length is 5, by Pythagoras, and its angle from the positive real axis is 53.1°:
32+42​=5
  • A spinning arrow. By Euler’s formula, the arrow below has length 1 and turns at ω radians per second, so its upward part is a sine of rate ω:
ejωt=cosωt+jsinωt

Its rate of change is jω times itself. So for this arrow, taking a rate of change becomes multiplying by jω. Multiplying by jω is the transfer function’sTransfer function (control): The number a linear system multiplies an exponential input e^(st) by, written as a function of s, such as G(s) = 1/(8s + 2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s² for its heading; it turns a differential equation into a fraction, so systems can be combined and read by algebra. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §2.1 An exponential goes in, the same exponential comes out own idea, an exponential est in and the same exponential out, with s=jω.

The loop’s equation. The yaw inertia I = 0.1 kg·m² turns under the PD torque, Kp times the heading error minus Kd times the turn rate (the derivative acts on the measured turn rate, as in RO-061). With:

  • θ, the heading, in rad, and θ′, θ′′, its rate of change and that rate’s rate of change;
  • θr​, the heading command, in rad;
  • I, the yaw inertia, 0.1 kg·m²;
  • Kp​, the proportional gain, 0.4 N·m/rad;
  • Kd​, the derivative gain, 0.08 N·m·s/rad.
Iθ′′=Kp​(θr​−θ)−Kd​θ′ 0.1θ′′+0.08θ′+0.4θ=0.4θr​

The derivation. We feed the loop the spinning arrow ejt, a weave at 1 rad/s, and guess that the heading is the same arrow times some complex number Y. Our numbers first:

θr​=ejt θ=Yejt θ′=jYejt θ′′=−Yejt (−0.1+0.08j+0.4)Yejt =0.4ejt (0.3+0.08j)Y=0.4 Y=0.4/(0.3+0.08j)

The same steps for any weave rate ω:

θr​=ejωt θ=Yejωt θ′=jωYejωt θ′′=−ω2Yejωt (−Iω2+jωKd​+Kp​)Yejωt =Kp​ejωt (−Iω2+jωKd​+Kp​)Y=Kp​ Y=Kp​−Iω2+jωKd​Kp​​ =G(jω)

The last line is the loop’s transfer function with s replaced by jω:

G(s)=Is2+Kd​s+Kp​Kp​​

The real command, a sine of 0.1 rad, is the upward part of the spinning arrow times 0.1. So the heading is the upward part of that arrow times 0.1 and times Y: a sine whose size is 0.1 times the length of Y and whose shift is the angle of Y. For ω = 1 rad/s:

∣Y∣=0.4/0.32+0.082​ =0.4/0.3105=1.288 ∠Y=−arctan(0.08/0.3)=−14.9∘

These are the gain and the phase that Figure 3 measured. So the frequency response, the gain and phase at each weave rate, is the transfer function at s = jω: the gain is the length of G(jω) and the phase is its angle (FBS §9.6). One complex number per rate replaces 30 s of simulation, but one number at a time does not yet show how the answer changes with the rate.

4. The Bode plot

Key point

The Bode plot draws the gain in decibels and the phase in degrees against the weave rate on a log scale; decibels turn the products of a loop’s pieces into sums, and the log scale gives slow and fast rates the same room.

4.1 Decibels and a log rate axis

To read the robot’s answer at any weave rate, we want the gain and the phase as two curves against the rate. Two problems appear on plain axes. The heading loop’s gain runs from 2.55 at 1.92 rad/s down to 0.01 at 20 rad/s, so on a plain scale every gain below 0.1 is squeezed onto the bottom line. And the rates of interest run from 0.1 to 20 rad/s, so on a plain scale everything below 1 rad/s is squeezed into the left edge.

Decibels. We write a gain in decibels (dB), 20 times its base-10 logarithm, the unit used for sensor signals. With M, the gain (the output’s swing divided by the input’s), a number without a unit:

gain in dB=20log10​M

Some values:

  • a gain of 10 is 20 dB, and a gain of 0.1 is −20 dB;
  • a gain of 1, output as large as input, is 0 dB;
  • a gain of 0.707 is −3.01 dB;
  • the resonant gain 2.552 is 8.14 dB.

The factor is 20, not 10, because a decibel compares powers, and a wave’s power grows with the square of its size (Control System Lectures, Bode plots). Decibels also turn products into sums: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms. A loop made of pieces multiplied together, such as a controller times a plant, has a gain in dB that is the sum of the pieces’ gains in dB, and a phase that is the sum of their phases (FBS §9.6). Section 5 builds on that.

A log rate axis. On the rate axis we give each factor of 10 the same width: 0.1 to 1 rad/s takes as much room as 1 to 10 rad/s. A factor of 10 in rate is a decade ()Decade: A factor of 10 in frequency, such as 0.25 to 2.5 rad/s; on a log axis every decade has the same width, and Bode-plot slopes are given per decade (−20 dB per decade for one pole). Explained in §4.1 Decibels and a log rate axis.. With gains in decibels and rates on a log axis, the robot’s answers at all rates fit on one page.

5. Sketching a Bode plot from straight lines

Key point

Far below and far above each corner, a loop’s gain in dB lies on straight lines, flat or falling 20 dB per decade for each pole; the lines meet at the corner frequency, where the real curve departs from them most.

5.1 One pole: the speed plant

To see why the heading loop’s gain is flat and then falls, we start with the simplest piece on the robot: the speed plant, which has a single pole. The drive force pushes the 8 kg robot against a rolling drag of 2 N·s/m. Its transfer function splits into two pieces, a fixed number and a lag. With:

  • P(s), the speed plant’s transfer function, from drive force (N) to speed (m/s);
  • s, the transfer function’sTransfer function (control): The number a linear system multiplies an exponential input e^(st) by, written as a function of s, such as G(s) = 1/(8s + 2) for the robot’s speed and 10/s² for its heading; it turns a differential equation into a fraction, so systems can be combined and read by algebra. Explained in: Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros, §2.1 An exponential goes in, the same exponential comes out variable: for an exponential input est, taking a rate of change multiplies by s.
P(s)=8s+21​ =0.5×4s+11​

Here 0.5 m/s per newton is the speed one newton holds against the drag (the fixed piece, which we call K), and 4 s is the plant’s time constant ()Time constant: The time a first-order system takes to close 63 % of the gap to its final value, m/(K_v + b) = 1 s for the robot’s speed loop; it fixes the rise time (about 2.2 time constants) and the 2 % settling time (about 4). Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant, the time it takes to close 63 percent of a gap: here the mass divided by the drag. Why the gain is flat, then falls. With:

  • ω, the rate of the force wave, in rad/s;
  • T, the time constant, 4 s (the symbol RO-061 uses).

The lag piece at s = jω is:

1+jωT1​=1+4jω1​

Two easy cases:

  • Slow waves, ω much smaller than 0.25 rad/s: the term 4jω is tiny next to 1, so the piece is close to 1, which is 0 dB with phase 0°. The robot’s speed keeps up with a slow push.
  • Fast waves, ω much larger than 0.25 rad/s: the 1 is tiny next to 4jω, so the piece is close to the line below. Ten times the rate gives a tenth of the gain, which is −20 dB per decade, and dividing by j is a phase of −90°. The 8 kg mass cannot change speed fast enough to follow a fast push.
1+4jω1​≈4jω1​

The phase line. The phase is close to 0° up to a tenth of the corner, 0.025 rad/s (exact −5.7°), and close to −90° from ten times the corner, 2.5 rad/s (exact −84.3°). In between, a straight line falling 45° per decade passes −45° at the corner (FBS §9.6). One pole gives one bend; the heading loop has two.

6. Resonance: the weave that over-turns the robot

Key point

A lightly damped loop has a weave rate at which each push lands in step with the loop’s own swing; there the output swings far more than the input. For our loop the peak is 2.55 times the command at 1.918 rad/s, close to the 2.5 that one over twice the damping ratio ζ = 0.2 gives at the natural frequency; with ζ of 0.707 or more there is no peak.

At 1.92 rad/s the robot over-turns: it swings 2.55 times as far as the command asks. Why there, and why so much?

The idea in plain words. A child on a swing goes highest when each push comes in step with the swing. The heading loop is such a swing: the proportional torque pulls the heading back towards the command like a spring, the yaw inertia keeps it moving past, and the loop’s own swing has the rate ωn​ = 2 rad/s. A weave command near that rate pushes each swing a little further. Only the derivative action, which brakes with the turn rate, stops the swing from growing without end. A large output at the rate of a system’s own swing is resonance ()Resonance: A large output when a lightly damped system is driven near the rate of its own swing, because each push lands in step with the swing; it makes the heading loop over-turn a weave near 1.92 rad/s. Explained in §6. Resonance: the weave that over-turns the robot. (FBS §6.3).

A check at the natural frequency. At ω = ωn​ = 2 rad/s the two real parts of the denominator cancel, our numbers first:

G(2j)=4−4+0.8×2j4​ =1.6j4​=2.5∠−90∘

The same in symbols, using the form of Section 5.2:

G(jωn​)=2ζωn2​jωn2​​ =2ζ1​∠−90∘

With ζ = 0.2:

1/(2×0.2)=2.5

At its natural frequency the loop multiplies the command by 2.5 and is a quarter period late (FBS §9.6). Only the damping term is left in the denominator, so the smaller the damping ratio, the taller the swing.

Three things to read from Figure 7:

  • The peak and the overshoot have one cause. The small damping ratio that lets the 0.5 rad turn overshoot by 53 percent in RO-061 is the one that lets a weave at 1.92 rad/s over-turn by a factor of 2.55.
  • RO-060’s tuned loop has no peak. Its gains, Kp = 0.2 and Kd = 0.283, give a damping ratio of 1 (at a natural frequency of 1.41 rad/s), and its gain never rises above 0 dB: the derivative chosen so a turn just does not pass its targetExplained in: PD and PID Control, §4.2 How much derivative leaves no weave rate amplified either.
  • ζ = 0.707 is not critical damping. It is the smallest damping ratio with no resonant peak, the one whose gain at ωn​, 1/(2ζ), is 0.707, that is −3 dB. Critical damping, the smallest damping with no overshoot in a step response, is ζ = 1, the critically damped ()Critically damped: A second-order system with damping ratio exactly 1: its characteristic equation has one double real root, so it gives the fastest step response that does not overshoot, such as the turn with K_p = K_d = 0.4 settling in 2.92 s. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.2 Two roots decide the kind of reply case. Between the two, a step still overshoots a little but no weave rate is amplified.

So the planner should keep the heading command’s weave rates away from 1.9 rad/s, a weave period near 3.3 s, while the loop runs with ζ = 0.2. Resonance explains the over-turn; the third answer of Figure 1, barely turning at 5 rad/s, needs a limit on the other side.

7. Bandwidth: how fast a weave the loop follows

Key point

The bandwidth is the rate at which the gain has fallen to 0.707 of its zero-frequency value, −3 dB. Our heading loop’s bandwidth is 3.02 rad/s; weaves faster than that get less than 71 percent of their size and come more than 150° late.

At 5 rad/s the robot barely turns. How fast a weave can the planner ask for before the robot stops following, that is before the gain, the heading’s swing divided by the command’s, drops away?

The idea. Above some rate, the robot’s inertia wins and the swing it manages shrinks fast (Section 5.2: 40 dB less for every decade). A single number marks where following gives way: the rate at which the gain has fallen to 0.707 (one over the square root of 2) of its value for very slow commands, the zero-frequency gain. That rate is the bandwidth ()Bandwidth (of a system): The rate at which a system’s gain has fallen to 0.707 of its zero-frequency gain (−3 dB), such as 3.02 rad/s for the first-try heading loop (Kp 0.4, Kd 0.08); it marks the fastest input the system still follows. Explained in §7. Bandwidth: how fast a weave the loop follows. of the loop (FBS §6.3). The value 0.707 is a convention with a reason: a wave’s power grows with the square of its size (Section 4.1), and 0.707 squared is 0.5, so at the bandwidth the heading’s swing carries half the power it carries for a slow weave. In decibels, 0.707 is 3.01 dB below the zero-frequency gain, so the bandwidth is often called the −3 dB point.

Figure 8 marks the bandwidth on two gain curves. Look where each curve crosses the red dashed line.

Figure 8: The gain in decibels of the heading loop with the first-try gains (Kp 0.4, Kd 0.08, ζ = 0.2, blue) and with RO-060’s tuned gains (Kp 0.2, Kd 0.283, ζ = 1, grey), rate on a log scale. Both start at 0 dB for slow weaves. The red dashed line is −3.01 dB, 0.707 of the zero-frequency gain; the blue curve crosses it at its bandwidth 3.02 rad/s, after its resonant peak, and the grey curve at 0.91 rad/s

What the numbers mean for the robot:

  • Our loop: 3.02 rad/s, a weave period of 2.08 s. Faster weaves, such as the 5 rad/s of Figure 1, get less than 71 percent of their size; at the bandwidth itself the heading is already 154.7° late (notebook).
  • Below the bandwidth is not the same as faithful. Between 1 and 3 rad/s our loop’s gain lies above 1: it over-turns there, as Section 6 showed. The resonant peak is a separate specification next to the bandwidth (FBS §12.2).
  • RO-060’s tuned loop has a bandwidth of 0.91 rad/s. Removing the overshoot, and halving Kp, costs speed: the tuned loop follows only slower weaves, but faithfully, with no rate over-turned.
  • The speed plant’s bandwidth is its corner frequency, 0.25 rad/s, because Section 5.1 showed the gain there is 0.707 of its zero-frequency value: force waves much faster than one every 25 s barely change the robot’s speed.

A rule of thumb for design is a bandwidth 5 to 10 times the fastest rate the loop must follow (MATLAB, Using Bode plots 1). With 3.02 rad/s, our heading loop follows weaves up to about 0.3 to 0.6 rad/s well, which is what Figure 1 showed at 0.5 rad/s: 1.06 times the command and 6° late. Bandwidth closes the opening question; the next section shows where real autopilots measure frequency responses and guard against resonance.

8. Where frequency responses are measured and used

Key point

Autopilots measure frequency responses with sine sweeps, filter sensor signals above the rates their loops need, and cut out structural resonances with notch filters.

The weave of Figure 5, a sine at rising rates, is how real vehicles are measured. Three examples from the PX4 autopilot, an open-source flight stack for drones; we read its parameter files on GitHub:

  • Sine sweeps. PX4’s fixed-wing auto-tuner shakes each axis of the aircraft with a sine whose rate sweeps from 1 Hz to 10 Hz over 10 s, a linear sweep by default (parameters FW_AT_SYSID_F0, FW_AT_SYSID_F1, FW_AT_SYSID_TIME and FW_AT_SYSID_TYPE in src/modules/fw_autotune_attitude_control/fw_autotune_attitude_control_params.yaml). One hertz is one cycle per second, 6.28 rad/s, so the sweep covers 6.28 to 62.8 rad/s. From the measured responses the tuner fits a model of the axis and computes new gains (fw_autotune_attitude_control.cpp).
  • Bandwidth of a filter. The gyroscope signal that feeds PX4’s turn-rate controller first passes a second-order low-pass filter whose cutoff, the −3 dB rate, is 40 Hz by default (IMU_GYRO_CUTOFF in src/modules/sensors/vehicle_angular_velocity/imu_gyro_parameters.yaml). Slow turns pass almost unchanged; vibration from the motors, faster than 40 Hz, is cut.
  • Resonance. The same file offers notch filters (IMU_GYRO_NF0_FRQ, IMU_GYRO_NF0_BW), which cut a narrow band of rates “to avoid feedback amplification of structural resonances”: a frame that bends at its own rate has a peak like the one in Section 6, and the loop must not push it.

The frequency response has told us how a stable loop answers each weave. The closed loop’s frequency response G(jω) cannot tell whether a loop is stable at all, for instance when the heading reaches the controller late; the Nyquist criterionExplained in: The Nyquist Criterion and Stability Margins, §4.3 Counting turns round the critical point answers that from the frequency response of the loop cut open, the controller times the robot.

  • A sine stays a sine of the same rate, because the loop only multiplies, takes rates of change and running totals, and adds, as long as the torque stays inside its 0.2 N·m limit.
  • The gain and the phase are the length and the angle of G(jω), because a spinning arrow passes through the loop’s equation multiplied by that complex number.
  • The Bode plot uses decibels and a log rate axis, so a loop’s pieces add and every rate from 0.1 to 20 rad/s gets room.
  • A pole’s asymptotes are flat and then fall 20 dB per decade from its corner, where the exact curve is 3.01 dB lower and the phase is −45°, so a sketch takes a few straight lines.
  • The heading loop resonates because its proportional torque acts like a spring against the yaw inertia; the peak, 2.552 at 1.918 rad/s, grows as the damping ratio falls, so the same small ζ that made the 0.5 rad turn overshoot by 53 percent makes weaves near 1.92 rad/s over-turn.
