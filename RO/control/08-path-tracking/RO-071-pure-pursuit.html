2. Chasing a point on the path

Key point

The line rule steers from where the car is; pure pursuit steers towards where the car should be a little later: the point on the path a fixed distance ahead of the rear axle, the look-ahead point. At the start, with a 4 m look-ahead, that point is (3.873, 0).

A driver does not steer by the paint under the car. A driver looks some way down the road and heads for that spot, and the spot moves on as the car moves (Coulter §1.2). Pure pursuit does the same: the car chases a point on the path some distance ahead, like a donkey chasing a carrot on a stick, and the name comes from that chase (Coulter §1.2; UW CSE 478 L11).

Two things are assumed known (Coulter §3.1):

  • the path, as a list of points with their positions, such as our course sampled every few millimetres;
  • the car’s pose, from its localisation.

The point to chase is found with a circle. Draw a circle around the rear axle; where it meets the path ahead, the car aims. The circle’s radius is the look-ahead distance ()Look-ahead distance (pure pursuit): The distance ld​ from the rear axle to the point pure pursuit chases, such as 4 m; a short one brings the car back sooner but weaves when the steering lags, a long one is smooth but cuts corners. Explained in §2. Chasing a point on the path., written ld​, and the point is the look-ahead point ()Look-ahead point (pure pursuit): The point of the path one look-ahead distance from the rear axle, found by walking forward from the nearest path point, such as (3.873, 0) for a car 1 m off a straight line with a 4 m look-ahead; pure pursuit steers along the arc that reaches it. Also called the goal point or carrot. Explained in §2. Chasing a point on the path., also called the goal point or carrot (Coulter §2.0; Nav2 RPP README). The circle can meet the path more than once, so the search starts at the path point nearest the car and walks forward along the path until the distance from the rear axle reaches ld​ (Coulter §3.3).

If the car is farther from the path than ld​, the circle misses the path and the rule has no point to chase (Paden §V.A.1); implementations then aim at the nearest path point, as ours does.

The car now knows where to head. But a car cannot turn on the spot to face the point; it can only drive forward along arcs. The next question is which arc reaches the point.

3. The arc through the look-ahead point

Key point

With the steering held still, the car drives a circle, so in one command it can only drive an arc. The arc that leaves the car along its heading and passes through the look-ahead point has a curvature of twice the point’s sideways distance y over the look-ahead distance ld​ squared: −0.125 per m at the start, a circle of 8 m to the right, which the car drives with the steering at −17.4°.

3.1 Why an arc, and which one

The look-ahead point is 3.873 m ahead of the rear axle and 1 m to its right. In the kinematic bicycle model ()Kinematic bicycle model: A car drawn as one steered front wheel and one rear wheel on its centre line, with no tyre slip: the rear axle moves along the heading and the heading turns at θ˙=vtanδ/L; it predicts a car’s motion well while the sideways acceleration stays below about half the road’s friction (grip) coefficient times gravity. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.2 The bicycle model’s equations, a car whose steering is held at one angle drives round one circle (Snider §2.1). So between two steering commands the car drives an arc, and pure pursuit picks the arc that ends at the look-ahead point.

Many arcs join the rear axle and the point. Only one of them can be driven: at this instant the rear axle moves straight along the heading, because the rear wheel cannot slide sideways. So the arc must leave the rear axle along the heading. The centre of an arc that starts along the heading lies square to it, on the line through the rear axle. The rear-axle line is where the turning car’s instantaneous centre of curvature ()Instantaneous centre of curvature (ICC): The point a turning robot’s whole body circles at a given moment; for a differential drive it lies on the axle line at distance R=v/ω from the midpoint, so it fixes how fast each wheel must move. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.2 Why the robot turns about a point on its axle line, the point the whole car turns about, has to be, for the same reason as for the differential drive (Snider §2.2; Coulter §2.0).

We measure the look-ahead point from the car itself: from the rear axle, x forward and y to the left, the car’s own frame. A point at world position (xw​,yw​), seen from a car at (xr​,yr​) with heading θ:

  • x is the point’s distance ahead of the rear axle, in m;
  • y is its distance to the left of the heading line, in m (negative: to the right).
x=cosθ(xw​−xr​)+sinθ(yw​−yr​) y=−sinθ(xw​−xr​)+cosθ(yw​−yr​)

At θ=0, the cosine is 1 and the sine is 0, so x and y are just the gaps along the world axes, as they should be. At the start the point is at x=3.873 m and y=−1 m.

The same curvature can be written with the angle α between the heading and the line to the look-ahead point (Figure 3). With:

  • α, the angle from the heading to the look-ahead point, positive to the left: −14.5° at the start.

The point’s sideways distance is the look-ahead distance times the sine of that angle:

The angle form is the textbook one (Snider §2.2, eq. 2; Paden §V.A.1, eq. V.2). Two checks at easy values:

  • The point straight ahead, α=0: the curvature is 0, and the car drives straight, as it should.
  • The point square to the left, α=90∘: the curvature is 2 over ld​, a circle whose radius is half of ld​, so its diameter is the line from the rear axle to the point.

3.3 From curvature to steering angle

The car does not take a curvature; it takes a steering angle. In the bicycle model the rear axle circles with radius R when the front wheel is turned by the angle whose tangent is the wheelbase over R (Snider §2.1, eq. 1), so the tangent of the steering angle ()Steering angle δ: The angle between a car’s steered front wheel and its heading, positive to the left, such as at most 26.6° for RO-055’s car; it sets how sharply the car turns, through tanδ=L/R. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.1 Steering angle and wheelbase is the wheelbase times the curvature. With:

  • L, the wheelbase, 2.5 m;
  • κ, the curvature of the arc, −0.125 per m;
  • δ, the steering angle of the front wheel, positive to the left, at most 26.6° either way.
tanδ=2.5×(−0.125) =−0.3125 δ=−17.4∘ tanδ=Lκ δ=tan−1(Lκ)

Put together, the steering from the angle to the look-ahead point is (Snider §2.2):

δ=tan−1(ld​2Lsinα​)

The car steers 17.4° to the right, within its 26.6°. The steering answers one moment. But after one tick, 0.05 s later, the car has moved 0.1 m and the look-ahead point is somewhere else, so the arc has to be found again.

4. Pure pursuit as a loop

Key point

Pure pursuit repeats the same steps at every tick: find the look-ahead point, compute the arc, set the steering, drive. From 1.0 m off, the 4 m look-ahead brings the car within 0.1 m after 7.4 m without crossing the line, because the look-ahead point’s sideways distance contains the heading error as well as the lateral error: near a straight path, a spring and a damper together, with a damping ratio of 0.707 for any look-ahead distance.

4.1 Find the point, steer, drive, repeat

The arc of Section 3 is right only for the instant it was computed. So the rule runs as a loop, 20 times a second (Coulter §3.3):

  1. Read the car’s pose.
  2. Find the path point nearest the rear axle.
  3. Walk forward along the path to the look-ahead point, the path point one look-ahead distance from the rear axle.
  4. Turn the point into the car’s frame (Section 3.1).
  5. Compute the curvature and set the steering (Sections 3.2 and 3.3); a steering angle beyond the car’s 26.6° is clipped to 26.6°, and our runs drive with the clipped value.
  6. Hold that steering until the next tick, 0.05 s later (a zero-order hold ())Zero-order hold: Keeping each command constant from its tick until the next one, so the actuator gets a staircase; simple and cheap, but the held value is on average half a sample period old, so it acts like a delay of T/2. Explained in: Digital Control: Sampling a Continuous Controller, §3.1 Holding each command until the next one, and go back to step 1.

The loop is pure pursuit ()Pure pursuit: A path tracker that, at every tick, finds the point on the path one look-ahead distance from the rear axle and steers along the circle that leaves the car along its heading and passes through that point, of curvature 2y/ld2​; looking ahead brings the heading error into the steering, so a car whose steering answers at once returns to the path with little swing past it. Explained in §4.1 Find the point, steer, drive, repeat. (Coulter 1992; Paden §V.A.1). As the car turns towards the look-ahead point, the point moves on along the path, so the steering eases off and the car slides onto the path.

The run answers the opening problem on the arc: by its middle the car is 0.03 m off, where the line rule stayed 0.36 m outside. Once the look-ahead point is on the arc, the arc that leaves the rear axle along the heading and reaches the point is the course’s own arc: on a circle, any look-ahead distance shorter than its diameter gives exactly the circle’s curvature (Coulter §4.2). From 1 m into our arc, look-aheads of 2, 4 and 8 m all command 0.100 per m (notebook). So pure pursuit turns with the arc without needing an error; its errors come where the curvature changes, at the arc’s two ends.

Like the line rule, pure pursuit also comes back from 1.0 m off without swinging past the line, though it has one setting instead of the line rule’s two gains. Why it does not swing is the next question.

Pure pursuit’s damping is fixed, but its look-ahead distance still sets how far the car drives before it settles, and what happens where the path bends. The next section varies it.

5. Choosing the look-ahead distance

Key point

On the chapter’s car a short look-ahead brings the car back sooner but asks for sharp steering, and a long one cuts corners: 8 m cuts 0.85 m inside the arc. Once we give the car a real steering motor’s lag of 0.4 s, the delay that makes real cars weave, at 5 m/s a 2 m look-ahead weaves 1.25 m off the line, while 8 m still cuts 0.56 m inside: the look-ahead trades weaving against cutting corners.

  • A short look-ahead returns sooner. The natural frequency ωn​ of Section 4.2 is 2​ divided by ld​, so it doubles when ld​ halves, and the car settles in about half the distance.
  • A short look-ahead asks for sharp steering. From 1.0 m off, the 2 m look-ahead puts the look-ahead point 1 m to the right of the heading line at only 2 m, which asks for more than the car can steer (worked below), so the wheels sit at full lock, 26.6°.

  • Inside a long arc the error dies away, whatever the look-ahead, because the arc through the look-ahead point is the course’s own arc (Section 4.1). The long look-ahead’s errors come from where the curvature changes.

Speed changes nothing here. At 5 m/s the three cars drive almost the same paths: within 0.1 m after 3.9, 7.2 and 23.8 m (notebook). On this car the shortest look-ahead looks best, apart from its sharp steering. Real cars weave with a short look-ahead (Coulter §4.1; Snider §2.2.1), because their wheels do not turn at once: pure pursuit assumes the car takes each curvature the moment it is asked, and a real steering motor answers with a lag (Coulter §4.3).

5.2 A real steering motor: the short look-ahead weaves

The chapter’s car turns its wheels the instant it is told; a real steering motor needs time. The team of Stanford’s car, Stanley, modelled its steering as a first-order system ()First-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with only a first derivative, such as the robot’s speed under P control; it closes the same fraction of its remaining gap in every time constant and can never overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant with a time constant ()Time constant: The time a first-order system takes to close 63 % of the gap to its final value, m/(K_v + b) = 1 s for the robot’s speed loop; it fixes the rise time (about 2.2 time constants) and the 2 % settling time (about 4). Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant of 0.4 s, a cautious value from their experiments (Hoffmann et al. 2007, §II.B, eq. 4). From here to Section 7 we change the chapter’s car: it gets the same steering lag. Without the lag, nothing in the simulation would show why the look-ahead must change with speed. With:

  • δc​, the steering angle commanded, in degrees;
  • δ, the angle the wheels are really at, in degrees;
  • δ˙, how fast the wheels turn, in degrees per second;
  • τ, the time constant, 0.4 s.
δ˙=(δc​−δ)/τ

The wheels close 63 percent of the gap in each 0.4 s. At the start, with the 4 m look-ahead, the command jumps to −17.4°:

0.632×(−17.4)=−11.0∘

so 0.4 s later the wheels are at −11.0°, not −17.4°. At 5 m/s the car drives 2 m in those 0.4 s, half of a 4 m look-ahead and the whole of a 2 m one. 6. A look-ahead that grows with speed

Key point

Anything that takes time, such as the 0.4 s lag of the steering motor added in Section 5.2, becomes distance at speed: 0.8 m at 2 m/s, 2 m at 5 m/s. So the look-ahead is set to the speed times a time, here 2 s, which gives 4 m at 2 m/s and 10 m at 5 m/s, kept between limits: at 5 m/s the car stops weaving, but the 10 m look-ahead cuts the arc.

We keep the 0.4 s steering lag of Section 5.2, added to the chapter’s car because real steering has one. With it, at 2 m/s the 4 m look-ahead distance, how far ahead the chased point lies, crosses the line by 0.037 m; at 5 m/s, by 0.26 m. In 0.4 s the car drives 0.8 m at 2 m/s but 2 m at 5 m/s, so the lag eats into the look-ahead more and more as the car speeds up. Paden et al. (§V.A.1) give the same reason from the turn rate. With:

  • v, the car’s speed, in m/s;
  • ω, the rate at which the car’s heading turns, in rad/s: the speed times the curvature of Section 3.2;
  • α, the angle from the heading to the look-ahead point, as in Section 3.2.
ω=vκ =ld​2vsinα​

At a fixed look-ahead, the same angle gives a turn rate that grows with the speed, and “a common fix for this issue is to scale L with the vehicle speed” (Paden §V.A.1).

The fix is a look-ahead distance proportional to the speed, kept between a smallest and a largest value: a velocity-scaled look-ahead ()Velocity-scaled look-ahead (adaptive pure pursuit): A look-ahead distance set to the speed times a look-ahead time and kept between limits, such as 2 s giving 4 m at 2 m/s and 10 m at 5 m/s; it keeps pure pursuit from weaving at speed, where the same steering delay covers more distance. Explained in §6. A look-ahead that grows with speed., whose pure pursuit Macenski et al. (§2) call adaptive pure pursuit. With:

  • v, the car’s speed, in m/s, as above;
  • tla​, the look-ahead time, 2 s, our choice so that the base speed keeps the base 4 m;
  • ld​, the look-ahead distance, kept between 2 and 10 m.
ld​=vtla​ 2×2=4 m at 2 m/s 5×2=10 m at 5 m/s

Put into the turn rate, the speed cancels:

ω=vtla​2vsinα​ =tla​2sinα​

So the same angle gives the same turn rate at every speed, and the car turns as briskly in time at 5 m/s as at 2 m/s.

Real trackers use the same idea with their own numbers (Figure 8):

  • Snider (§2.2.1): the look-ahead is a gain times the speed, kept between 3 and 25 m.
  • Nav2: lookahead_time 1.5 s, kept between 0.3 and 0.9 m, for robots that drive at most 0.5 m/s (parameter defaults in parameter_handler.cpp).
  • Autoware: 2.4 s times the speed, minus 120 m² times the path’s curvature, plus 3.6 times the lateral error when that is 0.5 m or more, kept between 4.35 and 15 m (calcLookaheadDistance, pure_pursuit.param.yaml). At 5 m/s that is 12 m on a straight but 4.35 m on our 10 m arc, where the curvature takes 12 m off.

Figure 8: Look-ahead distance against speed. Blue: ours, the speed times 2 s, kept between 2 and 10 m: 4 m at 2 m/s and 10 m at 5 m/s. Orange: Autoware’s pure pursuit, 2.4 s times the speed kept between 4.35 and 15 m, on a straight (solid) and on our 10 m arc (dashed), where its curvature term takes 12 m off

At 5 m/s with the steering lag, the 10 m look-ahead no longer swings past the line. But it returns slowly, within 0.1 m only after 22.6 m, and it cuts the arc: 0.98 m inside at the entry, where it is still coming back from the start, 0.60 m in the middle of the arc, and 0.76 m wide after it (notebook). Autoware’s curvature term shows the way out: shorten the look-ahead where the path bends. Slowing down there does the same, and does more.

7. Regulated pure pursuit: slow down where the path is tight

Key point

Regulated pure pursuit, the version in Nav2, lowers the speed in proportion to the radius of the arc it commands whenever that arc is tighter than a set radius, here 25 m (so 2 m/s on our 10 m arc), and also near obstacles; with a look-ahead that grows with speed, slowing also shortens the look-ahead. In the middle of the arc the car is 0.27 m off instead of 0.60 m, and 0.14 m instead of 0.76 m after it, for 11.4 s on the course instead of 7.0 s.

At a constant 5 m/s the 10 m look-ahead cuts the arc by 0.60 m in its middle; this section keeps the 0.4 s steering lag added in Section 5.2, so that speed costs accuracy as it does on a real car. At a constant 2 m/s, with the same steering lag, the 4 m look-ahead is 0.00 m off in the middle of the arc and 0.10 m after it, but the course takes 17.9 s. A driver does neither: a driver slows for the bend and speeds up on the straight. Macenski et al. (§3) build that habit into pure pursuit.

Regulated pure pursuit ()Regulated pure pursuit: Pure pursuit with rules that lower the speed when the commanded arc is tighter than a set radius, to vmax​R/rmin​, and when an obstacle is near, as in Nav2; with a velocity-scaled look-ahead, slowing also shortens the look-ahead, so the car cuts tight turns less. Explained in §7. Regulated pure pursuit: slow down where the path is tight. adds two rules to pure pursuit that lower the speed (Macenski et al. 2023, §3):

  • the curvature rule: when the arc pure pursuit commands is tighter than a set radius, the speed is cut in proportion to its radius;
  • the proximity rule: when an obstacle is closer than a set distance, the speed is cut in proportion to that distance.

The curvature rule as Nav2 codes it (curvatureConstraint in regulation_functions.hpp), with:

  • vmax​, the speed asked for, 5 m/s;
  • R, the radius of the arc pure pursuit commands, one over the size of its curvature, in m (always positive here, unlike the signed R of Section 3.2);
  • rmin​, the radius below which the rule acts, 25 m: our choice, so that an arc as tight as our 10 m one is driven at the base speed, 2 m/s;
  • v, the speed the rule allows, never below 0.25 m/s (Nav2’s regulated_linear_scaling_min_speed).

When R is below rmin​:

v=vmax​R/rmin​ 5×10/25=2 m/s on a 10 m arc 5×5/25=1 m/s on a 5 m arc

Figure 9 draws the rule. The proximity rule has the same shape, with the distance to the nearest obstacle in place of R: with Nav2’s defaults (cost_scaling_dist 0.6 m, cost_scaling_gain 1.0), an obstacle 0.3 m away halves the speed, from 0.5 to 0.25 m/s. Our Python model codes only the curvature rule, in Nav2’s form; our course has no obstacles, so the proximity rule would not act. Our car also changes speed by at most 2.5 m/s², Nav2’s default max_linear_accel.

Figure 9: Regulated pure pursuit’s curvature rule with a requested speed of 5 m/s and a set radius of 25 m: below 25 m the speed limit falls in proportion to the radius of the commanded arc, to 2 m/s for our 10 m arc and 1 m/s for the car’s tightest 5 m circle, but never below 0.25 m/s

Slowing helps twice. A slower car has less to lose to the steering lag, and its velocity-scaled look-ahead, the speed times 2 s, shrinks, so it cuts less. 8. Where pure pursuit runs: Nav2 and Autoware

Key point

Nav2’s Regulated Pure Pursuit controller and Autoware’s pure pursuit lateral controller both find a look-ahead point, the path point a set distance ahead, and command the curvature of the arc through it; both can make the look-ahead grow with speed, and Nav2 adds the slowing rules of Section 7.

Two open-source robot stacks run the rules of Sections 2 to 7, which our runs only modelled in Python. Each fact below comes from the stack’s code or parameter files; their paths are in Sources.

Nav2, the ROS 2 navigation stack: nav2_regulated_pure_pursuit_controller. The controller is one of Nav2’s plugins, the local planner ()Local planner: The planner that turns the next few metres or seconds of the global plan into a timed trajectory within the robot’s limits and around obstacles its sensors see, solving again many times a second, such as TEB at 10 Hz over 1.5 m; it handles what the global plan cannot. Explained in: Trajectory Optimisation: The Local Planner, §2.1 Levels of planning layer that turns the global plan into velocity commands (Nav2’s default configuration uses its MPPI controller instead). Each call:

  1. prunes the path to the point closest to the robot and turns the nearby part into the robot’s frame (Nav2 RPP README);
  2. finds the look-ahead point: lookahead_dist 0.6 m, or, with use_velocity_scaled_lookahead_dist, the speed times lookahead_time 1.5 s kept between 0.3 and 0.9 m (getLookAheadDistance);
  3. computes the curvature of Section 3.2, twice the point’s sideways distance over its squared distance from the robot (calculateCurvature);
  4. applies the curvature rule below regulated_linear_scaling_min_radius 0.9 m and the proximity rule within cost_scaling_dist 0.6 m, never below 0.25 m/s, and at most max_linear_vel 0.5 m/s (applyConstraints);
  5. sends a turn rate equal to the speed times the curvature, since most Nav2 robots are differential drives, which drive an arc by their linear and angular velocityAngular velocity ω: How fast a robot’s heading changes, in rad/s, positive to the left; for a differential drive, the difference of the wheel speeds divided by the wheel separation. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.1 Why we describe motion by linear and angular velocity;
  6. checks the arc it commands for collisions up to 1.0 s ahead (max_allowed_time_to_collision_up_to_carrot).

Autoware, the open-source self-driving stack: autoware_pure_pursuit. The package is one of the two lateral controllers of autoware_trajectory_follower_node; the node’s lateral_controller_mode picks MPC or pure pursuit. The package takes the planned trajectory and the car’s odometry and returns a steering angle (Autoware pure pursuit README). Its look-ahead is the rule of Section 6: it grows with speed (2.4 s), shrinks on curves (120 m² times the curvature), grows when the car is far off the path (3.6 times a lateral error of 0.5 m or more) and stays between 4.35 and 15 m, or at least 7.0 m when reversing (pure_pursuit.param.yaml).

  • The opening question has its answer: on the 10 m arc pure pursuit is 0.03 m off by the middle, where the line rule stays 0.36 m outside (up to 0.39 m), because once the look-ahead point is on the arc the arc through it is the course’s own arc, so the car turns with the bend without needing an error; from 1.0 m off it is back within 0.1 m after 7.4 m, against 7.70 m for the line rule.
  • The look-ahead point is where a circle of radius ld​ around the rear axle meets the path ahead, so the rule needs only the path’s points and the car’s pose.
  • The car can only drive arcs, and the drivable arc leaves along the heading, so its centre lies on the rear-axle line and one right triangle gives its curvature, twice the point’s sideways distance over the look-ahead distance squared, which the bicycle model turns into the steering angle.
  • Near the path, pure pursuit is a spring on the lateral error and a damper on the heading error, with the damping ratio 0.707 whatever the look-ahead, so on a car that steers at once it swings past the line by at most 4.3 percent of its start error, and a shorter look-ahead only makes it settle sooner (4.0 m for 2 m, 7.4 m for 4 m).
  • On a long arc any look-ahead gives the arc’s curvature, so the errors come where the curvature changes: a long look-ahead turns early and cuts corners (0.85 m inside for 8 m).
  • A steering motor that lags 0.4 s, which we added to the chapter’s car because real steering lags, makes a short look-ahead weave, more at speed, because the lag becomes 2 m of travel at 5 m/s, so the look-ahead must grow with speed: the speed times 2 s here, between 3 and 25 m in Snider’s car, 4.35 to 15 m in Autoware.
  • A long look-ahead at speed still cuts corners, so regulated pure pursuit slows down on arcs tighter than a set radius (25 m here) and near obstacles; slowing also shortens the velocity-scaled look-ahead, which cut the error in the middle of the arc from 0.60 to 0.27 m for 4.4 s more on the course.

The car can now follow its path from a small lateral error and choose its look-ahead and speed for the turns ahead. Still missing: pure pursuit never looks at the path’s direction or curvature at the car itself, so it cuts corners and its look-ahead must be retuned for every speed.
