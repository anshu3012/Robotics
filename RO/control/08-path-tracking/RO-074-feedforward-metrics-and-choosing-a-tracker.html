1. Overview

Key point

A curve needs a steering angle of its own, the arctangent of the wheelbase times the path’s curvature (14.04° on our 10 m arc), and sent before any error appears it removes the 0.38 m that steering from the errors alone keeps outside the arc. Measured with one ruler, the RMS and peak lateral error and the steering travel at the rear axle, the chapter’s four trackers rank differently at 2 m/s and at 5 m/s with a lagging steering motor, so the choice depends on the speed, the path and the steering.

2. Feedforward steering: the turn the curve needs

Key point

A steering rule that acts only on errors must keep an error to steer round a curve: 0.38 m outside our 10 m arc for the line rule. The curve’s own steering, the arctangent of the wheelbase times the path’s curvature, 14.04° here, can be sent before any error appears; added to the feedback, it leaves the car 0.001 m off at the arc’s middle.

2.1 Steering from the errors alone sits outside the arc

The first problem of the Overview is the line rule’s 0.384 m outside the arc. The line rule sets the car’s turn rate from its two errors:

  • ω is the turn rate the rule asks for, in rad/s;
  • e is the lateral error at the rear axle, in m, positive to the left;
  • θe​ is the heading error, the car’s heading minus the path’s, in rad;
  • KD​ is the lateral gain, 0.5 rad/s per m;
  • KH​ is the heading gain, 2 per s.
ω=−KD​e−KH​θe​

The kinematic bicycle model ()Kinematic bicycle model: A car drawn as one steered front wheel and one rear wheel on its centre line, with no tyre slip: the rear axle moves along the heading and the heading turns at θ˙=vtanδ/L; it predicts a car’s motion well while the sideways acceleration stays below about half the road’s friction (grip) coefficient times gravity. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.2 The bicycle model’s equations turns the turn rate into a steering angle ()Steering angle δ: The angle between a car’s steered front wheel and its heading, positive to the left, such as at most 26.6° for RO-055’s car; it sets how sharply the car turns, through tanδ=L/R. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.1 Steering angle and wheelbase, the angle of the front wheels from the car’s heading. With v the speed, 2 m/s, and L the wheelbase ()Wheelbase L: The distance from a car’s rear axle to its front axle, such as 2.5 m; with the steering angle it fixes the turning radius, R=L/tanδ, so a longer car turns more widely for the same steering. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.1 Steering angle and wheelbase, the distance between the axles, 2.5 m:

tanδ=Lω/v

On a straight path both errors can be zero and the rule asks for no turn. On the arc the car must turn, and the rule can only turn it through an error. To drive round the arc at 2 m/s the car must turn at its speed times the path curvature ()Path curvature κ: How fast a path’s direction turns per metre along it, 1/R on an arc of radius R, such as 0.1 per m on a 10 m arc and 0 on a straight; it fixes the steering a bend needs, tanδ=Lκ. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.3 Path curvature κ, 1 divided by the radius, 0.1 per m. Once settled the heading error is about zero, so the turn must come from the lateral error alone, as RO-070 found on the arcExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §5.3 Measuring a run: peak error, steady error and settling distance:

−0.5e=2×0.1 −KD​e=vκ e=−0.2/0.5=−0.4 m e=−vκ/KD​

The car settles about 0.4 m outside the arc, where its error is just large enough to supply the turn. The run reaches 0.384 m, a little less, because outside the arc the course turns under the car a little more slowly than 0.2 rad/s (see measuring a runExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §5.3 Measuring a run: peak error, steady error and settling distance).

RO-070’s run gives 0.388 m because it moves the car in straight 0.01 s steps, which drift slightly outward on a curve; this Note’s notebook moves the car along exact arcs, and gives 0.388 m too when it takes straight steps.

The 0.4 m is the steady-state error ()Steady-state error: The command minus the value a step response settles at, such as 0.125 m/s when a speed loop asked for 0.5 m/s settles at 0.375 m/s; a P loop keeps one whenever it must keep pushing against a load. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §2.1 Reading the four numbers of a proportional rule under a steady load, the same kind of lag as RO-066’s speed loopExplained in: Feedforward, Integral Action, Cascaded Loops and Windup, §2.1 Why feedback alone trails the plan, which had to fall behind before it pushed. The last line also says why speed makes it worse: at 5 m/s the arc needs 0.5 rad/s, and the same gain needs about 1.0 m of error to give it; the run swings out as far as 0.994 m. With feedback only, “the states will not all converge to zero while the vehicle is following a constant curvature path” (Snider §4.3). The missing piece is the turn the curve itself needs.

2.2 The steering the curve needs

How much steering does the 10 m arc need, with the car exactly on it? Figure 2 draws the car on the arc. The car turns about a point on its rear axle’s line, the centre of the arc; the rear axle is 10 m from it. The front wheel must roll square to the line from that centre, so the angle between the car’s axis and the front wheel equals the angle at the centre between the two lines.

Figure 2: The car on the 10 m arc, drawn 0.8 cm per metre. The rear axle is R = 10 m from the arc’s centre and moves square to that radius; the front wheel, L = 2.5 m ahead, points square to the line from the centre, so the angle δ at the centre equals the steering angle: tan δ = L / R = 0.25, δ = 14.04°

The symbols:

  • R is the arc’s radius at the rear axle, 10 m;
  • κ is the path curvature, 1 divided by R, 0.1 per m;
  • L is the wheelbase, 2.5 m;
  • δff​ is the steering the curve needs, in degrees.

The same number comes from the turn rate: the arc needs 0.2 rad/s at 2 m/s, and the bicycle model turns that into a steering angle:

tanδ=2.5×0.2/2=0.25 tanδ=Lvκ/v=Lκ

The speed cancels: the steering a curve needs depends only on the car and the curve, not on how fast the car drives, as long as the tyres do not slip sideways (the kinematic model is enough hereExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.4 Why the kinematic model is enough here). Section 2.3 adds this steering to the line rule.

2.3 Feedforward plus feedback

Now give the line rule the curve’s turn as well. With κ the curvature at the path point nearest the rear axle, 0 on the straights and 0.1 per m on the arc, and the other symbols as listed in Section 2.1 (speed v 2 m/s, gains KD​ 0.5 rad/s per m and KH​ 2 per s):

ω=vκ−KD​e−KH​θe​

On the arc with no error, the rule asks for 0.2 rad/s, which is the steering of Section 2.2, 14.04°. Figure 1 (purple) runs it from the same start: the error at the arc’s middle falls from −0.354 m to +0.001 m, and the run’s RMS lateral error from 0.351 m to 0.265 m (notebook). The feedback is unchanged; it now corrects only the start and what the curvature term does not foresee. The two parts share the work as in Kanayama’s trackerKanayama’s tracker: A trajectory tracker for a wheeled robot that sends the reference’s speed and turn rate plus feedback on the error in the robot’s frame, v=vr​cosθe​+Kx​xe​ and ω=ωr​+vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​); a Lyapunov function proves its error cannot run away while the reference drives forward. Explained in: Tracking a Timed Trajectory: Kanayama and Feedback Linearisation, §5.3 Why both parts are needed: feedforward drives the plan, feedback removes the error the plan leaves.

Feedforward has limits of its own. Feedforward steering needs the path’s curvature at every point, so the path must be smooth enough to have one (Boyali et al. 2018 compute it from the path or from the steering a driver used; Paden Table II). And it reacts only to the curvature where the car is, with no look ahead, so where the curvature jumps, at the arc’s ends, the steering jumps only once the car is there and the car overshoots a little (Snider §4.3.1: “the feed forward term can only be reactive”). The other trackers of the chapter carry a curvature term of their own.

3. Tracker metrics: one ruler for every tracker

Key point

One run is summed up by three numbers measured at the rear axle of every car: the RMS lateral error, the typical size of the error over the whole run; the peak lateral error, the worst moment; and the steering travel, how far the steering wheel is turned back and forth in total. Stanley’s front axle on the line puts its rear axle 0.318 m inside the arc, so the point of the car must be the same for all.

3.1 Three numbers for a run

The Overview’s second problem is that the four trackers were each measured their own way. RO-070 summed up a runExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §5.3 Measuring a run: peak error, steady error and settling distance by its largest error, its steady error on the arc and the distance it took to settle. Those three numbers suit one car on one course; to rank trackers, a review of lateral controllers measures the error over a whole run (it adds up the error’s size; we take the RMS, which counts large errors more), the worst error, and how much the steering moves (Artuñedo et al. 2024, §5.1.3 and §6). Figure 3 reads the three numbers this Note uses off one finished run, pure pursuit at 2 m/s starting on the line.

Figure 3: How the three metrics read one run: pure pursuit (4 m look-ahead) on the course at 2 m/s, starting on the line. Top: the lateral error at the rear axle; the dashed lines are at plus and minus the RMS lateral error, 0.086 m; the red dot is the peak, 0.167 m, just after the arc. Bottom: the steering; it rises to 14.7° into the arc and falls back out of it, and all its rises and falls add up to a steering travel of 30°

  • RMS lateral error ()RMS lateral error: The square root of the average squared lateral error over a run, such as 0.086 m for pure pursuit on the chapter’s course; it says how far off a tracker typically keeps the vehicle, with large errors counting more than small ones. Explained in §3.1 Three numbers for a run.: the square root of the average squared lateral error over the run, the root mean squared error ()Root mean squared error (RMSE): The square root of the average squared error (MSE), so the error is back in the output’s units. Explained in: Regression Metrics: MAE, MSE, RMSE, R² and Adjusted R², §4. Root mean squared error (RMSE) applied to the lateral error. The RMS says how far off the car typically is, in metres, and squaring makes large errors count more than small ones. For pure pursuit it is 0.086 m.
  • Peak lateral error ()Peak lateral error (maximum lateral error): The largest size of the lateral error during a run, such as 0.167 m for pure pursuit just after the chapter’s arc; it says how close the vehicle came to leaving its lane, which an average can hide. Explained in §3.1 Three numbers for a run.: the largest size of the lateral error during the run, the review’s “maximum lateral error” (Artuñedo et al. 2024, §6.1). The peak says how close the car came to leaving its lane: 0.167 m for pure pursuit, just after the arc.
  • Steering effort ()Steering effort (steering travel): How much a tracker works the steering, measured here as the steering travel, every change of the steering command added up, such as 30° for a turn into an arc and out; weaving adds every swing, so it shows a restless tracker that small errors can hide. Explained in §3.1 Three numbers for a run.: how much the steering works. We measure it as the steering travel, every change of the steering command added up, in degrees. Holding 14° round a bend costs only the turn in and out, while weaving adds every swing, so the travel grows with the swinging that the review measures as oscillation of the control action. Pure pursuit’s travel is 30°: up to 14.7° and back.

The RMS value, with the symbols:

  • ei​ is the lateral error at step i, one value every 0.01 s;
  • n is the number of steps in the run, 1783 for this run;
  • eRMS​ is the RMS lateral error, in m.
eRMS​=n1​i=1∑n​ei2​​

A check at easy values: an error that stays at 0.1 m all run has an RMS of 0.1 m. An error of 0.1 m for half the run and 0 for the other half has an average size of 0.05 m, and an RMS of:

(0.12+02)/2​=0.005​ =0.071 m

The RMS is more than the average size because squaring weights the bad half.

Each number answers a different question, so we quote all three: a car that holds 0.01 m but swings its steering through 200° is accurate but uncomfortable; one with a small RMS can still touch the kerb once. All three also depend on the point of the car they are measured at.

3.2 The same point of every car

Stanley measures its errors at the front axle, the other trackers at the rear axle. On a curve the two axles do not run on the same circle (Figure 4): with the front axle on the 10 m arc, the rear axle, which moves square to its own radius, is on a smaller circle.

Figure 4: The rear axle runs inside the front axle on a curve (car drawn larger than to scale). With the front axle (orange) on the 10 m course, the rear axle (blue), L = 2.5 m behind it, is 9.682 m from the arc’s centre: 0.318 m inside

The rear axle’s radius and the wheelbase are the two short sides of a right triangle whose long side is the front axle’s radius, as worked out for Stanley on the arcExplained in: The Stanley Controller and Rear-Wheel Feedback, §3.3 On the arc: the front axle stays on it, the rear axle cuts inside: with the front axle on the 10 m arc, the rear axle is 9.682 m from the centre. So a Stanley car whose front axle is exactly on the arc has its rear axle 0.318 m inside it, by geometry alone. Measured at its own front axle, Stanley’s run on the course at 2 m/s has an RMS of 0.003 m and a peak of 0.005 m; at the rear axle, 0.189 m and 0.316 m (notebook). Neither is wrong; they describe different points. We measure every tracker at the rear axle, the car’s position in the bicycle model and the point the other three hold on the path, so the numbers compare the same thing.

4. Comparing the trackers

Key point

On the chapter’s car at 2 m/s the two trackers with a curvature term stay within 0.007 m of the course, pure pursuit cuts the corners by up to 0.167 m and Stanley’s rear axle runs 0.316 m inside. At 5 m/s with steering that lags 0.4 s, the order changes: pure pursuit has the smallest RMS, 0.080 m, while rear-wheel feedback weaves, with 203° of steering travel.

4.2 Speed: 5 m/s and a steering motor that lags

On the chapter’s car the speed changes almost nothing: at 5 m/s every tracker’s RMS and peak are within 0.006 m of its numbers at 2 m/s (notebook). Without tyre slip or steering delay, pure pursuit, Stanley and rear-wheel feedback turn each metre of the course into the same path at any speed (see the look-ahead on the chapter’s carExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §5.1 Short or long, on the chapter’s car and why rear-wheel feedback convergesExplained in: The Stanley Controller and Rear-Wheel Feedback, §5.3 Why it converges: a spring and a damper over distance). The line rule alone is the exception: its steady error, the speed times the curvature over the gain, grows from 0.4 m to 1.0 m.

A real steering motor takes time. In 0.4 s the car drives 0.8 m at 2 m/s but 2 m at 5 m/s, so at speed the wheels answer the trackers late. Figure 6 races the four trackers again at 5 m/s with that lag.

The order has changed. The two trackers that were best at 2 m/s act hardest on the errors at the rear axle, and with the wheels answering late they overshoot and swing back: rear-wheel feedback turns its steering through 203°, almost seven times the 29° it needs at 5 m/s without the lag. Pure pursuit, which looks 4 m ahead, now has the smallest RMS. The lag hurts less at 2 m/s, where the same trackers have RMS errors of 0.042, 0.177, 0.028 and 0.033 m, but rear-wheel feedback’s steering travel already nearly triples, from 34° to 96° (notebook). Snider saw the same on a simulated car with tyres: the simple trackers’ errors in curves grow with speed, and a tracker tuned with high gains for one course may meet “instability on other paths” (Snider §6). The curve’s tightness is the third test.

4.3 Curvature: a tighter arc

To see what the curvature does, we tighten the course’s arc to a radius of 6 m and keep the straights; the course is otherwise the same, so the change in the numbers is the curve’s. The steering the arc needs grows to:

tan−1(2.5/6)=22.62∘

which is most of the car’s 26.57° (notebook). At 2 m/s on the chapter’s car:

Pure pursuit cuts the tighter corner more (peak 0.280 m against 0.167 m), and Stanley’s rear axle runs further inside, because the gap of Section 3.2 grows as the circle shrinks. With the front axle on the 6 m arc, the same right triangle gives the rear axle’s radius Rr​ and the gap:

Rr​=62−2.52​=5.454 m 6−5.454=0.546 m

The trackers with a curvature term stay within 0.012 m. A gotcha: the feedforward alone takes 22.6° of the 26.6° the car has, so only 4° are left for the feedback to correct an error on this arc; on a curve tighter than the car’s 5 m circle no tracker can hold the line. What remains is how much work each tracker’s settings take.

4.4 Tuning effort

Each tracker has its own settings, and they depend on the speed and the steering differently:

  • Pure pursuit has one setting, the look-ahead distance ()Look-ahead distance (pure pursuit): The distance ld​ from the rear axle to the point pure pursuit chases, such as 4 m; a short one brings the car back sooner but weaves when the steering lags, a long one is smooth but cuts corners. Explained in: Pure Pursuit, §2. Chasing a point on the path. Pure pursuit needs no curvature, but the right look-ahead depends on the speed when the steering lags, so real stacks set it from the speed (see a look-ahead that grows with speedExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §6. A look-ahead that grows with speed) and add limits to it.
  • Stanley has a gain and a softening speed. Its front-axle error shrinks at a similar rate at any speed (see sExplained in: s), and a steering lag puts an upper limit on the gain (see Stanley’s gainExplained in: The Stanley Controller and Rear-Wheel Feedback, §4.1 The gain).
  • Rear-wheel feedback has two gains, scaled by the speed so that it behaves the same per metre at any speed (see why rear-wheel feedback convergesExplained in: The Stanley Controller and Rear-Wheel Feedback, §5.3 Why it converges: a spring and a damper over distance), but it needs the path’s curvature, from a path smooth enough to have one (Paden Table II). With the steering lag at 5 m/s its gains must come down: halving the heading gain and quartering the lateral gain cuts its RMS from 0.235 to 0.171 m and its steering travel from 203° to 90° (notebook).
  • The line rule plus the curve’s turn has two gains in units of time, so its behaviour changes with speed, as the next lines show.

On a straight, for small errors, the line rule’s lateral error e obeys a second-order system ()Second-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with a second derivative, such as the robot’s heading under PD control; written e” + 2ζω_n e’ + ω_n² e = 0, its reply is set by two numbers, the natural frequency and the damping ratio. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio equation (the model in path coordinatesExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §4.4 How the errors change: the model in path coordinates: the lateral error changes at the speed times the heading error, and the heading error at the turn rate minus the path’s). A dot is the rate per second and two dots the rate of that rate; KH​ is the heading gain, 2 per s, KD​ the lateral gain, 0.5 rad/s per m, and v the speed:

e¨+KH​e˙+vKD​e=0

The damping ratio ()Damping ratio: A second-order system’s damping compared with the damping that just stops it from swinging, ζ=Kd​/(2Kp​I​), 0.2 for D gain 0.08 and 1 for 0.4; it alone sets the shape of the reply and its overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio, the damping compared with the damping that just stops a swing, is then the heading gain over twice the square root of the speed times the lateral gain. At 2 m/s:

ζ=2/(22×0.5​) =1

At 5 m/s:

ζ=2/(25×0.5​) =0.632 ζ=KH​/(2vKD​​)

The same gains that are critically damped at 2 m/s swing past at 5 m/s. Rear-wheel feedback avoids this by multiplying its gains by the speed, the reason Paden’s survey writes its gains that way (Paden §V.A.2).

So the tuning effort is not the number of settings but how often they must change. Pure pursuit needs a new look-ahead when the speed changes and the steering lags, the line rule new gains whenever the speed changes; rear-wheel feedback and Stanley keep theirs until the steering lags.

5. Choosing a tracker

Key point

No tracker wins everywhere (Snider §6). Pick by the speed, the path and the steering, then measure the three numbers on your own car and course before trusting the choice.

The race of Section 4 and Snider’s comparison on a simulated car with tyres point the same way:

  1. A slow robot on a rough or jagged path, with few settings to tune: pure pursuit. Pure pursuit needs no curvature, copes with “large errors and discontinuous paths” (Snider §6), and at 5 m/s with a lagging motor had the smallest RMS here. Pure pursuit cuts corners and swings wide after them: up to 0.167 m off on our arc and 0.280 m on the tighter one. Nav2 runs regulated pure pursuit on indoor robotsExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §8. Where pure pursuit runs: Nav2 and Autoware.
  2. A car on smooth lanes with known curvature: feedforward from the curvature plus feedback. Rear-wheel feedback and the line rule with the curve’s turn held 0.007 m on the 10 m arc and 0.012 m on the 6 m arc at 2 m/s; Snider calls the LQR tracker with a feedforward term “a great choice for highway driving and many urban driving scenarios” (Snider §6). Their gains need retuning for speed and steering delay.
  3. When the front of the car must be on the line, or the path has no curvature: Stanley. Stanley holds the front axle within 0.005 m at 2 m/s and 0.017 m at 5 m/s with the same gain, “outperforms Pure Pursuit in most scenarios” (Snider §6), and drove Stanford’s car in the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge (Thrun et al. 2006). Its rear axle runs inside curves.
  4. Driving backwards, as when parking: rear-wheel feedback, which works for either sign of the speed (see rear-wheel feedback driving backwardsExplained in: The Stanley Controller and Rear-Wheel Feedback, §5.1 Why the rear axle: driving backwards); lower its gains if the steering lags.

Whatever the choice, measure it on the car’s own course at its own speeds with the three numbers of Section 3, at one point of the car.

6. Where feedforward steering runs: Apollo and Autoware

Both open stacks for self-driving cars steer with feedforward from the path’s curvature plus feedback (Figure 7):

  • Apollo’s lateral controller (lat_controller.cc in the folder lat_based_lqr_controller of modules/control/controllers, ApolloAuto/apollo repository) adds three parts: feedback, a linear-quadratic regulator’s gains times the lateral error, the heading error and their rates; feedforward, from ComputeFeedForward; and a lead-lag term on the lateral error (the steering angle is the sum of steer_angle_feedback, steer_angle_feedforward and steer_angle_feedback_augment). Its feedforward starts with wheelbase_ * ref_curvature, the wheelbase times the curvature, which is our 0.25 rad (14.3°) on the 10 m arc before the arctangent: close to 14.04° for gentle curves. Apollo adds two terms that our kinematic car, measured at the rear axle, does not need: a speed term for tyre slip at speed, from the car’s mass and tyre stiffness, and a term that offsets the heading part of its feedback on a curve (Snider §4.3 derives all three).
  • Autoware’s model predictive controller (control/autoware_mpc_lateral_controller in autoware_universe) starts from a reference steering along the path, u_ref = atan(wheelbase * curvature) in vehicle_model_bicycle_kinematics.cpp, our formula exactly, and sets it to 0 when it is below mpc_zero_ff_steer_deg, 0.5°, to “ignore curvature noise” (mpc.cpp). Its optimiser then works out the steering over the next 5 s, and its cost also weighs how much the steering changes from step to step, a measure of steering effort. A later Note on model predictive control for path following builds it.

Figure 7: Feedforward steering in two open stacks, read from their code. Apollo’s lateral controller adds a feedforward (the wheelbase times the curvature, plus a speed term for tyre slip and a term for the heading feedback), an LQR feedback on the lateral and heading errors and their rates, and a lead-lag term. Autoware’s model predictive controller starts from the reference steering arctan(L κ), set to 0 below 0.5°, and optimises the steering over the next 5 s

Open code shows the same structure: PythonRobotics’ lqr_steer_control.py computes ff = math.atan2(L * k, 1) and steers with delta = ff + fb.

  • On the chapter’s car the trackers with a curvature term are best (0.007 m at worst); with a 0.4 s steering lag at 5 m/s pure pursuit is best on RMS and rear-wheel feedback weaves, because trackers that act hard on errors at the car overshoot when the wheels answer late.

The car can now choose a tracker for its speed, path and steering, and measure the choice with three numbers. Still missing: every tracker here reacts to the path at the car or at one point ahead, with a simple model and no limits in the law; planning the steering over the next seconds with the car’s model and limits is model predictive control, which a later Note builds, after the chapter on the autonomy stack that puts these trackers in their place.
