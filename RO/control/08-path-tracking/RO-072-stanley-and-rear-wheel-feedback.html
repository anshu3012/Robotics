1. Overview

Key point

Pure pursuit steers for a point ahead and never looks at the path where the car is, so it turns early at the corners and its look-ahead must be retuned for speed. The Stanley controller steers the front wheels from the errors at the front axle, and rear-wheel feedback turns the car from the errors and the path’s curvature at the rear axle; both follow the 10 m arc with no look-ahead to tune.

2. Stanley: steer the front wheels from the front axle

Key point

Stanley measures the lateral error and the heading error at the front axle, and turns the front wheels parallel to the course (the heading term), then further towards it (the cross-track term), so that they aim at a course point a set distance ahead, the speed plus a softening speed over a gain: at the start, 3 m ahead, so the wheels turn 18.4° to the right at 2 m/s.

2.1 Why the front axle

Pure pursuit’s corner errors come from aiming at a point ahead. The front wheel is the wheel that steers, and it rolls the way it points. Point it at the course and the front axle heads for the course at once; point it along the course and the front axle runs along it. A cyclist riding along a painted line does the same: the front wheel is kept on the line, and the rest of the bicycle follows.

So Stanley measures its errors where the steering acts: the lateral error is “the lateral distance of the center of the vehicle’s front wheels from the nearest point on the trajectory” (Thrun et al. §9.2; Hoffmann et al. §III.A). At the start the front axle is 2.5 m ahead of the rear axle, at (2.5, 1.0), so its nearest course point is (2.5, 0): its lateral error is 1.0 m, and its heading error is 0, because the car points along the course.

2.2 The heading term and the cross-track term

Stanley turns the wheels in two steps (Snider §2.3; Thrun et al. §9.2):

  1. The heading term turns the wheels from the car’s heading to the course’s direction at the front axle’s nearest point, so that with no lateral error the wheels point along the course; the term is minus the heading error.
  2. The cross-track term turns them further towards the course, more the farther away the front axle is, by the angle whose tangent is the gain times the lateral error over the speed plus a softening speed.

The cross-track angle has a simple picture. A wheel turned by that angle from the course’s direction heads for the course point a fixed distance ahead of the nearest point, because the tangent of the angle is the lateral error over that distance. With:

  • e, the front axle’s lateral error, positive to the left: 1.0 m at the start;
  • θe​, its heading error, positive to the left: 0 at the start;
  • v, the car’s speed, 2 m/s;
  • k, the gain, 1.0 per s;
  • ks​, the softening speed, 1.0 m/s (Section 4.2);
  • d, how far ahead of the nearest point the wheels aim, in m;
  • δ, the steering angle, positive to the left, at most 26.6° either way.
d=(1+2)/1=3 m tan(cross-track term)=1.0/3 d=kks​+v​ tan(cross-track term)=de​=ks​+vke​

The law adds the two terms, with signs that steer towards the course (Hoffmann et al. eqs. 5 and 9; Snider eq. 5; Paden eq. V.15):

δ=−θe​−tan−1ks​+vke​

and the result is held within ±26.6°, the car’s limit. At the start, at 2 m/s:

δ=−0−tan−1(1×1.0/3) =−tan−1(0.333)=−18.4∘

The wheels turn 18.4° to the right, towards the course. At 5 m/s the aim point is 6 m ahead and the wheels turn 9.5°. Hoffmann et al. write the same law with the course’s direction seen from the car and the lateral error positive to the right; with our signs, positive to the left, it is the line above. The steering limit is part of the law: the steering angle is held at the limit whenever the formula asks for more (Hoffmann et al. eq. 5).

The rule is the Stanley controller ()Stanley controller (front-wheel feedback): A path tracker that measures the lateral and heading errors at the front axle and steers the front wheels parallel to the path and then towards it by tan−1(ke/(ks​+v)), aiming them at a path point ahead; the front-axle error then shrinks by the same fraction each second, but the law is not stable when reversing. Explained in §2.2 The heading term and the cross-track term., also called front-wheel feedback (Paden §V.A.3): the steering law of Stanford University’s car Stanley (Thrun et al. 2006; Hoffmann et al. 2007).

3. Why the front-axle error shrinks by the same fraction every second

Key point

With the wheels turned by Stanley’s two terms, the front axle moves at the cross-track angle to the course, so its lateral error, its distance from the course, shrinks in proportion to itself: by the same fraction every second, set by the gain k, almost whatever the speed, and on the chapter’s car, whose wheels turn at once, it never swings past the line. At 2 m/s the error halves in 1.03 s; at 5 m/s in 0.81 s, though the car drives twice as far.

3.1 The front axle heads for the course

At the start the front wheel points 18.4° to the right of the course, so the front axle moves towards the course at 2 m/s times the sine of 18.4°. The heading term keeps cancelling the car’s own turning, so at every tick the front wheel makes the cross-track angle with the course, and only that angle decides how fast the error shrinks (Hoffmann et al. eq. 7; Thrun et al. eq. 8; Paden eq. V.13). With the symbols of Section 2.2, the lateral error e, the gain k, the softening speed ks​ and the speed v, and:

  • e˙, how fast the front axle’s lateral error changes, in m/s;
  • v, the speed, also stands for the front wheel’s speed, which is close (exactly, the rear axle’s speed divided by the cosine of the steering angle).

At the start, at 2 m/s:

e˙=−2×sin(18.4∘) =−2×0.316=−0.632 m/s e˙=−vsin(tan−1ks​+vke​)

For a small error the sine of a small angle is the angle, and the angle is about its tangent:

e˙≈−ks​+vkv​e

The error shrinks at a rate in proportion to itself, which makes it a first-order system ()First-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with only a first derivative, such as the robot’s speed under P control; it closes the same fraction of its remaining gap in every time constant and can never overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant: it closes the same fraction of its gap in every second and can never overshoot. Without the softening speed, the rate is the gain k itself, 1 per s, whatever the speed, so after a time t in seconds the error from a 1.0 m start is (Thrun et al. eq. 9; Hoffmann et al. §III.A):

e(t)=1.0×exp(−1×t) e(t)=e(0)exp(−kt)

With the softening speed of 1 m/s the rate is a little lower:

rate at 2 m/s=1×2/3=0.667 per s rate at 5 m/s=1×5/6=0.833 per s

The time constant ()Time constant: The time a first-order system takes to close 63 % of the gap to its final value, m/(K_v + b) = 1 s for the robot’s speed loop; it fixes the rise time (about 2.2 time constants) and the 2 % settling time (about 4). Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant, one over the rate, is 1.5 s at 2 m/s and 1.2 s at 5 m/s. The time to halve the error is the natural log of 2, 0.693, over the rate:

0.693/0.667=1.04 s at 2 m/s 0.693/0.833=0.83 s at 5 m/s

When the error is large the arctangent nears 90° and is held at the steering limit, so the car drives straight at the course and the error shrinks no faster than the speed allows (Hoffmann et al. §III.A).

3.3 On the arc: the front axle stays on it, the rear axle cuts inside

On the 10 m arc the front axle’s lateral error falls to 0.004 m by the middle of the arc (notebook). There the cross-track term is about zero, so the heading term alone does the steering. Paden et al. (§V.A.3) point out that this term is then not a heading error to be removed: on a curve the front wheel must point along the course, while the car’s body, with its rear axle on a smaller circle, points 14.5° to the outside of the course’s direction at the front axle (worked out below; Figure 4), and the heading term holds exactly the steering angle that the curve needs.

4. Tuning Stanley: the gain and the softening speed

Key point

A larger gain k brings the front axle back sooner, until the steering hits its limit; once we give the car RO-071’s steering lag of 0.4 s, the delay of real steering, at 5 m/s gains of 2.5 and 5 per s swing 0.24 and 0.46 m past the line, so the gain has an upper limit. The softening speed ks​, 1 m/s, keeps the gain on the lateral error, the front axle’s distance from the course, below 1 per m when the car is slow, so noise in the measured error does not shake the wheels.

4.1 The gain

The gain sets the rate at which the front axle returns. On the chapter’s car, whose wheels turn at once, more gain only means a faster return: at 5 m/s, with k of 0.5, 1, 2.5 and 5 per s, the front axle is within 0.1 m after 32.3, 15.4, 7.8 and 5.1 m along the course, and at 5 per s the wheels sit at full lock at the start (notebook). Section 3.1 says why: the error shrinks in proportion to the gain, and nothing in that kinematic model can swing.

A real car’s steering does lag, and Snider, testing on a full car simulator with delays added to the steering (Snider §1), found an upper limit on the gain (§2.3.1). So, as RO-071 did, we change the chapter’s car for this sweep: it gets RO-071’s steering motorExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §5.2 A real steering motor: the short look-ahead weaves, which turns the wheels with a time constant of 0.4 s. Without the lag, no gain would show the limit. Figure 5 sweeps the gain again at 5 m/s. 4.2 The softening speed

The cross-track term divides by the speed. Slowly, the gain on the lateral error becomes huge: at 0.5 m/s, with k = 1 per s and no softening, it is 2 per m, and at a standstill it has no limit. Measured positions jitter, so the measured lateral error jitters too, and a large gain passes the jitter to the wheels (Hoffmann et al. §III.A). A jump of 0.05 m in the measured error at 0.5 m/s turns the wheels by (notebook):

tan−1(1×0.05/0.5)=5.7∘

Adding a softening speed ()Softening speed (Stanley): A constant ks​, such as 1 m/s, added to the speed in the Stanley controller’s cross-track term, so its gain on the lateral error never exceeds k/ks​ at low speed; it stops noise in the measured error from shaking the wheels when the car is slow. Explained in §4.2 The softening speed. ks​ to the speed in the denominator caps the gain at the gain k over ks​, 1 per m, “permitting control to be arbitrarily soft at low speeds”; Stanford’s car used 1 m/s (Hoffmann et al. §III.A). With it, the same jump at 0.5 m/s turns the wheels by:

tan−1(1×0.05/1.5)=1.9∘

Figure 6 draws the gain on the lateral error against speed, with and without the softening speed.

Figure 6: Stanley’s gain on the lateral error against speed, for k = 1 per s. Red: k / v, without a softening speed, which grows without limit as the car slows (2 per m at 0.5 m/s). Blue: k / (1 m/s + v), with a softening speed of 1 m/s, which never exceeds 1 per m (0.667 per m at 0.5 m/s)

The cost shows at higher speeds: at 2 m/s the softened gain is 1/3 per m instead of 1/2, so the aim point is 3 m ahead instead of 2 m and the error shrinks at 0.667 per s instead of 1 per s (Section 3.1). At 5 m/s the two differ by less than a fifth.

4.3 What Stanford’s car added

Extra

On the real car, tyres slip and the steering column, the steering motor and the computer all add delay. Stanford’s final law (Hoffmann et al. eq. 9; Thrun et al. §9.2) adds three terms to the two of Section 2:

  • a damping term on the yaw rate, the rate at which the car’s heading turns, because the tyres damp the car’s yawing less as the speed grows;
  • a damping term on how fast the steering angle changes, against the motor’s delay;
  • a steady heading offset on curves, worked out from a bicycle model with tyre slip, so that the car can lean its heading into a turn as a slipping car must.

Hoffmann et al. report that the law ran “without fault on Stanley for hundreds of miles of testing” and in the race. MATLAB’s Stanley block offers the same choice: a kinematic bicycle model, “suitable for path following in low-speed environments”, and a dynamic one for high speeds, which also takes the path’s curvature, the yaw rate and the steering angle, with gains on the last two (MathWorks Lateral Controller Stanley documentation).

Stanley handles the corners and its gain has a clear meaning. But it has one blind spot that matters for a car that parks: driving backwards.

5. Rear-wheel feedback: the rear axle, the path’s curvature, and reverse

Key point

Rear-wheel feedback asks for a turn rate with three parts: the turn the path’s curvature needs, a part against the heading error, how far the car points off the course’s direction, and a part against the rear axle’s lateral error, its distance from the course; on the 10 m arc the first part alone holds the car on the line. Near the line it is a spring and a damper over distance, with a damping ratio of 0.707 for our gains, and it works driving backwards, where Stanley turns the car round.

5.1 Why the rear axle: driving backwards

The car of the Hybrid A* plannerExplained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §1. Overview ended its route by backing into a parking bay. Backwards, Stanley’s front axle trails behind, and pointing the front wheels at the course turns the car the wrong way: Paden et al. (§V.A.3) warn that Stanley “is not stable in reverse making it unsuitable for parking”. Stanford’s car reversed with a variant that uses the rear wheels as the guiding wheels (Hoffmann et al. §III.A): measure at the axle that leads, which is the idea of this section.

Figure 7 tries it. The car faces along the first straight, 1.0 m left of it, and drives backwards at 2 m/s for 8 s. The blue car steers by rear-wheel feedback, built in Sections 5.2 and 5.3: it is within 0.1 m after 2.1 s, having driven 4.1 m back.

5.2 Three parts of a turn rate

Rear-wheel feedback ()Rear-wheel feedback: A path tracker that measures the errors at the rear axle and asks for a turn rate made of the path’s own turn (the speed times the path curvature) plus parts against the heading error and the lateral error, turned into steering by the bicycle model; it holds curves with no steady error and still works when the car reverses. Explained in §5.2 Three parts of a turn rate. measures its errors at the rear axle and asks for a turn rate, the rate at which the car’s heading should turn, written ω as for the differential driveDifferential drive: A robot with two independently driven wheels on one axle (plus a caster); it steers by running the wheels at different speeds and can spin on the spot. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.1 Why we describe motion by linear and angular velocity (Paden §V.A.2). Its three parts (Paden eq. V.12):

  1. the path-curvature part, the turn rate that keeps a car on the line turning with the course: the speed times the path curvature ()Path curvature κ: How fast a path’s direction turns per metre along it, 1/R on an arc of radius R, such as 0.1 per m on a 10 m arc and 0 on a straight; it fixes the steering a bend needs, tanδ=Lκ. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §2.3 Path curvature, corrected for the car being off the line or askew;
  2. the heading part, against the heading error, in proportion to the speed;
  3. the lateral part, against the lateral error, in proportion to the speed.

With:

  • e, the rear axle’s lateral error, positive to the left: 1.0 m at the start;
  • θe​, the heading error at the rear axle’s nearest point, in rad: 0 at the start;
  • κ, the course’s curvature at that point: 0 on the straights, 0.1 per m on the arc;
  • v, the speed, 2 m/s (negative when driving backwards);
  • kθ​, the heading gain, 1.0 per m;
  • ke​, the lateral gain, 0.5 per m²;
  • ωκ​, ωθ​ and ωe​, the path-curvature, heading and lateral parts, in rad/s;
  • ω, the turn rate asked for, their sum, in rad/s;
  • L, the wheelbase, 2.5 m, and δ, the steering angle.
ωκ​=1−κevκcosθe​​ ωθ​=−kθ​∣v∣θe​ ωe​=−ke​vθe​sinθe​​e ω=ωκ​+ωθ​+ωe​

The bicycle model turns the turn rate into a steering angle: the car turns at its speed times the curvature it drives, and that curvature is the tangent of the steering angle over the wheelbase (pure pursuit’s steering stepExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §3.3 From curvature to steering angle):

δ=tan−1vLω​

The units of the gains make each part a turn rate: kθ​ times a speed in m/s and an angle in rad gives rad/s when kθ​ is in 1/m, and ke​ times a speed and a distance in m gives rad/s when ke​ is in 1/m². The values 1.0 and 0.5 are PythonRobotics’ defaults (KTH, KE in rear_wheel_feedback_control.py). The fraction sinθe​/θe​ is close to 1 for small angles (0.993 at 0.2 rad) and is 1 at θe​=0. The divisor 1−κe is 1 on the line; off the line it corrects for the nearest course point moving faster or slower than the car, as the path coordinates of the errors’ motionExplained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §4.4 How the errors change: the model in path coordinates show (Paden eq. V.10).

At the start, on the straight, the curvature part and the heading part are 0, and the fraction sinθe​/θe​ is 1:

ω=0−0−0.5×2×1×1.0 =−1.0 rad/s δ=tan−1(2.5×(−1.0)/2) =tan−1(−1.25)=−51.3∘

More than the car can steer, so the wheels go to the limit, −26.6°.

On the arc, on the line, the lateral and heading parts are 0 and the curvature part does all the turning:

ω=2×0.1=0.2 rad/s δ=tan−1(2.5×0.2/2)=14.0∘

the steering of the rear axle’s 10 m circle. 5.3 Why it converges: a spring and a damper over distance

Near a straight course the law is pure pursuit’sExplained in: Pure Pursuit, §4.2 Why it does not weave: the look-ahead point sees the heading spring and damper again, with its two gains chosen directly. As there, we follow the error along the distance driven. With the lateral error e, the heading error θe​ and the gains kθ​ and ke​ of Section 5.2, for small angles and forward driving, and:

  • e′, how fast the lateral error changes per metre driven: the heading error θe​;
  • e′′, how fast e′ changes per metre: the turn rate over the speed.

Dividing the turn rate by the speed, on a straight course:

e′′=−1.0e′−0.5e e′′+1.0e′+0.5e=0 e′′+kθ​e′+ke​e=0

Matched to the natural frequency and damping ratio (, )Natural frequency: The rate, in rad/s, at which a second-order system would swing with no damping, ωn​=Kp​/I​, 2 rad/s for the heading loop; it sets how fast the reply is, and raising it squeezes the whole reply in time. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratioDamping ratio: A second-order system’s damping compared with the damping that just stops it from swinging, ζ=Kd​/(2Kp​I​), 0.2 for D gain 0.08 and 1 for 0.4; it alone sets the shape of the reply and its overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio, per metre driven:

ωn2​=0.5 ωn​=0.7071 per m ζ=1.0/(2×0.7071) =0.707 ωn​=ke​​ ζ=2ke​​kθ​​

With a damping ratio of 0.707 the overshootOvershoot (step response): How far a step response’s peak goes past its final value, as a percentage of the final value, such as 52.7 % when a 0.5 rad turn peaks at 0.763 rad; it says how far a reply swings past its target. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §6.1 Overshoot depends only on the damping ratio is 4.3 percent, 0.043 m from a 1.0 m start; the run crosses by 0.039 m (notebook). The speed has divided out, so the car returns over the same distance at any speed: within 0.1 m after 4.1 m at 2 m/s (2.1 s) and 4.0 m at 5 m/s (0.83 s). Paden et al. multiply the heading gain by the size of the speed for exactly this reason (§V.A.2).

On the arc the curvature part turns the car with the course, so the errors stay at zero: 0.000 m in the middle of the arc and 0.005 m after it (notebook).

Backwards, the speed is negative. The heading part keeps its sign, because it uses the size of the speed, so it still damps. The lateral part changes sign with the speed, and so does the way the lateral error changes as the car rolls, so the part still pushes the error towards zero. Paden et al. (§V.A.2) state that the law converges for either direction of travel, shown with an energy-like function of the two errors that can only fall; such functions are the tool of Kanayama’s trackerKanayama’s tracker: A trajectory tracker for a wheeled robot that sends the reference’s speed and turn rate plus feedback on the error in the robot’s frame, v=vr​cosθe​+Kx​xe​ and ω=ωr​+vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​); a Lyapunov function proves its error cannot run away while the reference drives forward. Explained in: Tracking a Timed Trajectory: Kanayama and Feedback Linearisation, §6.1 An energy of the error that can only fall. In Figure 7 the reversing car returns over 4.1 m with 0.039 m past the line, the same as forwards.

6. Where they run

Key point

Stanley steered Stanford’s car to win the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, at 20 commands a second with a softening speed of 1 m/s. Autoware and Nav2 ship neither Stanley nor rear-wheel feedback: Autoware’s lateral controllers are model predictive control and pure pursuit, Nav2’s are regulated pure pursuit, MPPI and others.

Stanley in the desert. Stanley “won the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge”, a race for driverless cars across the desert (Thrun et al. 2006). Its steering controller took the planner’s trajectory, the estimated pose and speed and the measured steering angle, and sent steering commands 20 times a second (Thrun et al. §9.2), the rate our trackers use. The law was Section 2’s with a softening speed of 1 m/s and the damping terms of Section 4.3; Stanford’s team later drove its Urban Challenge car, Junior, with it at up to 71 mph on pavement (Hoffmann et al. §III.A). MATLAB’s Automated Driving Toolbox ships a Stanley lateral controller block, in kinematic and dynamic versions, with a position gain of 2.5 by default and a steering limit of 35° (MathWorks Lateral Controller Stanley documentation).

Not in Autoware, Nav2 or Apollo. None of the three open stacks ships either tracker:

  • Autoware: its trajectory follower picks its lateral controller with lateral_controller_mode, which is either mpc (model predictive control) or pure_pursuit (controller_node.cpp in autoware_trajectory_follower_node);
  • Nav2: its controller plugins are regulated pure pursuit, MPPI (its default in nav2_params.yaml), DWB, the graceful controller and the rotation shim;
  • Apollo: it steers with an LQR controller (lat_based_lqr_controller) or MPC.

Open code. PythonRobotics implements both: stanley_control.py, with gain 0.5 per s, the error at the front axle and no softening speed; and rear_wheel_feedback_control.py, which is Paden’s eq. V.12 with the gains 1.0 and 0.5 used here.

Rear-wheel feedbackrear axlecurvature part + heading part + lateral part, then δ=tan−1(Lω/v)within 0.1 m after 4.1 m at any speed; 0.000 m mid-arc; works backwards
  • The opening problem has its answer: pure pursuit’s rear axle ran 0.12 m inside the arc and 0.17 m wide after it, while Stanley’s front axle is 0.004 m off in the middle of the arc and rear-wheel feedback’s rear axle 0.000 m, because both use the course’s direction at the car rather than at a point ahead.
  • Stanley points the front wheels parallel to the course and then towards it, so they aim at the course point a set distance ahead, the speed plus the softening speed over the gain: 3 m at 2 m/s, because that one angle shrinks the front axle’s error directly.
  • The front wheel rolls the way it points, so Stanley’s front-axle error shrinks in proportion to itself, a first-order system whose rate is the gain times the speed over the speed plus the softening speed (0.667 per s at 2 m/s): on a car whose wheels turn at once it never swings, and it takes a similar time at any speed, so the fast car uses more road (4.0 m against 2.0 m to halve the error).
  • On the arc Stanley’s heading term supplies the 14.5° the curve needs, so its front axle stays on the course while its rear axle runs 0.32 m inside; trackers must be compared at the same point of the car.
  • A larger gain returns faster, but with a steering motor that lags 0.4 s, which we added to the chapter’s car because real steering lags, gains of 2.5 and 5 per s swing past the line by 0.24 and 0.46 m, because the law acts on errors the lagging wheels answer late, so the gain has an upper limit.
  • The softening speed caps the gain on the lateral error at the gain over the softening speed, 1 per m, so a 0.05 m jump in the measured error turns the wheels 1.9° instead of 5.7° at 0.5 m/s.
  • Rear-wheel feedback adds the turn the path’s curvature needs to feedback on the heading and lateral errors, so it holds the arc with no error and, near the line, is a spring and a damper over distance with a damping ratio of 0.707, settling within 4.1 m at any speed.
  • Rear-wheel feedback works backwards and Stanley does not, because the rear axle’s errors keep shrinking for either sign of the speed while the trailing front axle turns the car round.

The car can now follow a path through its corners, forwards or backwards, with no look-ahead to retune.
