1. Overview

Key point

A timed trajectory says where the robot should be at every moment; Kanayama’s tracker sends the timetable’s own speeds plus feedback on the gap, measured along the robot’s own axes, and a Lyapunov function proves that the error cannot grow without bound. Our robot starts 0.224 m off its timetable and is within 1 cm of it after 3.35 s.

  2. a 90° left arc of radius 1 m;

2. The reference robot and its timetable

Key point

The reference robot is an imagined second robot that drives the timetable exactly; tracking means keeping our robot at its pose. Copying its speeds only works from where it starts: two robots facing the same way with the same commands keep the same gap for ever.

2.1 A robot that drives the timetable exactly

The opening’s target moves: where the timetable says to be changes every moment. So we picture a second robot that drives the timetable exactly, always where our robot should be, facing the way it should face, at the speeds it should drive. This imagined robot is the reference robot ()Reference robot: An imagined robot that drives a timed trajectory exactly, so at every moment its pose and its speeds are what the real robot’s should be; tracking a timetable then means staying on top of this one moving robot. Explained in §2.1 A robot that drives the timetable exactly., the orange robot of Figure 1 (Kanayama 1991 §2.2 calls its pose the reference configuration). Our robot’s task is to stay at its pose, which is tracking ()Tracking: Making an output follow a reference that changes over time, such as a planned speed rising at 0.25 m/s²; also called the servo problem, it needs the controller to keep up with the change, and a correction from the error alone lags behind. Explained in: Feedback Basics: Open and Closed Loop and the Block Diagram, §6. Holding a value and following a plan: regulation and tracking, following a reference ()Reference: The value we want the controlled output to have, such as a wanted speed of 0.5 m/s from the plan; the controller compares the measured output with it. Explained in: Feedback Basics: Open and Closed Loop and the Block Diagram, §4.1 The parts of the loop, named on the robot that changes over time.

The reference robot at time t:

  • xr​ and yr​ are its position, in m: (1.717, 0.303) at t=6 s, on the arc;
  • θr​ is its heading, in rad: 45.8° at t=6 s;
  • vr​ is its forward speed, 0.3 m/s all the way;
  • ωr​ is its turn rate, in rad/s: 0 on the straights, 0.3 rad/s on the arc.

On the arc it turns at its speed divided by the radius, the turning radius ()Turning radius R: The distance from the point a robot is turning about (the instantaneous centre of curvature, ICC) to the robot’s reference point; R=v/ω, so it says how tight a turn is. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.2 Why the robot turns about a point on its axle line rule of RO-001 run backwards:

ωr​=0.3/1.0=0.3 rad/s

The timetable’s three parts take:

1/0.3=3.333 s (π/2)×1/0.3=5.236 s 1/0.3=3.333 s

So the arc starts at 3.333 s, ends at 8.569 s, and the timetable ends at 11.903 s, which we write 11.90 s.

Our robot is the differential drive of RO-001: its pose is x, y and θ, and we command its forward speed v and its turn rate ω. The kinematic model ()Kinematic model: Equations that give how a robot’s configuration changes from its speed inputs alone, ignoring masses and forces, such as x˙=vcosθ, y˙​=vsinθ, θ˙=ω; used to predict motion at low speed. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model turns them into motion, the robot moving the way it faces:

Each wheel turns at most 0.6 m/s, and a tracker sends a new command 20 times a second (20 Hz), the rate of Nav2’s sample controller configuration (Nav2 nav2_params.yaml). The simplest way to keep to the timetable is to drive what the reference robot drives.

2.2 Replaying the timetable’s speeds: feedforward alone

The opening’s first failure is this simplest tracker: it sends the reference robot’s own speed and turn rate as its commands. A command computed from the plan, before any error appears, is feedforward ()Feedforward: A control force computed from the plan and the robot’s model, such as mass times the planned acceleration plus drag (mar​+bvr​), and sent before any error appears; it lets the robot follow a changing plan without first falling behind, while feedback removes what the model gets wrong. Explained in: Feedforward, Integral Action, Cascaded Loops and Windup, §2.2 Feedforward from the robot’s model. Figure 1 (grey) shows it on our start: it drives the timetable’s shape 0.2 m behind and 0.1 m to the left, and the gap never closes.

Why the gap stays. Both robots start facing 0° and turn at the same rate, so at every moment they face the same way. Both drive at 0.3 m/s. The rate of the gap along x is the reference robot’s x speed minus ours; at t=6 s, both facing 45.8°:

0.3cos45.8∘−0.3cos45.8∘=0 vr​cosθr​−vcosθ=0

The same holds along y with sines, and the headings turn at the same rate. So the gap of 0.2 m and 0.1 m stays for ever; in the run the 20 Hz command steps shift it only a little, between 0.215 and 0.224 m. Feedforward alone keeps any error the robot starts with or meets on the way (MR §11.3.1), so it works only from a start exactly on the timetable. To close the gap, the tracker must measure it.

3. Feedback linearisation: steering a point ahead of the axle

Key point

A point P a distance d ahead of the axle can move in any direction, because turning swings it sideways at d times the turn rate, so a law can make its gap to the reference shrink by the same fraction every cycle, at any size. The heading is left out of that law: with P behind the axle, the robot turns round and drives the timetable backwards with P on time.

3.1 The axle midpoint cannot be steered sideways

Replaying the speeds left the gap of 0.2 m and 0.1 m in place. To close it, the tracker must measure the gap and steer against it. The most direct way steers our robot’s position straight at the reference robot’s: at the start, move 0.2 m/s along x and −0.1 m/s along y on top of the timetable’s speed. But the position of our robot is the axle midpoint, which can only move along its heading, the nonholonomic constraint ()Nonholonomic constraint: A rule on velocities that does not follow from any equation on the pose, such as a wheel that cannot slide sideways; it limits which way a robot can move at each moment but not which poses it can reach. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §6.2 Can the robot still reach every pose? of RO-001: facing along x, it has no way to move along y.

3.2 A point ahead of the axle can move any way

Take instead a point P fixed to the robot a distance d ahead of the axle midpoint (MR §13.3.1, Figure 13.12; Paden §V.B.2). The wheels then turn at 0.45 and 0.55 m/s, under the limit. At d=0 the second line divides by zero: the midpoint has no sideways speed to give, and the commands for it do not exist (MR §13.3.1 calls the matrix from v and ω to the point’s velocity singular there). Why P moves that way. P sits at the midpoint plus d along the heading. In the room’s axes, at heading θ:

xP​=x+dcosθ yP​=y+dsinθ

3.3 The gap shrinks 5 percent every cycle

We choose P‘s wanted velocity as a feedforward part, the reference point’s own velocity as in Section 2.2, plus a feedback part on the gap. The reference robot has its own point Pr​, 0.2 m ahead of its axle. At the start:

  • Pr​ is at (0.2, 0) m and moves at 0.3 m/s along x, its velocity P˙r​;
  • P is at (0, 0.1) m, 0.2 m ahead of our robot at (−0.2, 0.1);
  • k is the gain, 1 per s;
  • u is P‘s wanted velocity in the room.
u=P˙r​+k(Pr​−P)

Along x and along y at the start:

ux​=0.3+1×(0.2−0)=0.5 m/s uy​=0+1×(0−0.1)=−0.1 m/s

The two speeds are those of Figure 2b (facing along x, the robot’s axes are the room’s). Section 3.2 turns u into v and ω exactly, so P moves at u. The gap e is the reference point minus ours, an arrow with an x part and a y part, 0.224 m long at the start:

e=Pr​−P

Its rate:

e˙=P˙r​−u =−ke

The gap shrinks at k times itself, whatever its size and whatever the robot’s heading: an exactly straight-line (linear ())Linear function: A function y=ax+b: a fixed multiple of the input plus a constant, whose graph is a straight line; passing a normal variable through it gives another normal variable, which is why the Kalman filter is exact for linear models. Explained in: Kalman Filter, §2.3 A linear function keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian rule, with no sines or products. Choosing the commands through the inverse of the robot’s model so that an output obeys a linear rule is feedback linearisation ()Feedback linearisation: Choosing the commands through the inverse of a robot’s model so that a chosen output obeys a linear rule exactly, such as a point’s gap to its reference shrinking at k times itself; it makes the error’s reply the same at every size, so one gain sets it. Explained in §3.3 The gap shrinks 5 percent every cycle. (Paden §V.B.2, output feedback linearization; MR eq. 13.29 and 13.21 steer P the same way without the feedforward part). The law is the feedforward plus proportional feedback that makes an error decay exponentially (MR §11.3.1, eq. 11.15 without its integral part), made possible for a robot that cannot slide sideways by steering a point ahead of the axle.

Our tracker sends one command every 0.05 s, so in each cycle the gap shrinks by k times 0.05, 5 percent. After 20 cycles, 1 s:

0.9520=0.3585 0.2236×0.3585=0.0802 m

The run gives 0.0800 m (notebook); a gap shrinking smoothly at the same rate, with no 0.05 s steps, would be at 0.0823 m. The law steers only P; the body follows P without a law of its own.

The law of Section 3.3 holds P on time but says nothing about which way the body faces. MR §13.3.4 allows P anywhere on the robot off the axle line, so Figure 3 (right) runs the same law with P 0.2 m behind the axle (d=−0.2 m). Watch the right-hand robot: within about 3 s it swings round and then drives the whole timetable backwards.

What sets the final heading. Once P is on time, P moves with Pr​, at 0.3 m/s along the timetable on a straight. If the timetable heads at an angle to the left of our robot, the part of that velocity to our robot’s left is 0.3 m/s times the sine of the angle, and Section 3.2 turns the robot at that sideways speed divided by d. The symbols:

  • φ is the angle the timetable heads to the left of our robot, 10° in the example, where its sine is 0.1736;
  • vr​ is the timetable’s speed, 0.3 m/s;
  • d is the distance of P ahead of the axle, 0.2 m (−0.2 m when P is behind);
  • ω is the turn rate the law then commands, in rad/s.
ω=0.3×0.1736/0.2 =0.26 rad/s ω=vr​sinφ/d

With P ahead, the robot turns left, towards the timetable’s heading, so φ shrinks. With d=−0.2 m, the same line gives −0.26 rad/s: the robot turns away and φ grows until the sine is zero again at 180°, facing backwards. A trailer behaves the same way: towed with its wheels behind the hitch it follows, and pushed with its wheels in front it swings round. Here P is the hitch and the robot’s wheels are the trailer’s.

Feedback linearisation gives an exactly linear gap and a single gain, at the cost of an uncontrolled heading, turn rates that grow as d shrinks, and forward driving with P ahead (Paden Table II: “forward driving only”). To keep the heading, a tracker must measure it, together with the rest of the gap.

4. The error in the robot’s own frame

Key point

The robot can only speed up, slow down and turn, so the gap to the reference robot is measured along the robot’s own axes: xe​ ahead, ye​ to the left, and θe​, the reference heading minus ours. The same gap in the room means “speed up” for a robot facing one way and “turn right” for a robot facing another.

4.1 Why the room’s axes are the wrong ones

The offset point measured only P‘s gap, along the room’s axes, and left the heading uncontrolled. Kanayama measures the whole gap, heading included, from the robot itself. Take the opening’s gap again: in the room, the reference robot is 0.2 m further along x and 0.1 m lower in y. What our robot should do about that depends on which way it faces (Figure 4):

  • Facing 0°, at the start: the reference robot is 0.2 m straight ahead and 0.1 m to the right, so the robot should speed up and turn a little right.
  • Facing 45°, halfway round the arc while replaying: the same gap in the room is only 0.071 m ahead but 0.212 m to the right, so the robot should hardly speed up and should turn right.

(b) Facing 45°, the same gap in the room is x_e = 0.071 m ahead and y_e = −0.212 m. (c) The third number, θ_e: the reference robot’s heading minus ours, here 30°

The robot’s two commands act along its own axes: v moves it ahead, ω turns it, and the nonholonomic constraint ()Nonholonomic constraint: A rule on velocities that does not follow from any equation on the pose, such as a wheel that cannot slide sideways; it limits which way a robot can move at each moment but not which poses it can reach. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §6.2 Can the robot still reach every pose?, the rule that its wheels cannot slide, stops it moving sideways. So we measure the gap along those axes (Kanayama 1991 eq. 3):

  • xe​ is how far the reference robot is ahead of ours, along our heading, in m;
  • ye​ is how far it is to our left, in m, negative to the right;
  • θe​ is its heading minus ours, in rad, positive when it heads further left.

The three numbers together are the error in the robot’s frame ()Error in the robot’s frame (error configuration): The gap from a robot to its reference measured along the robot’s own axes: xe​ ahead, ye​ to the left and θe​ the reference heading minus the robot’s; it tells the robot directly whether to speed up or turn, because its speed acts along xe​ and its turn on all three. Explained in §4.1 Why the room’s axes are the wrong ones.. They are closely related to three errors of RO-070, measured from the path: the along-track error ()Along-track error: How far behind (or ahead of) its timed reference a vehicle is, measured along the path, such as 3.0 m after a 1.5 s hold against a 2 m/s timetable; trajectory tracking drives it to zero by changing speed, path following ignores it. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §3. Path following and trajectory tracking, how far behind the timetable; the lateral error ()Lateral error e (cross-track error): The signed distance from a path to a point of the vehicle, measured along the path’s normal at the nearest path point and positive to the left, such as −0.770 m for a car outside an arc; it is one of the two numbers a path tracker drives to zero. Explained in: Path Following and Tracking Errors, §4.3 Heading error, the heading minus the path’s. 4.2 How the error changes as both robots drive

A tracker needs to know what its commands do to the three errors, xe​ ahead, ye​ to the left and θe​ in heading. Three motions change them:

  1. The reference robot drives at vr​ along its heading, which is θe​ from ours. So it moves ahead of us at vr​cosθe​ and to our left at vr​sinθe​, the same cosine and sine split.
  2. Our robot drives at v along its own heading, so everything ahead comes closer: xe​ falls at v.
  3. Our robot turns at ω. Its axes turn left, so a point standing still in the room swings right in our robot’s frame, at ω times its distance, the arc ruleExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.3 Why angles are measured in radians of RO-001. A point ahead slides to the right: ye​ falls at ωxe​. A point to the left slides ahead: xe​ grows at ωye​. The heading error falls at ω while the reference robot’s turning raises it at ωr​.

Adding the three (Kanayama 1991 Lemma 1; Paden §V.B.1):

x˙e​=ωye​−v+vr​cosθe​ y˙​e​=−ωxe​+vr​sinθe​ θ˙e​=ωr​−ω

5. Kanayama’s tracker

Key point

Kanayama’s tracker drives the reference robot’s speeds and corrects them by the error: it speeds up in proportion to the gap ahead and turns in proportion to the gap to the left and to the sine of the heading gap, both scaled by the reference speed. On our start it is within 1 cm of the timetable after 3.35 s and asks at most 0.548 m/s of a wheel.

5.1 The rule, part by part

From the start, the reference robot is 0.2 m ahead and 0.1 m to the right, facing our way (Figure 4a). The rule of Kanayama 1991 (eq. 8; Paden §V.B.1) has a feedforward part and a feedback part in each command:

  • Feedforward, vr​cosθe​ and ωr​: drive what the timetable drives. The speed is the part of the reference robot’s speed that lies along our heading, so by rule 1 of Section 4.2 the reference robot then pulls ahead no faster than we follow. “The first term in each velocity is a feedforward part” (Kanayama 1991 §3).
  • Speed feedback, Kx​ times xe​: the reference robot is ahead, so drive faster, in proportion to the gap, proportional control ()Proportional control (P control): Feedback that sets the command in proportion to the control error, such as a torque of 0.2 N m per rad of heading error; it pulls the output towards the reference like a spring, but alone it swings when nothing damps the motion and leaves an error under a steady load. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §3.1 The rule, and why it overshoots.
  • Sideways feedback, vr​Ky​ye​: the reference robot is to the left, so turn left. The reference speed multiplies it because turning moves a robot sideways only as fast as it drives.
  • Heading feedback, vr​Kθ​sinθe​: turn towards the reference robot’s heading.

The symbols, with the values this Note uses (chosen in Section 6.3):

  • vr​ and ωr​ are the reference robot’s speed and turn rate, 0.3 m/s and 0 on the first straight;
  • xe​, ye​ and θe​ are the error in the robot’s frame, 0.2 m, −0.1 m and 0 at the start;
  • Kx​ is the speed gain, 1 per s: 1 m/s more speed for each metre of gap ahead;
  • Ky​ is the sideways gain, 16 per m²;
  • Kθ​ is the heading gain, 8 per m;
  • v and ω are the commands, in m/s and rad/s.
v=vr​cosθe​+Kx​xe​ ω=ωr​+vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​)

This rule is Kanayama’s tracker ()Kanayama’s tracker: A trajectory tracker for a wheeled robot that sends the reference’s speed and turn rate plus feedback on the error in the robot’s frame, v=vr​cosθe​+Kx​xe​ and ω=ωr​+vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​); a Lyapunov function proves its error cannot grow without bound while the reference drives forward. v=0.3×1+1×0.2 =0.5 m/s ω=0+0.3×(16×(−0.1)+0) =−0.48 rad/s

5.3 Why both parts are needed

Without the two feedforward parts, the rule is:

v=Kx​xe​ ω=vr​(Ky​ye​+Kθ​sinθe​)

Then the robot never catches up: it is 0.287 m behind at 2 s and 0.297 m behind at the end, about 1 s late, and on the arc its heading lags by up to 15° (notebook). The speed now has to come from the gap. To drive at 0.3 m/s with a speed gain of 1 per s, the gap ahead must be:

xe​=0.3/1=0.3 m xe​=vr​/Kx​

The 0.3 m is the same kind of lag as RO-066’s speed loopExplained in: Feedforward, Integral Action, Cascaded Loops and Windup, §2.1 Why feedback alone trails the plan, which ended 10 cm behind its plan because its feedback also had to fall behind before it pushed. The two parts share the work: feedforward drives the timetable, and feedback removes the gap the start left. One run converged, but one run is not a guarantee; the next section asks whether every start does.

6. Why the gap must close: a Lyapunov function

Key point

Kanayama’s V, half the squared distance to the reference robot plus a heading part, is zero only on the timetable and positive everywhere else, and under his tracker its rate is never above zero. So V can never grow, from any start, as long as the reference robot drives forward, and the error cannot grow without bound; the gap closes fastest without swinging past the line when the damping ratio is 1.

6.1 An energy of the error that can only fall

Figure 5 showed one start closing its gap. To know that every start does, without trying them all, we use an everyday analogy. A marble rolling inside a bowl with some friction loses height all the time, so it must end at the bottom, wherever it started.

So we look for one number built from the error that behaves like the marble’s height:

  1. it is zero when the error is zero, and positive for every other error;
  2. under the tracker it never rises.

Such a number is a Lyapunov function ()Lyapunov function: A number built from a system’s error that is zero only at zero error, positive otherwise, and never rises while the system runs, like the height of a marble in a bowl with friction; finding one proves the error cannot grow without bound, without testing every start. Explained in §6.1 An energy of the error that can only fall. (Kanayama 1991 §3; Paden §V.B.1). If one exists, the number can never rise above its start value, so the error cannot grow without bound; if it also keeps falling until the error is zero, the robot ends on its timetable. Kanayama turned to one because the robot’s equations have sines and products and only two commands for three errors, and “the use of a Liapunov function resolves these difficulties” (Kanayama 1991 §1): the proof below needs only the error rates of Section 4.2, never their solution.

6.2 Kanayama’s V and why it falls

Kanayama’s choice adds a distance part and a heading part:

  • V is the Lyapunov function, in m²;
  • xe​ and ye​ are the gap ahead and to the left, in m (0.2 and −0.1 at the start);
  • θe​ is the heading gap, in rad (0 at the start);
  • Ky​ is the sideways gain, 16 per m².
V=2xe2​+ye2​​+Ky​1−cosθe​​

At the start:

V=20.04+0.01​+0 =0.025 m2

V meets the first condition: the distance part is half the squared distance to the reference robot, and the heading part is zero at θe​=0 and positive at every other heading gap, because a cosine is below 1 there. Dividing it by Ky​ is what makes the second condition work, as the derivation shows.

For the second condition we need the rate of V. By the chain rule ()Chain rule: To differentiate a function of a function, multiply the outer derivative by the inner derivative. The first rate uses the speed command: v−vr​cosθe​ is Kx​xe​. In the sum, two pairs cancel: 0.0144 against −0.0144, and 0.015 against −0.015. Each cancels for a reason we can name:

  • The turning pair, ωxe​ye​: turning only swings the reference robot round in our robot’s frame (rule 3 of Section 4.2); it does not change the distance between them.
  • The sideways pair, vr​ye​sinθe​: the sideways gap grows as the heading gap carries us sideways, and the Ky​ye​ part of the turn command, weighted by the 1/Ky​ in V, cancels exactly that term. Kanayama chose the two to match.

What is left is a square times a minus sign, twice. With positive gains and a reference robot driving forward (a reference speed above zero), the rate of V is never above zero, at any error (Kanayama 1991 Prop. 1). At the start of our run it is:

V˙=−1×0.22−0=−0.04 m2/s

The rate is zero only when xe​=0 and sinθe​=0. The robot cannot stop there with a sideways gap left: with ye​ not zero, the heading rate −vr​Ky​ye​ is not zero, so θe​ moves off zero at once and V falls again. So V keeps falling until the whole error is zero. The full proof that the error then reaches zero needs a reference whose forward speed stays above a positive value and whose speeds change slowly (Kanayama 1991 Prop. 2); Paden §V.B.1 states it for constant vr​ and ωr​, which our timetable has on each of its three parts. A timetable that spins on the spot, with zero reference speed, breaks the condition: both feedback parts of the turn command are multiplied by vr​, so the tracker cannot correct a sideways gap there. MR §13.3.4 warns the same for its own tracker.

Why Ky​=16 per m². With damping ratio 1, the sideways gap at time t is its start value times a factor (Kanayama 1991 eq. 22):

(1+ωn​t)e−ωn​t

The factor is 9.2 percent when the natural frequency times the time reaches 4:

(1+4)×e−4=0.092 t=4/1.2=3.33 s 0.3×3.33=1.0 m

In metres driven that distance is 4 divided by the square root of Ky​, whatever the speed:

4/Ky​​=4/4=1 m

So Ky​=16 per m² brings the sideways gap down to 9.2 percent within the 1 m of the first straight, before the arc starts. Kx​=1 per s closes the gap ahead with a 1 s time constant while keeping the first command at 0.548 m/s, under the 0.6 m/s wheel limit. The gains are tuned for small errors on a straight: far from the timetable the rule has sines and products, so its reply is not exactly this second-order one, unlike the offset point’s exact 5 percent per cycle (Section 3.3). In exchange, Kanayama’s tracker controls the heading and has a convergence proof.

7. Where timed tracking runs

Both trackers need a timetable from a planner and send speed and turn commands many times a second. In widely used stacks:

  • Autoware (Figure 8). The planner hands the controller a trajectory whose points each carry a pose, a speed and a time from the start (time_from_start in autoware_planning_msgs/msg/TrajectoryPoint.msg). The trajectory follower runs every 0.03 s (ctrl_period) and splits the job: a lateral controller, model predictive control or pure pursuit, sets the steering, and a PID controller on the speed sets the acceleration (autoware_trajectory_follower_node/src/controller_node.cpp). Its trajectory_reference_mode is spatial by default, which finds the car’s place on the trajectory by distance, or temporal, which uses each point’s time as given: timed tracking (autoware_mpc_lateral_controller/README.md). The model predictive controller plans 50 steps of 0.1 s ahead (lateral_controller_defaults.param.yaml); a later Note on model predictive control for path following builds it.
  • Nav2. Its controller plugins receive the plan as a list of poses with no times (nav_msgs/Path in nav2_core/controller.hpp) and choose the speed themselves, which is path following. The timed elastic bandTimed elastic band (TEB): A trajectory written as a list of poses with the time between neighbours, optimised for the least total time while penalties keep the motion rule, the speed and acceleration limits and the clearance from obstacles; a ROS and Nav2 local planner that solves it again every cycle from its last answer. Explained in: Trajectory Optimisation: The Local Planner, §10.2 TEB in ROS and Nav2 plans its own timed poses and turns them into commands in the same loop.
  • Kanayama’s rule itself is not one of Autoware’s lateral modes (mpc, pure_pursuit) or Nav2’s controller packages. Paden’s survey lists it as a trajectory tracker for the kinematic model (Paden Table II), and Kanayama’s group ran it on the Yamabico-11 robot (Kanayama 1991).
