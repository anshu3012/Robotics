1. Overview

Key point

To dock, the robot must stop at a place and face a direction: aiming at the place and slowing down near it arrives facing 64.0° instead of 90°, and following the dock’s line faces the right way but never stops. The polar-coordinate controller steers on the distance and two angles at once and arrives at the dock facing 90.0°.

The robot can already hold one number at a time. 2. Driving to a point

Key point

The first part of the docking problem is to get to the dock point, 2.97 m away: driving forward in proportion to the distance and turning in proportion to the angle to the point gets there, because both shrink like first-order systems. But nothing in the rule mentions the dock’s heading, so the robot arrives facing 64.0°.

2.1 Aim at the dock and slow down near it

To reach the dock point the robot needs a rule. How do we walk to a chair across a room? We turn towards it, walk, and slow down as we get close. The same two habits make a controller. The robot needs two numbers, both measured from where it stands to the dock point (Figure 4 in Section 4.1 draws them):

  • ρ (rho) is the distance from the robot’s middle to the dock point, in m, 2.97 m at the start;
  • the line of sight ()Line of sight (to a goal): The straight line from a robot to its goal point; its direction minus the robot’s heading is the angle the robot must turn to face the goal, the angle a point or pose controller steers on. Explained in §2.1 Aim at the dock and slow down near it. is the straight line from the robot to the dock point, here at 57.4° from the x-axis; its direction comes from the atan2 ()atan2: A function atan2(rise, run) that returns the direction of a line over the full circle from its rise and run given separately; needed because the plain inverse tangent of the ratio cannot tell opposite directions apart. Explained in: Probabilistic Motion Models: Velocity and Odometry, §4.2 Turn, drive, turn of the dock point’s offset, rise 2.5 m and run 1.6 m;
  • α (alpha) is the angle from the robot’s heading ()Heading θ: The angle from the world x-axis to the direction a robot faces, measured counter-clockwise; it decides which way the robot moves when it drives forward. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §2.2 Why position is not enough: the heading, its facing direction, to the line of sight, in rad, positive when the dock point is on the left: 57.4° − 30°, so 27.4° (0.4779 rad) at the start. Like every difference of angles, it is wrapped ()Angle wrapping: Adding or subtracting full turns until an angle or angle difference lies between −180° and 180°, so that 175° and −178° count as 7° apart, not 353°; needed whenever bearings or headings are compared. Explained in: Landmark Measurement Model, §3.3 Why angle differences must be wrapped into −180° to 180°.

A distance and an angle that place a point are the point’s polar coordinates ()Polar coordinates: Describing a point by its distance r from the origin and its angle θ: (rcosθ,rsinθ). Explained in: The Jacobian and Gradients of Matrices, §2.2 Functions with several outputs, here measured from the robot. The rule turns each into one command, by proportional control ()Proportional control (P control): Feedback that sets the command in proportion to the control error, such as a torque of 0.2 N m per rad of heading error; it pulls the output towards the reference like a spring, but alone it swings when nothing damps the motion and leaves an error under a steady load. Explained in: PD and PID Control, §3.1 The rule, and why it overshoots, a command in proportion to an error (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.1). The gains:

  • kρ​ is the speed per metre of distance, 1 m/s per m (1 per s);
  • kα​ is the turn rate per radian of angle, 5 rad/s per rad (5 per s).
v=kρ​ρ ω=kα​α

Steering by ρ and α like this is driving to a point ()Driving to a point: Steering a robot to a goal point by setting its forward speed in proportion to the distance to the point and its turn rate in proportion to the angle between its heading and the line of sight, v=kρ​ρ, ω=kα​α; the robot slows down as it nears the point and reaches it, but its arrival heading is not controlled. Explained in §2.1 Aim at the dock and slow down near it. (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.1). At the start:

v=1×2.968=2.968 m/s ω=5×0.4779=2.389 rad/s

The robot turns left, towards the dock point, and the forward command is proportional to the distance, so the robot slows down as it gets close. But 2.968 m/s is five times what the wheels can do.

2.2 The wheel limit: slowing both commands together

The first command asks for more than the wheels can give. By the robot’s inverse kinematics ()Inverse kinematics (wheeled robot): Working out the wheel speeds that give a wanted linear and angular velocity, used by a controller to command the motors. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.5 The reverse direction: the wheel speeds for a wanted motion, each wheel runs at v plus or minus ω times half the wheel separation, 0.1 m:

vR​=2.968+2.389×0.1=3.207 m/s vL​=2.968−2.389×0.1=2.729 m/s

Cutting only the speed would change the arc the robot drives. Instead we shrink both commands by the same factor, the one that brings the faster wheel down to 0.6 m/s. The robot then drives the same arc, only more slowly, because the turning radius ()Turning radius R: The distance from the point a robot is turning about (the instantaneous centre of curvature, ICC) to the robot’s reference point; R=v/ω, so it says how tight a turn is. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.2 Why the robot turns about a point on its axle line v divided by ω does not change. The symbols:

  • c is the scale factor, a number with no unit;
  • vmax​ is the largest wheel speed, 0.6 m/s;
  • vR​ is the faster wheel’s commanded speed, 3.207 m/s.
c=0.6/3.207=0.1871 v=0.1871×2.968=0.555 m/s ω=0.1871×2.389=0.447 rad/s

In symbols, scaling only when a wheel would pass the limit:

c=vmax​/vR​ v←cv ω←cω

The turning radius stays at 1.242 m before and after. The check at easy values: if the faster wheel already runs at exactly 0.6 m/s, the factor is 1 and nothing changes. Nav2’s graceful controller keeps the arc in the same way when it caps the turn rate (Section 5). With the limit in place, the rule drives the robot to the dock point; Section 2.3 shows why it must get there.

2.3 Why the robot gets there: two errors that shrink

Does the rule reach the dock point? Figure 2 plays the drive with ρ and α redrawn every 0.25 s. Watch the red arc: α falls from 27.4° to 11.6° in the first second and is near 0 by about 4 s, while ρ shrinks steadily. From then on the robot drives straight at the dock point.

Figure 2: Driving to the dock point with forward speed v = 1 × ρ and turn rate ω = 5 α, scaled together at the wheel limit; one frame per 0.25 s. The grey dashed line is ρ, the distance to the dock point; the red arc is α, from the robot’s heading (blue dotted) to the line of sight. Lower panels: ρ and α over time. α is near 0 by 4 s; ρ falls at about 0.6 m/s until it reaches 0.6 m at 4.11 s (black dot), then shrinks to about a third every second. The last frame: within 1 cm at 8.10 s, facing 64.0°

Why do both shrink? Two rates decide it, and each follows from how the robot’s velocity, v along its heading, splits against the line of sight, the same split into a cosine and a sine partExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.1 Why the speed splits into cosine and sine parts that gives the robot’s motion along x and y:

  • the part along the line of sight, v times the cosine of α, closes the distance, so ρ falls at that rate;
  • the part across it, v times the sine of α, swings the line of sight round the dock point. At distance ρ, a sideways speed turns the line at that speed divided by ρ, by RO-001’s arc ruleExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.2 Why the robot turns about a point on its axle line, speed equals turn rate times distance;
  • α is the line of sight’s direction minus the heading, so it changes by the line’s swing minus the turn rate ω.

The symbols, with a dot for a rate of change, the dot notation ()Dot notation x˙: A dot over a quantity means its rate of change per second (its derivative with respect to time), such as x˙=0.433 m/s; models of motion are written with it. Explained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §5.2 The kinematic model:

  • ρ˙​ is the rate of change of the distance, in m/s;
  • α˙ is the rate of change of α, in rad/s.
ρ˙​=−vcosα α˙=ρvsinα​−ω

Near the dock point the speed is kρ​ times ρ (no wheel limit below 0.6 m) and α is small, so its cosine is about 1 and its sine about α itself. Our numbers first, at ρ = 0.5 m and α = 0.1 rad, where v = 0.5 m/s and ω = 0.5 rad/s, then the same in symbols:

ρ˙​=−0.5×cos0.1=−0.4975 ρ˙​≈−1×0.5 α˙=0.5×sin0.1/0.5−0.5 =0.0998−0.5=−0.4002 α˙≈(1−5)×0.1 ρ˙​≈−kρ​ρ α˙≈(kρ​−kα​)α

Each error shrinks at a rate in proportion to its own size, which makes each a first-order system ()First-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with only a first derivative, such as the robot’s speed under P control; it closes the same fraction of its remaining gap in every time constant and can never overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant, the kind that closes the same fraction of its gap in every time constant ()Time constant: The time a first-order system takes to close 63 % of the gap to its final value, m/(K_v + b) = 1 s for the robot’s speed loop; it fixes the rise time (about 2.2 time constants) and the 2 % settling time (about 4). Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §4.1 The time constant. Choosing the commands so that each error obeys such an equation, and so decays to zero, is the standard way to build the rule (COS 495 L6, slide 9). Here:

  • ρ has time constant 1 divided by kρ​, 1 s. Figure 2 shows it: ρ reaches 0.6 m at 4.11 s and 0.215 m one second later, 36 % of 0.6 m. A smooth loop would keep 37 %; holding each command for 0.05 s, the zero-order hold ()Zero-order hold: Keeping each command constant from its tick until the next one, so the actuator gets a staircase; simple and cheap, but the held value is on average half a sample period old, so it acts like a delay of T/2. Explained in: Digital Control: Sampling a Continuous Controller, §3.1 Holding each command until the next one of a 20 Hz loop, costs the difference.
  • α shrinks only if kα​ is larger than kρ​: otherwise the line of sight swings away faster than the robot turns after it. With 5 and 1, α has time constant 1/4 s, so it decays long before ρ.

So the robot reaches the dock point, as Figure 2 shows: within 1 cm after 8.10 s. Its arrival heading, however, is not controlled.

2.4 What driving to a point cannot do: the arrival heading

Once α is near 0, by 4 s, the robot drives straight along the line of sight, and the heading it has then is the heading it arrives with: 64.0°, the last frame of Figure 2. The rule has no number for the dock’s heading, so nothing pulls the heading towards 90°. The arrival heading depends only on where the robot started and how fast it turned: with kα​ = 3 instead of 5, the same start arrives facing 70.7° (notebook).

One fix is to stop at the dock point and spin 26.0° on the spot: position first, heading second. The robot has two inputs for the three numbers of its poseExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §6.1 The rule: no sideways motion, so a two-stage plan is a natural one, and Nav2’s graceful controller uses it by default at the end of a path (prefer_final_rotation; its README: the robot follows the path’s direction and makes “a final rotation in place to the goal orientation”). Our robot can spin on the spotExplained in: Pose and the Differential Drive, §4.4 Four ways to drive, but it must stop to do so, and a car cannot spinExplained in: Hybrid A* and Path Smoothing, §1. Overview at all. A better way corrects the heading during the approach. The dock’s heading defines a line, and Section 3 makes the robot follow it.

3. Driving along a line

Key point

The second part of the docking problem is the arrival heading: steering on the distance from the dock’s line and on the heading error, how far the heading points away from the line, brings the robot onto the line facing 90° without swinging, like a block on a spring and a damper. But nothing in the rule says where to stop, so the robot meets the dock at 0.50 m/s.

3.1 Two errors from the line: the distance and the heading error

A robot that arrives along the dock’s line arrives facing the dock. The dock’s line runs through the dock point at 90°: the line x = 3.6 m, followed upwards. The robot needs two numbers about it, both shown in Figure 3:

  • e is the signed distance from the robot’s middle to the line, in m, positive when the robot is to the line’s left: 1.60 m at the start;
  • ψ (psi) is the heading error, the robot’s heading minus the line’s direction, in rad, wrapped: 30° − 90°, so −60° (−1.0472 rad) at the start.

A driver joining a lane does the same: steer towards the lane in proportion to how far off it the car is, and straighten up in proportion to how far the car points away from the lane’s direction. The robot keeps a fixed forward speed and steers on both errors (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.2):

  • v is the forward speed, 0.5 m/s;
  • Kd​ is the turn rate per metre of distance, 2 rad/s per m;
  • Kh​ is the turn rate per radian of heading error, 2 per s.
v=0.5 m/s ω=−Kd​e−Kh​ψ

The minus signs make the robot turn back towards the line: right when it is left of the line, right when it points left of the line’s direction. Steering on e and ψ with a fixed speed is driving along a line ()Driving along a line: Steering a robot onto a straight line at a fixed speed, with a turn rate set by its signed distance from the line and its heading error against the line’s direction, ω=−Kd​e−Kh​ψ; the robot settles onto the line like a spring and damper, but a line has no end, so it never stops. Explained in §3.1 Two errors from the line: the distance and the heading error. (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.2). At the start:

ω=−2×1.60−2×(−1.0472) =−3.2000+2.0944 =−1.1056 rad/s

The distance term asks for a right turn of 3.200 rad/s and the heading term, since the robot already points 60° to the right of the line’s direction, asks for 2.094 rad/s back to the left. The distance term is larger, so the robot turns right first, down to a heading of 13.7°, and approaches the line at a steeper angle. The faster wheel would need 0.61056 m/s, so the wheel limit of Section 2.2 scales both commands by 0.9827, to 0.491 m/s and −1.086 rad/s.

Figure 3: Driving along the dock’s line x = 3.6 m (green dashed) at 0.5 m/s with turn rate ω = −2 e − 2 ψ; one frame per 0.25 s. The purple arrow is e, the distance from the line; the red arc is ψ, from the line’s direction (green dotted) to the robot’s heading (blue dotted). Lower panels: e and ψ over time. The robot first turns right to 13.7°, then curves onto the line without crossing it. The last frame: its front meets the dock at 6.60 s, still at 0.50 m/s, 0.022 m from the line and facing 87.9°

The robot joins the line without crossing it; Section 3.2 shows why it does not swing past.

3.2 Why it settles without swinging: a spring and a damper

Figure 3 shows the robot curving onto the line without crossing it. Why does it not swing past the line? Near the line both errors are small. The distance changes with the part of the velocity square to the line, v times the sine of ψ, about v times ψ, and the heading error changes at the turn rate, because the line’s direction is fixed. Then, with numbers first:

e˙≈0.5ψ ψ˙​=ω=−2e−2ψ e¨≈0.5ψ˙​ =0.5×(−2e−2ψ) =−1e−2e˙ e¨+2e˙+1e=0

The same steps in symbols, with e¨ the rate of change of e˙:

e˙=vsinψ≈vψ ψ˙​=−Kd​e−Kh​ψ e¨≈vψ˙​ =−vKd​e−Kh​vψ e¨+Kh​e˙+vKd​e=0

The last line is the error dynamics ()Error dynamics: The differential equation the error of a controlled system obeys, found by putting the controller’s rule into the plant’s equation of motion, such as 0.1 e” + 0.08 e’ + 0.4 e = 0 for the heading under PD control; its solution predicts the step response from the gains. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §3.1 The heading error is a block on a spring and a damper of a block on a spring and a damper, a second-order system ()Second-order system: A system whose output follows a differential equation with a second derivative, such as the robot’s heading under PD control; written e” + 2ζω_n e’ + ω_n² e = 0, its response is set by two numbers, the natural frequency and the damping ratio. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio: the speed times Kd​ is the spring, 1, and Kh​ is the damper, 2. In RO-061’s form, with the natural frequency ()Natural frequency: The rate, in rad/s, at which a second-order system would swing with no damping, ωn​=Kp​/I​, 2 rad/s for the heading loop; it sets how fast the response is, and raising it compresses the whole response in time. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio ωn​ and the damping ratio ()Damping ratio: A second-order system’s damping compared with the damping that just stops it from swinging, ζ=Kd​/(2Kp​I​), 0.2 for D gain 0.08 and 1 for 0.4; it alone sets the shape of the response and its overshoot. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.1 Natural frequency and damping ratio ζ:

ωn2​=0.5×2=1 ωn​=1 rad/s 2ζωn​=2 ζ=2/(2×1)=1

A damping ratio of 1 is critically damped ()Critically damped: A second-order system with damping ratio exactly 1: its characteristic equation has one double real root, so it gives the fastest step response that does not overshoot, such as the turn with K_p = K_d = 0.4 settling in 2.92 s. Explained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §5.2 Two roots decide the kind of response: the fastest response that does not overshoot, and the reason we chose Kh​ = 2 for Kd​ = 2 at 0.5 m/s. The spring depends on the speed: it is the speed times Kd​, so the same gains swing at a higher speed. At 2 m/s:

ωn2​=2×2=4 ζ=2/(2×2)=0.5

The robot settles onto the line facing 90°: at 6 s it is 0.036 m from the line and facing 86.6°. Line following corrects the heading, but it cannot stop the robot.

3.3 What line following cannot do: stop

The rule drives at 0.5 m/s whatever happens, and the dock point is not in it. In the last frame of Figure 3 the robot’s front meets the dock at 6.60 s at full speed, 0.022 m from the line and facing 87.9°. A line has no end, so a line follower has nothing to stop at. Driving along a line is the basis of the next Note’s task, following a path that has no end in view; for a dock we need both: Section 2’s slowing distance and Section 3’s heading, in one rule.

4. Driving to a pose: the polar-coordinate controller

Key point

The whole docking problem asks for the place and the heading together: adding one angle, β, from the line of sight to the dock’s heading, and turning against it, makes the robot swing out and come in along the dock’s line. Near the dock the two angles behave like a critically damped spring and damper, and the robot arrives at 8.45 s facing 90.0°.

4.1 Three numbers for a pose goal: a distance and two angles

Driving to a point used ρ and α; driving along a line used a heading error. To stop at the dock facing 90°, the robot needs one more number that says how far the dock’s heading is from the way the robot is approaching. The new number is β (beta), drawn in Figure 4 at the start:

  • ρ is the distance to the dock point, 2.97 m (Section 2.1);
  • α is the angle from the robot’s heading to the line of sight, the straight line to the dock point, 27.4°;
  • β is the angle from the line of sight to the dock’s heading, in rad, wrapped: 90° − 57.4°, so 32.6° (0.5693 rad) at the start.

Figure 4: The three numbers of the polar-coordinate controller at the start. The robot (blue disk) at (2.0, 1.0) faces 30° (blue dotted); the grey dashed line of sight to the dock point runs at 57.4° and is ρ = 2.97 m long; the red arc α = 27.4° turns from the heading to the line of sight. At the dock point the purple arc β = 32.6° turns from the line of sight to the dock’s heading of 90°; both lines are drawn continued backwards through the dock point, below it, which gives the same angle

When the robot is at the dock facing 90°, all three are 0. The robot’s heading is the line of sight’s direction minus α, and the dock’s heading is the line of sight’s direction plus β. So the heading still to turn, 60° at the start, is α plus β:

27.4∘+32.6∘=60.0∘

Driving all three to 0 brings the robot to the dock point facing 90°. Bringing a robot to a given position and heading is driving to a pose ()Driving to a pose: Bringing a robot to a given position and heading together, such as stopping at a charging dock facing it; it needs a controller that steers on the heading still to turn as well as the distance, because aiming at the point alone leaves the arrival heading to chance. Explained in §4.1 Three numbers for a pose goal: a distance and two angles. (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.4). Section 4.2 builds a rule that drives all three to 0.

4.2 The rule: aim off to the side

How does β help? Consider the dock’s line. The robot starts to its left, so to come in along the line it must first move right, across in front of the dock, and only then turn towards it. Moving right swings the line of sight from 57.4° towards 90°, which shrinks β. So the rule turns the robot to the right in proportion to β, on top of turning it towards the dock point in proportion to α (Corke RVC3 §4.1.1.4; PythonRobotics, Move to a Pose). The gains:

  • kρ​ is the speed per metre of distance, 1 per s, as in Section 2;
  • kα​ is the turn rate per radian of α, 5 per s, as in Section 2;
  • kβ​ is the turn rate per radian of β, −4 per s; it is negative, so a positive β asks for a right turn.
v=kρ​ρ ω=kα​α+kβ​β

4.3 Why it works: the two angles near the dock

The table shows α and β reaching 0 together, before ρ does. To see why, take the rates of the three numbers. ρ and α change as in Section 2.3. β is the dock’s fixed heading minus the line of sight’s direction, so it changes by minus the line’s swing. The symbols are those of Section 4.1 and Section 4.2, with β˙​ the rate of change of β, in rad/s (COS 495 L6, slides 22 to 25):

ρ˙​=−vcosα α˙=ρvsinα​−ω β˙​=−ρvsinα​

Near the dock, v is kρ​ times ρ and both angles are small, so the sine of α is about α. Put in the rule, our gains first, with β¨​ the rate of change of β˙​:

α˙≈1α−5α+4β =−4α+4β β˙​≈−1α β¨​≈−1α˙ =4α−4β =−4β˙​−4β β¨​+4β˙​+4β=0

The same steps in symbols:

α˙≈kρ​α−kα​α−kβ​β β˙​≈−kρ​α β¨​≈−kρ​α˙ =(kα​−kρ​)kρ​α+kρ​kβ​β =−(kα​−kρ​)β˙​+kρ​kβ​β β¨​+(kα​−kρ​)β˙​−kρ​kβ​β=0

Again a block on a spring and a damper, as in Section 3.2, and α obeys the same equation. The spring is minus kρ​ times kβ​, 4; the damper is kα​ minus kρ​, 4. In RO-061’s form:

ωn2​=−1×(−4)=4 ωn​=2 rad/s ζ=4/(2×2)=1

A critically damped pair with a natural frequency of 2 rad/s is the response of RO-061’s chosen turn, which settlesExplained in: Reading a Controller’s Response: Step Response and Second-Order Systems, §6.4 Choosing the gains for the turn within 2 % in 2.92 s. Since α and β both go to 0, so does the heading still to turn, their sum, and the robot faces 90°.

A block stays near its rest position only if the spring pulls it back and the damper resists its motion: both must be positive (COS 495 L6, slide 28). Requiring both gives the three conditions on the gains, with the distance’s own rate kρ​ positive so that ρ shrinks:

kρ​>0 kβ​<0 kα​−kρ​>0

The second condition says the β term must turn the robot away from the dock’s heading, as in Section 4.2; the third is Section 2.3’s condition again: turning must outpace the swing of the line of sight. Section 4.4 picks gains that meet all three, and breaks each in turn.

4.4 Choosing the gains, and what breaks them

Why 1, 5 and −4? Three reasons, one per gain:

  • kρ​ = 1 per s sets how fast the robot closes the last 0.6 m, with a time constant of 1 s (Section 2.3).
  • The angles must settle faster than the distance, or the robot reaches the dock point still turning. A natural frequency of 2 rad/s, twice kρ​, achieves this, and gives kβ​: minus 4 divided by 1.
  • A damping ratio of 1 gives the fastest settling without a swing past the dock’s line, and gives kα​: the damper, 4, plus kρ​.

The second reason shows when it is broken. With kα​ = 3 and kβ​ = −1, which meet all three conditions but give a natural frequency of only 1 rad/s, as fast as the distance, eight starts round the dock arrive facing anywhere from 84.7° to 95.3° (notebook).

One case breaks the rule as written: a dock point behind the robot, with α beyond ±90°. Then the part of the velocity along the line of sight points away from the dock, and the linear analysis of Section 4.3, which assumed small angles, does not hold. The standard fix drives the robot backwards: measure α from the robot’s back instead of its front and make v negative (COS 495 L6, slides 30 to 33). Corke’s code picks the direction once, at the start, and PythonRobotics’ code picks it at every step. A real navigation stack docks with a controller of the same family, which Section 5 runs on our dock.

5. Where it runs: Nav2’s graceful controller and docking

Key point

Nav2, the navigation software of ROS 2, docks robots with a pose controller of the same family: its graceful controller steers on the distance and two angles to a target pose, and sets the speed from how tightly the robot must turn. On our dock it arrives facing 90° to within 0.1°, more slowly than our controller.

The docking problem of the Overview is a standard task, and Nav2 solves it with a pose controller. Its package nav2_graceful_controller implements the smooth control law of Park and Kuipers (ICRA 2011), named in its README. A controller that drives to a pose by choosing the curvature of the arc to drive from the distance and two angles, and the speed from that curvature, is a graceful controller ()Graceful controller (Park–Kuipers smooth control law): A pose controller that chooses the curvature of the arc to drive from the distance to a target pose and two angles to it, aiming the heading off the line of sight by a capped amount, and sets the speed lower on tight arcs and near the target; Nav2’s nav2_graceful_controller implements it and Nav2’s docking server uses it for the final approach. Explained in §5. Where it runs: Nav2’s graceful controller and docking.. In Nav2’s code (the files ego_polar_coords.hpp and smooth_control_law.cpp of the package, read for this Note):

  • It measures the same three numbers. Nav2 calls them r, the distance (our ρ); ϕ, the target’s heading minus the line of sight, the line from the robot to the target (our β); and δ, the robot’s heading minus the line of sight (our α with the opposite sign).
  • Instead of a turn rate, it chooses the curvature of the arc to drive. The heading is steered towards an angle off the line of sight that grows with ϕ but is capped by an arctangent, so a large ϕ cannot ask for more than a quarter turn; this is the “slow subsystem” of the README. The heading’s own error is fed back with gain k_delta, the “fast subsystem”.
  • The speed is the largest speed divided by 1 plus beta times the curvature squared (lambda = 2), so the robot slows on tight arcs. Within slowdown_radius of the target it slows further in proportion to the distance, and the turn rate is the curvature times the speed, capped at v_angular_max with the speed cut to keep the arc, as in Section 2.2.

The defaults in src/parameter_handler.cpp are k_phi 2.0, k_delta 1.0, beta 0.4, lambda 2.0, v_linear_max 0.5 m/s, v_linear_min 0.1 m/s, v_angular_max 1.0 rad/s and slowdown_radius 1.5 m. The package’s README still lists a motion_target_dist that the code has replaced by min_lookahead 0.25 m and max_lookahead 1.0 m (src/graceful_controller.cpp): as a path follower, the controller takes as its target the pose on the planned path max_lookahead ahead, or a nearer one if that one cannot be reached without a collision, and drives to it again at every cycle. The docking server (controller.cpp of the package opennav_docking, in Nav2’s nav2_docking folder) builds the same control law with gentler numbers for the last approach: k_phi 3.0, k_delta 2.0, v_linear_max 0.25 m/s and slowdown_radius 0.25 m.

Figure 8 runs Nav2’s law, with the plugin’s defaults, on our dock next to our controller. Watch the speed panel: the graceful controller starts below 0.5 m/s, slows from 3.60 s, 1.38 m from the dock, where its slow-down near the target (which starts 1.5 m out) first asks for less than the 0.47 m/s it was driving, and makes the final approach at its 0.1 m/s floor.

Both arrive facing 90°, the graceful controller at 11.15 s against 8.45 s, along almost the same path. The difference is the speed rule: the graceful controller slows on tight arcs and well before the target, which is what “graceful” means in the paper’s title: in the README’s words, “a smooth and comfortable trajectory”. Open versions of the polar-coordinate controller itself are Corke’s driveconfig.py and PythonRobotics’ move_to_pose.py.
